# Chapter 4 — Home

*Day 0, 8:00 p.m. – 11:42 p.m.*

Kwame drove home the twenty-six minutes with the windows down.

It was the only time the Corolla felt cool. At noon it was an oven, at evening it was a warm tin. At night, with the road empty and the air coming from the bush, it was almost a good car. He drove the way he always drove after a slow day — not fast, not slow, letting the headlights make that pale wedge that cut the dark, picking his line around the potholes while he could still see them.

₵285 had become ₵295 on the way because a boy at the hospital junction flagged him for a short jump — two cedis, then eight more because the boy had added a stop. Ten cedis for five minutes' work. Almost enough.

Almost was a word Mansa used often.

The hill was quiet when he turned off. Eight single-room self-contained apartments in a line, one lane connecting them to the nearest visible house, compound walls low enough to see over and high enough to argue that you hadn't looked. His and Mansa's was the third from the gate, the pawpaw tree behind it thick enough to drop fruit on the roofing sheets at night and make everyone think someone was throwing stones. The cemetery lay east, beyond the compound, its iron gate faintly visible only as a crooked post where the road bent. At night it was just a darker dark.

He stopped at the metal gate. The gate that screeched unless you did it right.

He put his palm flat, lifted, pushed. The hinge took his weight and made no sound. He let out a breath he had not known he was holding. It was a small thing — lifting a gate so eight doors did not open — but it was his. In a day where he had paid for eggs he did not break, stopped a beating he did not start, and watched a black car break a barrier he did not man, this one quiet success felt like something he could keep.

He parked under the pawpaw tree. The curtain in their window moved, though there was no wind.

Inside, Mansa was at the table.

She did not stand. That was not new. When she was happy she stood. When she was tired she stayed seated and looked up with a smile that reached her eyes later than her mouth. When she was angry she stayed seated and looked up without smiling at all.

This was the third.

“At long last,” she said. Not loud. Not yet.

Kwame put his sales pouch on the stool and kept his hands on it, as if steadying it. His Bible with the ribbon was still where he had left it that morning. Matthew 5 had not moved.

“Last passengers,” he said.

“Mmm.” She looked at the pouch. Mansa was thirty-eight and kept the books in a small exercise book with a red cover where she wrote every amount in a careful hand that made even cots look neat. Longest-serving tenant alongside him, second only to him in knowing which tenant's child was sick, which tenant's husband had not come home. She knew money the way he knew queues — by sight, by habit, by the gap where something should be.

“How be market?” she asked. It was her normal way. Not *how much*, first. *How be market*. As if market were a person who could be tired or fine.

“Slow,” he said. True enough.

“Bad Luck,” she said, and almost smiled. She had heard it already. In Mallam Town a phrase made at the station at six in the evening reached the hill by seven. “That is what Abena say. She say morning eggs break so afternoon no sales. Yaw say that.”

Abena. Next door. *Nkonkonsa*. The name itself was a small verdict — gossip — but said affectionately, the way you name a goat that always finds the garden.

Kwame shrugged. “Small slow.”

He moved to wash his hands at the basin by the window. The window was open a hand's breadth to let the heat out. Its curtain — thin, white, hemmed by Mansa herself — moved again. Beyond it, the pawpaw leaves brushed the wall. He did not look out. He never did.

Through the wall, thin as paper in that place, voices came. Not loud enough to be called listening-to, loud enough to be called hearing-whether-you-wanted-or-not.

Abena's voice first, chatty and confident, the salon voice.

“…you still think I should follow him?”

That was Mansa. Kwame heard his wife's voice through concrete, an octave higher than she used with him, the voice she used when she wanted an answer from another woman.

Abena answered, half-laugh, half-advice, words falling quick. Kwame caught only the shape: “…if you no follow, how you go know? You for know, Mansa. Men — they keep quiet, eehh, na so they be…”

Kwame lifted his hands from the basin. The water dripped.

He did not knock on the wall. He did not call out. In his account of this night, later, he would not remember this exact exchange at all — only that Abena's radio had been low and that Mansa had been quieter than usual when he came in. At this moment, drying his hands on the towel that Mansa had folded into thirds that morning, he thought the women were talking about a film on television.

Mansa's voice, clearer now, as if she had moved closer to the wall or further from it: “He go hide money again.”

Abena: “Then follow am. See with your eye.”

Kwame put the towel down. He had not told Mansa about the ₵300. The sales pouch still held ₵295, which was ten cedis less than what he owed and five cedis less than what he had promised himself would be enough to make the conversation easy.

“Food dey?” he asked, to the room.

Mansa turned back from where her ear had been, though Kwame did not know it had been there.

“Food dey,” she said. Flat.

She brought it — rice and stew, small portion, the stew's oil making a neat orange edge on the plate. She had cooked early and kept it covered. It was warm, not hot. That meant she had cooked expecting him at seven, then kept it, then stopped keeping it hot.

He ate sitting, she sitting. The wall was now quiet.

“How much you make?” she asked after he had taken three spoonfuls. The question she always asked. The pulse check.

Kwame chewed one beat longer than needed.

“₵295,” he said. He did not add the other number.

Mansa's spoon stopped. She was good with numbers in a way that felt like hearing.

“₵295 whole day?”

“Morning slow,” he said. Which was true.

She looked at the pouch, then at his left forearm where a faint pink still marked where Kofi's elbow had hit him. She did not ask about the mark. She asked about the other thing she had been counting.

“You borrow?” she said. Not accusing yet. Testing the sentence before she spent it.

Kwame kept his eyes on the plate. This was the place where, in other marriages, a man explained. He borrowed to pay for a Madam's eggs because a boy would have been beaten otherwise. He owed Ebo and Kpakpo. He was five cedis short and would make it up tomorrow. Three sentences. He could say them. He had the facts in order.

He said instead, after a silence that lasted too long because he was trying to make it shorter by not speaking, “Small thing.”

Mansa heard the silence, not the words. In her arithmetic, small thing plus quiet meant something bigger being hidden.

“Small thing eehh,” she repeated. Her voice had moved. It was now sharp, direct, emotionally exposed — where his went quiet, hers rose. “Kwame Asante, I dey ask you money matter and you dey tell me small thing. You think I no dey count? You come late, you say slow, you hide hand for pouch like pouch be Bible?”

“I no hide,” he said. Quietly. Which, in the thin-walled house, was louder than shouting.

“You hide,” she said, standing now. The chair scraped. Through the wall, a small interested shift — Abena moving, perhaps, to hear better without being heard. Eight doors, thin walls; arguments here were compound property before they were finished.

“I dey manage station whole day, drivers dey talk,” Mansa went on, faster now. The specific accusation arriving, not generic nagging — money, always money, but today money as proof of some other thing: another woman, another house, a new building seven houses from Last Stop that a man who had money might be building. She did not say it exactly. She stayed close enough that Kwame could feel the shape without being given the word to refute.

“You no dey talk true again,” she said. “You think say silence be answer? You think if you keep quiet long, I go tire? I go tired, Kwame, but trouble no go tired!”

Kwame stood too, because sitting while she stood felt like another silence. He did not raise his voice. He had not raised his voice in an argument in years. That was the immovability that made the station trust him and made his wife, at that moment, feel mocked by calm.

“If you dey hear me, answer me,” she said, and her voice cracked on *answer* in a way that made her angrier, because she had not intended to crack.

He looked at the window. The curtain moved. The pawpaw leaves outside were black against a black sky. The hill was quiet except for them, except for Abena's radio which someone had turned up a fraction, then down again, as if to cover listening.

What he wanted to say — *I borrowed ₵300 to settle a Madam's eggs because a crowd would have beaten a boy for ₵20, and I am ₵5 short before repaying but I will tomorrow* — sat in his mouth and did not come out. It required three sentences. It required explaining himself. Peace, in Kwame's account, meant keeping quiet long enough for other people's noise to die down. If he waited, Mansa's noise would die. If he spoke, he would add noise. He had proven this at the station that morning: quiet between a crowd and a boy had been enough.

He did not see that a marriage is not a crowd, and that quiet, in a small room, can read as distance rather than peace.

Mansa heard the quiet and translated it.

“You're just good for nothing,” she said. The sentence landed in the small room as a thing with edges. She said it because fear had turned, as it always turns when not reassured, into an attack. “You say you be elder, you say you be station master — but for house you no be nothing. Just good for nothing.”

The wall held its breath. Somewhere beyond it, Abena's radio clicked off entirely.

Kwame's hand went to the pouch, then fell. He picked up nothing. He looked at Mansa — not long, because long would have been another sentence — and then at the door.

He did not slam it when he opened it? Later, others would say he slammed it. Thin walls make any door sound slammed. He walked to the gate, and there, even hurt, even hot, he did the practiced thing: palm flat, lift, push. The gate did not screech.

He walked toward Last Stop without looking back. He did not check the window. He did not think about the window at all.

Behind him, the room was empty in a way he had not yet made it empty.

Mansa stood alone with her exercise book unopened and her husband's sales pouch still on the stool — ₵295, uncounted by her, now proof of whatever story she would tell herself in the next ten minutes.

Through the wall, Abena breathed out, small.

And at the window, the thin white curtain lifted once more on a draft that came from outside, not inside, and settled. Mansa went to it, because the advice still sat hot in her ear — *if you no follow, how you go know?* — and because following quietly meant not using the door that the whole compound had just heard.

She put her hands on the sill. The sill was low, the pawpaw tree close, the ground closer than it looked by day.

During the day, you could see the drop coming and swerve.

At night, the drop found you.
