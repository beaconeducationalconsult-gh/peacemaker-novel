# Chapter 20 — The Missing Minutes

*Later — same period, custody*

The case was not wrong in facts. It was wrong in minutes.

Kwame sat with the flat light and counted them the way he counted sales — not because he wanted to, but because the station had trained him to know how long a run should take.

Ebo's visit had said *recording found at seven-house house — girl dey phone father — background Musah*. The interrogator had said *argument 11:42, you leave, gate no screech, Mansa for pawpaw tree base, phone dead*. The constable with the phone copy had played *Daddy? You dey house? I lock —* and *Make Musah bring car —* and *Saaba! Saaba!* before cut.

He laid them as a driver lays a route.

11:42 — he leaves, walks to Last Stop/junction, same barrier hit at 4 p.m. He sits, calls Mansa — dead. Receives Yaw's call about stranded passengers — he stands, walks back, lifts gate quiet, finds empty room, curtain moving with cool draft, window open a hand, does not look down, takes car, drives to Last Stop. Short.

12:25 — flagged at gravel edge by three (two men, one woman) — *You go Lapaz or no?* Extra ₵50. Refuses, even at pistol low along door, hand reaching past chest to kill ignition, wire snaps, gravel, beating, uncompleted building, not cemetery — that distinction the bible had made him keep — phone cracked on chest, Corolla starts second try and goes not to Lapaz but seven houses to Saaba's, where lights are still warm.

Inside that seven-house house, ordinary call already open on speaker, *Daddy, you lock?* Musah at window.

That was one story — the true one, which he had lived and not been believed.

The filed story was shorter and smoother: *You argue, you push Mansa for window, you switch off phone, you drive your own car to kill police pikin for seven-house house for revenge or money, you run to Nene hut, we find you behind door.*

Shorter stories are often smoother than true ones, because true ones have missing minutes where a man sits at a wall.

Kwame counted those minutes.

If he had pushed Mansa at 11:42, why would he walk to Last Stop and sit? Pushers do not sit at junctions where police posts are. Pushers hide cars, do not take borrowed-money cars to rob glass-window houses with lights on where a father is on speaker.

If he had driven to Saaba's house to kill, why would he first go home for the car after finding Mansa not in the room — a fact he would later be told was because she was already outside under the pawpaw tree — and why would the robbers need to ask him *You go Lapaz or no?* twice, offer extra, draw pistol, reach in to kill ignition — mechanics that matched the Camry crew's afternoon method (photograph before shooting) — if the car were lent?

If Musah was his boy, why would Chocho — whose Lacoste was flat, whose slippers were quiet, whose men had helped search and whose suggestion *make boys check Nene side last* had been heard at 4:40 — be brought to hear Musah and say *not my man* — the ledger's twist that the culprits were an outside land-guard group, not Mallam, not Chocho?

And the window: a cool draft moving a curtain. He had felt it on his ankle at 12:18 and tugged it inward irritably. A constable at mid-morning had seen the same draft and looked. Between those two drafts, a woman lay under a pawpaw tree with a phone dead from impact, not choice, and a bang that someone in the compound heard and ignored — the plantable clue that, if heard properly, would have changed everything, the way Nene had heard a different bang at the uncompleted building and not ignored it.

The missing minutes were not missing from his story. They were missing from the filed story, which had made flight from *empty room* and *dead phone* without accounting for *seven houses* distance that the stolen car needed, or *Musah* that needed an outside identifier.

He did not shout this reasoning. He wrote it, small, in the margin of the form they had given him to sign — not as a lawyer, but as a station master who kept queues tight: *Last Stop = barrier, 7 houses to Saaba house, car steal not lent, phone dead not off, window open before search, outside group.*

The officer who collected the form glanced at the margin, did not argue, just said, “We go bring Commander tomorrow. He go tell you what he see for cemetery gate for dusk. That one no dey file.”

Kwame nodded.

The flat room kept its storybook linework, careful on ordinary objects, while the man who had kept quiet long enough for noise to die now, from custody, started to notice that the case against him did not add up on its own logic — investigative payoff, but from the accused's side, where noticing matters more than access.

Outside, Kasoa FM turned down for night, and the hill's gate — which he had muffled every night to keep peace — waited for the chapter where he would, once, let it screech on purpose.
