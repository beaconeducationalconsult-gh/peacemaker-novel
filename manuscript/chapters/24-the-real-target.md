# Chapter 24 — The Real Target

*Same period — at his own hearing, custody — flat light*

They told him at the hearing, not in the cell.

That distinction matters the way thin walls matter: a cell is where you are told alone, a hearing is where you are told with people and a stamp that makes telling become filing.

The room was still flat light — Style B's daylight, uniform outline, minimal hatching on the table's edge and on the pen the officer held, white walls, storybook linework on ordinary objects — but more people made it a hearing: a table longer, two chairs on one side and three on the other, a man with a stamp whose stamp had its own felt pad that was redder than it needed to be, a constable at the door who did not offer tea because hearings do not offer tea, and a constable by the window who kept the shutter half-closed not for shade but for procedure.

Kwame stood because they told him to stand, not because he wanted to. His cuffed hands were in front, not behind — a small, procedural mercy for a man whose file now had two charges and two confessions that cleared only one and whose ribs, at Day 2 onward, still made sitting without a back less comfortable than standing. He stood at the side where the accused stands, which is also the side where the station master who knows queues stands when he wants to see which destination sign is about to be lifted.

One charge had begun to clear — the attempted one.

That file now had a doctor's note about angle and fall not push, written in the careful hand that makes even cots look neat; it had a woman's *I fall* said at noon while a fan turned slowly enough to count, said to him, not to the exercise book, before a nurse said *time*; it had a neighbor's *I told her to follow — you still think I should follow him? if you no follow how you go know? window not gate so no screech* said face-to-face on a stool with a plastic bag, not gossiped. All secondhand to the court — copies of copies — but first-hand to Kwame via Nene at 5:40 behind a half-wall and via the curtain that had moved with a draft at 12:18 and for a constable at mid-morning.

That left the other charge — the one that needed a different voice, not a wife's, not a neighbour's, but a land-guard leader's.

The officer with *fact* — mid-fifties, nail cut too short, who had built a tidy case from what Kwame didn't say at 11:42 — did not say *fact* in this room. He said, “We bring identifier,” in the voice of a man who knows that *identifier* is a word that makes a hearing listen the way *last house* makes a search listen.

He brought Chocho.

Chocho did not look like an identifier. He looked as he had at midday in Chapter 2 when he passed the station while Kasoa FM said *land guard disturbances* with a thrill — Lacoste flat, collar precise, trousers dark, slippers that made no sound in dust, hair cut low and exact, the look of a senior driver who had retired early and kept his car clean. At a distance, like that. At a closer distance, you understood why drivers moved their cars a few inches when he passed even though he had asked for nothing, and why a room with a man with a stamp gave him a chair that was not offered to show respect but to keep his slippers from being heard when he shifted, because not hearing a man can be a kind of respect.

Two boys were not with him. He came alone, which in Mallam Town is a stronger statement than coming with two boys who stand a little back. Coming alone says *I am visible enough to be alone*.

He did not look at Kwame first — that was also statement, not rude, just transactional order. He looked at the room — at the pots and stools not there but given, in the guide, the same outline care as people, at the high window's shutter, at the stamp pad's red — and then at the constable's phone on the table, which already held the copy of the copy of the house recording that had been taken from a counter where a phone on speaker had captured *Daddy? You dey house? I lock —* *Musah bring car —* *Saaba!* The phone on the table was the same shape as the phone on the counter, but with a copy.

“Play,” the man with the stamp said.

The constable played.

It was the third time Kwame had heard it — once on the constable's glass-held phone copy in the flat room, once in his memory where wood on wood kept returning as gate-lift and window-force, and now in a hearing with Chocho in the room, which was different because the background *Musah* was still low, practical, flat — *make Musah bring car comot — fast —* — as Chocho used names when he sent boys — name meaning *do* — but now it had a second ear that knew what a name's cadence sounded like when the name belonged to one network and not another, the way Ebo knew which driver's horn meant which queue.

The recording stopped after *Saaba! Saaba!* and the two seconds of house dust that had been silence after cut. The chair scrape, the woman's *fast*, the chair, the father's calls — all present, all flat.

The man with the stamp looked at Chocho. No *please*. Just the look that *identifier* requires.

“You know voice?”

Chocho's answer was guarded, transactional, exactly as the dossier had promised — quiet fear/respect in the room not because he raised his voice — he did not — but because everyone knew what his work was and was not, and because self-interest and civic instinct are not mutually exclusive the way silence and peace are not the same.

“I know voice,” Chocho said. Not loud. Transactional in the exact degree that made it believed: a word and a pause, not a speech. He turned his palm slightly — a small, clarifying gesture that drivers use when correcting a wrong destination queue — *no, not Kasoa, Lapaz* — but here *no, not Mallam, outside*.

“Not my boy.”

The words the ledger had waited for since it seeded the land-guard/ Camry association on Day 0 evening as *community blames land guards for Camry breaking barrier* and since the barber said *new house, no wall finish — na invitation*.

The room made a small sound — not gasp, not *Whaaaaaat* elongated, because this beat, the guide said, was reserved for high-intensity mob and arrest and argument, not for a hearing's quiet twist — just the way a queue does when the right destination sign is finally lifted and the people behind it adjust without needing to be told.

“Not my boy,” Chocho repeated, because repetition is emphasis that does not need to be loud. “Musah be outside. Different community. That side boys dey do fresh house work — they watch new builds along Last Stop road. They see seven-house house — fine, glass, fresh paint, low wall no render, no paper — they use tinted Camry for work, they also do that barrier that afternoon with photo before tyre. Not Mallam. Outside.”

He said it the way a man says *water* when he knows there is not enough: flat.

“Why you sure?” the man with the stamp asked, because he had to and because a hearing without *why* is only *who*.

Chocho gave the secret the dossier had noted he held and rarely let police see — the visibility that made him more than a local collector — he knew land-guard networks across neighboring communities well enough to identify a rival crew's voice on a recording — implying he had more eyes than he let on to police at manhunts.

“Musah voice,” Chocho said, flat, economical. “He drive for that side. He talk so — *make Musah bring car comot* — na that side slang. I hear for land meeting before at Weija junction when they argue plot. My boys — my Mallam boys — no talk so for work. They talk *move car* or *carry am*. Outside boys talk *bring car comot* and add *no make headlight catch wall*. That phrase — *headlight catch wall* — outside boys always talk about headlight. My boys no dey fear headlight on fresh house — they fear paper.”

He shrugged, precise, a half-inch, to show *this is not my defence, this is my grammar*.

“I no be friend to that side. I be work man like you — but that house no be my target. Seven houses from your barrier, I know house — fine, but not my paper to protect or collect. I dey watch peace same like station master — we dey watch different peace.”

That was the twist the outline marked as *clears Chocho's own crew and gives police concrete external lead*. Helps solve case genuinely, not performatively, while representing exactly the coercive extortion economy the case exposed — Chocho helps not because he is redeemed, not because he is guilty of this, but because his *not my boy* is true and also self-interested, and the book does not need to resolve that tension, just not to pretend it isn't there.

The man with the stamp nodded once. He did not say *thank you* — hearings do not thank identifiers, they file them. He lifted the stamp.

*Wood on paper.* Ordinary, not gate screech, not pawpaw thud.

He stamped: *identifier states voice belongs to land-guard member from neighbouring community (outside Mallam) — Musah — outside group to be pursued via inter-community liaison.*

That phrase *inter-community liaison* — bureaucratic, boring — was the road that would lead, the next morning, to the cemetery gate east of his hill house — crooked post, neglected iron, cleared path, stash tarp, tyre track ending inside — where Nanfuri had stood at dusk and said *Carefully* and withdrawn alone with two constables, and where, now, with backup, he would go daylight.

Kwame stood with his hands cuffed in front and understood, without being told in a sentence, that *Musah bring car comot — fast — no make headlight catch wall* — which had lived as a background name wrapped in a daughter's *Daddy?* — was now a road, an address, a community, a plot meeting, a slang, a headlight fear.

The officer at the door — young, the one who had straightened the barrier pole — leaned to the man with the stamp and then to the constable with the phone and said, quiet but so the hearing heard, because hearings need to hear where the road goes:

“We go cemetery tomorrow first light. That gate. We go with men. We go check.”

Kwame heard *cemetery* — east, his hill house's east, where the roosters at 4 a.m. were not roosters but also roosters, where the iron gate kept a stillness that didn't feel empty because it was operational — and heard, in the same sentence, what *gate* now meant to the file: not supernatural, not juju, not texture — operational staging ground/hideout of the outside crew, the place the Day 0 chase had ended with tyre track and where, with backup, an arrest would close the structural loop back to Day 0 dusk.

He did not ask to go. Per the reworked outline, per single-POV, Kwame learns of the arrest from Nanfuri personally in Chapter 25, not by witnessing, not by joining the line that would frame through foliage with a pale wedge. But here, at his own hearing, he was shown the hinge that would lead there: an identifier — guarded, transactional, slippers quiet — saying *not my man* in a way that also said *your car was taken seven houses because my town's new house was the freshest invite and your refusal at Last Stop was straight.*

Chocho stood to leave. He had not looked at Kwame before; now he did, directly, because *straight* deserved a look.

“You refuse them for Last Stop,” he said, not as accusation but as identification — the same ear that identified Musah's cadence identifying Kwame's. “You no gree carry even at gun. You no gree even after extra. That be why they thief car quick, go that seven-house. If you gree, they go argue price and girl go lock. Your refusal —” He searched for the word a land-guard leader would use for a station master who took an elbow for a Madam's thirty eggs at seven a.m. and a pistol at midnight for a wife — the immovability that was beloved and nearly lethal —

“— be straight. Straight be rare for junction.”

Kwame, who had refused at gunpoint because he was looking for his wife — preoccupied, not heroic — whose *straight* had been immovable calm in both places, received that *straight* as a verdict heavier than *Bad Luck Nkoaa* and heavier than *good for nothing* and, for a man with two charges, as the first character testimony that was not about what he did not do.

Chocho left with slippers that made no sound in dust that hearing rooms try not to have. The hearing, which had needed a name and had gotten one and a grammar for that name, adjourned to let the road be used. The stamp's red felt looked less red after stamping, as if it had given some of its blood.

The man with the stamp turned to Kwame and, for the first time in Act IV — which the outline warned would be thin unless visitors enriched it — said a sentence that was not a charge or a question:

“We go check cemetery. You rest. We tell Commander bring your car after.”

Rest, again. Not release. But the second of the two charges — Saaba — had, in that room, found its real target: not a jealous husband's car lent for murder, not a debt-ridden taxi master's revenge on a policeman's fine girl for a barrier break, but an outside crew's opportunistic work on the freshest, unpapered house mistaken for a rich man's house because glass catches sun seven houses from a junction that a commander had once withdrawn from with one word.

Outside, the flat daylight held — white ground, uniform line weight, minimal hatching. Inside, the background voice *Musah* — *bring car comot* — was now an address outside Mallam, seven houses turned into a map.
