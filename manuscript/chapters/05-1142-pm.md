# Chapter 5 — 11:42 p.m.

*Day 0, 11:42 p.m. onward*

The road down from the hill at night was a different road.

Kwame walked it without a torch. He knew where the potholes were by day — he had memorized them in the way drivers do — but at night they waited. During the day, you could see them coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you.

He walked in the middle of the lane because the edges were bush and the middle, even in darkness, kept a faint grey that was not light but the memory of light. His shoes scuffed where the tar had thinned to gravel, then to packed earth. Somewhere behind him, a dog tested a bark and gave up. The compound's gate — lifted, silent, as always — was now behind him and, with it, the thing he had not said.

He had not meant to make silence large. He had meant to make it small. Keep quiet long enough for noise to die down and you could step back into the room and find the noise gone. That worked at the station with crowds, with an elbow, with a boy who owed ₵20. He had seen it work that morning. He carried that proof like money.

Now, on the lane, the same silence felt heavier than the bag he was not carrying. Mansa's words — *good for nothing* — had not been loud, but they had been specific, and specificity, at night, did not die down when left alone. It sharpened.

He passed the first house below the hill — window dark — and the second — a television flickering without sound — and told himself he would walk to Last Stop and sit, just sit, until the compound settled. It was what he did when they argued. He went to the junction, where the police barrier was, where the road chose directions, and he sat at the edge where no one needed him to speak and no one could hear thin walls.

He was fifty steps from the compound when, behind him, the sound happened.

It was not loud. In the compound, someone else heard it as a bang and, not knowing where it came from, ignored it. At this distance, for Kwame, it was less than that — a soft, flat impact wrapped in leaves, the kind of sound a pawpaw makes when it falls and does not quite break, or when a crate settles. The night took it and kept it.

Kwame did not turn.

He thought, briefly, that it was fruit. Then he thought of nothing, because he was trying, with the sincerity of a man who believed stillness was a kind of repair, to let the house have quiet without him in it. He walked.

Last Stop at 11:52 was not a place people sat at night unless they had to. The barrier pole was up, the police post empty but for a single bulb over the desk that buzzed and threw a thin cone of light that did not reach the road. The junction smelled of dust and, faintly, of fried oil from the closed kiosk. Beyond it, seven houses down, Saaba's new house showed a single warm light behind glass. Kwame saw it without registering it the way you see a house you have passed a hundred times.

He sat on the low wall where he had sat that afternoon to count money that had not been enough. The wall was still warm from day. He put his elbows on his knees and looked at the road.

The afternoon came back to him uninvited. The black Camry hitting the pole. The pickup limping back with both left tyres shot. Nanfuri's *Carefully*, not to anyone, just to his men and himself. The way the station had turned the incident, within an hour, into a story that already knew its villain — land guards, barrier, Bad Luck. How quickly a town filled a silence with noise when the truth was tight-lipped.

His phone said 11:47. No message. Mansa's phone, when he tried not to call it and then called it because not calling felt like another silence, rang once, twice, and went to that flat nothing that meant off, not ringing elsewhere.

He told himself she had switched it off to punish him. It fit the shape of her anger — specific, pointed. It did not occur to him, in that concrete way that facts occur, that a phone could die from a fall, from impact, from being on a woman who had tried to follow her husband quietly through a window because another woman had said *if you no follow, how you go know?* He did not know that, seven minutes earlier, Mansa had put her hands on the sill, that the pawpaw leaves had brushed her wrist, that the ground had been closer than it looked by day.

He sat at Last Stop/junction — same barrier he had seen broken that afternoon — and let the night widen around him. Somewhere west, a generator started and stopped. Somewhere east, toward the cemetery that lay beyond his house but could not be seen from here, a dog began to bark with purpose, as if someone had passed the iron gate, and then was quiet.

Kwame did not know that east. He knew only that the road out of Mallam Town at night found you, that his gate at home required a palm to stay quiet, that his wife's last sentence had used words she did not mean to mean and he had answered with silence that she would hear as meaning. He had left because leaving felt like peacemaking. He understood, dimly, already too late, that leaving could also be read as going.

At 12:03 his phone lit — not Mansa, the station.

Yaw's voice, small with tiredness and excitement: “Station master, Charlie, sorry for night — drivers stranded for Last Stop side, they need car. You dey come?”

Kwame looked at the road. Last Stop was where he sat. The road to the station was behind him, the road home was behind him the other way. The road out was dark.

He stood. The wall's warmth stayed on his palms a second longer. He answered the way he always answered that call, the same immovable duty that had made him pay for eggs at seven a.m. and stand between an elbow and a boy's face.

“I dey come,” he said.

He walked back toward the hill, toward the gate that would need his palm again, toward the room that was not empty in his mind yet, toward the car that would take him, in minutes, away from the compound and toward the three people waiting near Last Stop who would ask him for a ride and, when he refused, would reach through his window to kill his engine.

He did not check his phone again. It had gone flat and silent in his hand without him noticing, because the bang that would have told him to look outside the window had already been ignored by someone else, and because night, having found its pothole, does not announce it.
