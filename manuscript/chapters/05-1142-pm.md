# Chapter 5 — 11:42 p.m.

*Day 0, 11:42 p.m. onward — the lane down from the hill*

The road down from the hill at night was a different road.

That was not a metaphor the first time Kwame said it to junior drivers — *During the day, you could see the potholes coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you* — it was a fact you learned with your tyres before you learned it with your chest. By day the Mallam road out was manageable if you paid attention: you picked your line early, let the left tyre kiss the good tar, missed the hole that had been there since the rains took the shoulder. At night, same road, same holes, but you drove by memory and hope, and the hole chose you. The hole found you while you were looking for something else — a gate that would not screech, a wife who had said *good for nothing* and meant *you no dey talk true*, a phone that said *dead* but was read as *off*.

He walked it without a torch.

He knew where the potholes were by day — he had memorized them in the way drivers do — pillow-sized near the church corner, lip-deep where gravel thinned to packed earth — but at night they waited without being seen, which is how the worst potholes work. He walked in the middle of the lane because the edges were bush where dogs tied loose tested barks and gave up, and the middle, even in darkness, kept a faint grey that was not light but the memory of light that the tar had kept from day. His shoes — church shoes, not driving shoes — scuffed where the tar had thinned to gravel, then to packed earth that still held the day's heat under the soles.

Somewhere behind him, a dog tested a bark and gave up. Crickets did not give up. The compound's gate — lifted, silent, as always when he did it right — was now behind him and, with it, the thing he had not said. Three sentences that would have made ₵300 ordinary: *I borrow for Madam eggs, I hide because shame, no woman.* He had carried them like money he forgot to spend.

He had not meant to make silence large. He had meant to make it small.

Keep quiet long enough for other people's noise to die down and you could step back into the room and find the noise gone. That worked at the station at seven a.m. with a crowd that wanted a boy on an okada to be beaten for thirty broken eggs — *leave am*, quiet, flat, between elbow and face, and the crowd had let go. It worked with senior drivers when they asked about queue order and he gave a short answer that held. It worked with the preacher's PA when he checked the verse silently rather than arguing and the preacher kept fundraising. He had seen it work that morning and carried that proof the way he carried his sales pouch — as if peace were a thing you could put in a pocket and take out when needed.

Now, on the lane, the same silence felt heavier than the bag he was not carrying, heavier than the ₵295 he still owed. Mansa's words — *You're just good for nothing* — had not been loud the way crowds are loud; they had been specific, and specificity, at night, did not die down when left alone. It sharpened on the walk like a stone you realize is in your shoe only after you have walked far enough for it to cut.

He passed the first house below the hill — window dark, a generator's cord coiled and sleeping — and the second — a television flickering without sound behind lace, a child asleep with mouth open — and told himself he would walk to Last Stop and sit, just sit, until the compound settled the way a station settles after a loud thing stops. It was what he did when they argued in eight rooms with thin walls. He went to the junction, where the police barrier was, where the road chose directions — cemetery east, uncompleted bush north, town south — and he sat at the low wall at the edge where no one needed him to speak and no one could hear thin walls decide what he had done.

He was fifty steps from the compound — about the distance of seven houses measured in houses — when, behind him, the sound happened.

It was not loud.

In the compound, someone else — a woman who kept her radio low and whose name would later matter as the unnamed witness who heard but dismissed — heard it as a bang and, not knowing where it came from because thin walls make every bang seem elsewhere, ignored it. That was the plant the Ledger promised: a witness who unknowingly heard the one sound that would have changed everything, and said nothing because it did not seem worth mentioning.

At Kwame's distance, fifty steps down the lane at 11:44, it was less than a bang — a soft, flat impact wrapped in pawpaw leaves, the kind of sound a pawpaw makes when it falls and does not quite break but bruises, or when a crate settles after being put down heavy. Leaves — big as hands — took the sound and kept it. The night, which by day you could swerve around, at night found a different hole: a woman's hands on a sill low, worn where hands grip, Abena's voice still warm in her ear — *you still think I should follow him? if you no follow how you go know?* — curtain catching then not holding, ground closer than it looked by day, phone in hand, phone on impact.

Kwame did not turn.

He thought, briefly, that it was fruit. That was the translation a man gives a sound when he has not been given a better translation and when the other translation — *your wife just fell while trying to follow you quietly so the gate would not screech* — has not been invented for him yet because windows are for air and watching, not for exiting like a thief, not for proving *good for nothing* is not true.

Then he thought of nothing, because he was trying, with the sincerity of a man who believed stillness was a kind of repair and who had repaired a crowd that morning with stillness, to let the house have quiet without him in it. He walked. The sincerity was real. The repair was wrong.

Last Stop at 11:52 was not a place people sat at night unless they had to — drivers stranded, policemen half-asleep, boys who had nowhere else to wait for a fare that would not come because *Bad Luck Nkoaa* had made people stay home.

The barrier pole was up. The police post was empty but for a single bulb over the desk that buzzed and threw a thin cone of light that did not reach the road — Style A's wedge, white on black, cross-hatching on dust where light hit, flat black where it did not. The junction smelled of dust and, faintly, of fried oil from the closed kiosk that had fed the station at six a.m. and now kept its oil as memory. Beyond it, seven houses down — he did not count, but the town counted and the ledger counted — Saaba's new house showed a single warm light behind glass, the light of a daughter on speaker with her father: *Daddy? You dey house? I lock —*. Kwame saw it without registering it the way you see a house you have passed a hundred times and will be told later was the reason a tinted Camry broke a barrier at four p.m.

He sat on the low wall where he had sat that afternoon to count money that had not been enough — ₵285, then later ₵295 — and where, in daylight, you could see the potholes and swerve. The wall was still warm from day. He put his elbows on his knees, hands clasped, not slumped, not upright — the way to sit at Last Stop that tells anyone who passes what kind of man you are: waiting, not wasting.

The afternoon came back to him uninvited, as afternoon does when night is quiet enough to let it. The black Camry hitting the pole — not shatter, but hollow *thwack* — the striped pole swinging, then dust. The pickup limping back with both left tyres shot to ruined, not punctured, rims sound that made passengers wince. Nanfuri standing dusty to the shoulders, hat in hands, looking through the station to somewhere east where the cemetery's crooked post kept a cleared path, saying *Carefully* to boys who had expected *after them*. The way the station, within an hour, turned the incident into a story that already knew its villain — land guards, barrier, Bad Luck — and the barber saying *fresh house, no wall finish — na invitation*. How quickly a town fills a silence with noise when the truth is tight-lipped and still holds its hat.

His phone said 11:47. No message. Mansa's phone, when he tried not to call it and then called it because not calling felt like another silence that would be read as *he no call, so he no care*, rang once, twice, and went to that flat nothing that meant off, not ringing elsewhere, not *switched off by vex* but *dead* the way a thing dies when it hits ground with the hand that holds it — the same flat nothing he had heard at 12:18 in the empty room.

He told himself she had switched it off to punish him. It fit the shape of her anger on that night — specific, pointed, accountant's anger where numbers become morals: *you hide money, so you hide woman*. It did not occur to him, in that concrete way that facts occur when they are given names like *Last Stop* and *seven houses*, that a phone could die from a fall, from impact, from being on a woman who had tried to follow her husband quietly through a window because another woman in the next room had said *if you no follow, how you go know?* and because following quietly meant not using the door the compound had just heard him slam. He did not know that, seven minutes earlier, at perhaps 11:44, Mansa had put her hands on the sill low, worn where hands grip, that pawpaw leaves had brushed her wrist the way curtains brush ankles with drafts, that the ground — during the day you could see the drop and swerve — at night found her.

He sat at Last Stop/junction — same barrier he had seen broken that afternoon, same wall where he had zipped a sales pouch at 10 a.m. — and let the night widen around him the way bush widens at night and noon does not.

Somewhere west, a generator started and stopped — same one from afternoon, perhaps. Somewhere east, toward the cemetery that lay beyond his house but could not be seen from here because hills block gates, a dog began to bark with purpose, as if someone had passed the iron gate that was also a staging ground's throat, and then was quiet, as if corrected.

Kwame did not know that east. He knew only that the road out of Mallam Town at night found you whether you were driving or walking, that his gate at home required a palm flat and a lift to stay quiet, that his wife's last sentence — *good for nothing* — had used words she did not mean to mean and he had answered with silence that she would hear as meaning. He had left because leaving felt like peacemaking — same category as standing between an elbow and a boy — and he was understanding, dimly, already too late, already fifty steps beyond a soft flat impact wrapped in leaves, that leaving could also be read as going, and going as flight, and flight as guilt, and that the town at kiosks had already begun to write that reading.

At 12:03 his phone lit — not Mansa, not again the flat nothing, but the station's small light.

Yaw's voice, smaller with tiredness and excitement and the particular youth of a man asked to call his station master at night: “Station master, Charlie, sorry for night — drivers stranded for Last Stop side, they dey for station? They beg. Two passengers. You dey come? We no get car.”

Yaw did not know about the room empty, the curtain, the pawpaw tree, the window low, the phone dead from impact. He knew about stranded passengers the way station masters always know: someone needs a car at the edge of town at midnight because market made them late and because they trust a station master who comes.

Kwame looked at the road. Last Stop was where he sat — the junction where barrier had been broken, where tyres had been shot, where *Carefully* had been said, where *Bad Luck Nkoaa* had been laughed. The road to the station was behind him, dusty, flat, queues waiting in his mind. The road home was behind him the other way — lane, gate, curtain, pawpaw, empty. The road out was dark and, by definition, found you.

He stood. The wall's warmth stayed on his palms a second longer, the way day stays on walls at night.

He answered the way he always answered that call, the same immovable duty that had made him pay for a Madam's thirty eggs at seven a.m. with ₵150+₵150 borrowed, and stand between an elbow and a boy's face without shouting, and refuse even at gunpoint later that night because he was looking for his wife.

“I dey come,” he said. Two words, terse, checking no verse.

He walked back toward the hill, toward the gate that would need his palm again — lift, push, quiet — toward the room that was not empty in his mind yet, toward the car that would take him, in minutes, away from the compound and toward the three people waiting near Last Stop — two men and a woman with a bag — who would ask him for a ride and, when he refused even for extra ₵50, would reach through his window that was open only a hand to kill his ignition, snap his wire-fixed handle, drag him to gravel at the same junction where he counted money, and seven houses later leave his Corolla where it would tag a police pikin.

He did not check his phone again. It had gone flat and silent in his hand without him noticing — not because he did not care, but because the bang that would have told him to look outside the window had already been heard by someone else and ignored as fruit, and because night, having found its pothole seven steps behind a man who thought silence was repair, does not announce it. It lets you walk back to it.
