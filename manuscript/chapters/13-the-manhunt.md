# Chapter 13 — The Manhunt

*Day 1, ~4:30 p.m. onward — the uncompleted building to Nene's bush path*

At 4:30 the town came for him the way it came for everything — building to building.

Kwame heard it before he saw it because the uncompleted building had no door to muffle and the bush around it carried sound well when it wanted to. A whistle — not the policeman's whistle, a boy's — then a man's voice, then another, overlapping until the road's line became a calling line.

“Make we start for uncompleted side! — No, that one done! — Check that one! — Oga, next house!”

He pressed his back flatter to the half-wall. The shadow that had been his cover at 4:15 now felt thin. Dust, when disturbed by many feet, rose and made the wedge of light visible in a way that felt like being pointed at.

A pair of slippers appeared in the doorway wedge and stopped.

Kwame held the only silence he could control — breath shallow, ribs refusing — and watched the slippers. White linework on black: the wedge on the slippers' straps, the cross-hatching on the leaves behind them, the flat black of the doorway's unlit half where his body was.

The slippers belonged to a boy. He was not alone. A second, older voice, lower, said, “Inside, look proper. Not just eye.”

The boy leaned in — not far, just enough that his head crossed the light. Kwame could see his cheek, his quick glance at sand and sachet bags, at the half-wall behind which a man was being quiet in the same way a gate is quiet when you lift it — demanding, practiced.

The boy did not lean further. He had been told to check uncompleted buildings and he had checked this one's doorway. Uncompleted buildings were notorious, bush-surrounded, known for hoodlums and peddlers, which made them both the first place to look and the place you looked quickly because you had been warned they were dangerous.

“Nothing,” the boy called back.

“Next,” the older voice said. Whistle again.

Slippers withdrew. The wedge was empty.

Kwame did not move.

Later, when Nene told him what the manhunt looked like from the other side, she would describe the line: police at the front with a list, community boys between houses, and Chocho's men interspersed, not leading, not following, but present — quiet, Lacoste collars flat, slippers silent, organizing not by shouting but by a nod that made the line correct itself. It read, at the time, faintly suspicious. Helping search could be controlling outcome. That suspicion was a genuine red herring: Chocho's cooperation would turn out to be sincere, but Kwame, on his floor, tasted the suspicion anyway when he heard, faintly, Chocho's voice for two seconds at the road edge:

“Make boys check Nene side last. She go talk.”

Not an order, a suggestion that moved men.

Kwame heard that and misread it as the whole town looking for him to prove he had pushed his wife — which was half true. The other half — that the same men were also looking for a man in a Camry who had taken his car seven houses to a new house — he could not hear from his half-wall.

At 5:00 the light changed. The wedge moved off the sand and onto the block. Flat black grew. Somewhere west, a generator started — the same one from last night, or another. The calling line had moved further toward Last Stop, then beyond it, then back. They were working systematically along the Mallam road, building to building, last house being Nene's near the bush.

Nene was gathering herbs.

He heard her before she saw him.

Not the search — her. A soft, unhurried sound in bush that knew its own paths: a stick tapping, a breath, a small hum that was not a song but the way older women keep time when hands are busy. She came from the north side, where the herbs grew best near the uncompleted buildings, and she did not call out. Her eyes, when they found the doorway, found the wedge, found the sand, found the half-wall, found the man behind it.

She was not surprised in the way the search would be when it found him behind a door. Her face did what faces do when they have doubted a story and the doubt is proven — not triumph, but a small, firm nod.

She put a finger to her lips — not to him as a command, but as a sharing — and motioned, small: stay.

Then, practical, she moved deeper into the ruin, between him and the doorway, as if she herself were the wall that would now keep him.

The search line called again, closer this time, but from the road side. Her hut was last. They would reach it by morning.

Night was coming. Night, when potholes found you, when manhunts paused for poor visibility and postponed to next day. That pause would be the mercy that let her guide him, after dark, to her door, and let his cracked phone be taken to charge somewhere a boy would not see it.

For now, at 5:40, Kwame had only her back — gentle healer, firm enough to stand off an armed search team alone — between his silence and the town's noise.

He had not yet learned, from her mouth, what the town had already decided he did.
