# Chapter 13 — The Manhunt

*Day 1, ~4:30 p.m. onward — the uncompleted building to Nene's bush path*

At 4:30 the town came for him the way it came for everything — building to building.

Not quickly, not with sirens — Mallam Town did manhunts the way it did everything else when formal and informal authority tangled: police with a list at the front, community boys in the middle who knew which wall was loose and which bush still held snakes, and Chocho's men interspersed, not leading, not following, but present — Lacoste flat, slippers silent, organizing not by shouting but by a nod that made the line correct itself. Later, Nene would describe it to Kwame exactly that way. Now, on his floor behind a half-wall at four-thirty, he heard it before he saw it because the uncompleted building had no door to muffle and the bush around it carried sound well when it wanted to and swallowed it when it did not.

A whistle — not the policeman's whistle, a boy's, plastic — then a man's voice, hoarse from calling since morning, then another, fresher, overlapping until the road's line became a calling line that moved like a queue at the station, house to house.

“Make we start for uncompleted side! — No, that one done! — Check that one! — Oga, next house!”

He pressed his back flatter to the half-wall.

The shadow that had been his cover at 4:15 now felt thin. That was the trick of the single pale wedge through the doorway: at noon it made dust visible grain by grain, at four thirty it made any moving air visible as well, and many feet moved a lot of air. Dust, when disturbed by many feet, rose and made the wedge of light visible in a way that felt like being pointed at — white linework carved from near-solid black, dense fine-line cross-hatching on the sand's texture where light hit, sparse on the flat black of the doorway's unlit half where his body was.

A pair of slippers appeared in the wedge and stopped.

They were cheap slippers, blue, the strap worn. Kwame held the only silence he could control — breath shallow, ribs refusing to be ribs, the tooth still mobile — and watched the slippers. White linework on the straps where sun hit, cross-hatching on the leaves behind them where the wedge hit, flat black in the half where his body was and where, in this noir, nothing was shown so the eye would be forced to look at the lit thing.

The slippers belonged to a boy. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, old enough to be given a whistle and not old enough to be given a reason.

He was not alone. A second, older voice, lower, belonging to a man who had been at the station that morning when Kwame intervened for the egg seller — perhaps the same man who had raised an elbow — said, “Inside, look proper. Not just eye.”

The boy leaned in — not far, just enough that his head crossed the light. Kwame could see his cheek, the still-soft mustache, the quick glance that wanted to be thorough but was also afraid of what thorough might find in a notorious place. Bush-surrounded uncompleted buildings were known for hoodlums and peddlers; boys were told to check them and also told, by older brothers, not to stay long inside them. He looked at sand and sachet bags, at the half-wall behind which a man was being quiet in the same way a gate is quiet when you lift it — demanding, practiced, the palm flat taking weight off hinge.

The boy did not lean further. He had been told to check uncompleted buildings and he had checked this one's doorway. On his floor, Kwame could smell the boy's faint sweat — young, nervous — and his own dried blood.

“Nothing,” the boy called back, already stepping out. Not lying; just seeing what the light allowed and declaring it whole.

“Next,” the older voice said. Whistle again, small and plastic, as if to agree.

Slippers withdrew.

The wedge was empty.

Kwame did not move.

He counted. One. Two. Three breaths where breathing was a negotiation. He did not let the relief become a sound. Relief, in a search, is a sound people are listening for.

For five minutes the calling line moved away toward the road, toward Last Stop where the barrier pole had been hit and where, seven houses away, a new house with glass windows now had a rope around its front while a different search — CID, not community — had found a recording of a father's call. Kwame could not hear that search. He heard only this one, receding, then — because the search was systematic along the Mallam road — returning on the other side of the building, as if the line had overshot and corrected.

During that lull he heard, faintly, a voice he recognized without wanting to.

Chocho.

Not loud — Chocho never needed loud. Two seconds at the road edge, not inside the building, not at the wedge, but close enough that the bush carried it when bush wanted to:

“Make boys check Nene side last. She go talk.”

Not an order. A suggestion that moved men the way a nod moves a queue. Practical. Transactional. The kind of sentence that later, when the recording's *Musah bring car comot* would be played and Chocho would say *not my boy — outside group*, would be reread as sincere. Now, on a floor behind a half-wall at 4:40, it read — as the Ledger had planned the red herring — as faintly suspicious. Helping search could be controlling outcome. *Make boys check Nene side last* could be *let me arrange where they find him*.

Kwame heard that and misread it as the whole town looking for him to prove he had pushed his wife — which was half true. The other half — that the same men were also looking for a man in a Camry who had taken his car seven houses to a new house — he could not hear from his half-wall. Gossip that morning at the barber — *new house, no paper — na invitation* — had not yet become a case file that needed a name.

At 5:00 the light changed.

The wedge moved off the sand and onto the block, making the block's aggregate visible — small stones like knuckles — and then, as the sun lowered, onto the rebar's rust, making it darker. Flat black grew where sand had been lit. Somewhere west, a generator started — the same one from last night near the junction, or another west of the cemetery where cleared paths waited and where tyre marks continued past where the eye thought they stopped. The calling line had moved further toward Last Stop, then beyond it toward the seven-house house, then back, then gone.

They were working systematically, building to building, house to house, along the Mallam road that was, by definition, a tissue of informal walls and formal posts. Last house, everyone knew, was Nene's near the bush where herbs grew best. That was the last house because she had chosen it last.

Nene was gathering herbs.

He heard her before she saw him.

Not the search — her. A soft, unhurried sound in bush that knew its own paths and was not afraid of snakes: a stick tapping at the density test, a breath that had its own rhythm, a small hum that was not a song but the way older women keep time when hands are busy — a low *hmm-hmm* on two notes. She came from the north side, where the *uncompleted* bush gave the best bitter leaves near where the hoodlums left sachets, and she did not call out. Her eyes, when they found the doorway, found the wedge, found the sand, found the half-wall, found the man behind it, found the cracked phone on his chest where the tall man had placed it, found the blood line from nostril to lip that had dried and then cracked.

She was not surprised in the way the search would be tomorrow when it found him behind a door and shouted *HE DEY!* Her face did what faces do when they have doubted a story and the doubt is proven — not triumph, not *I told you*, but a small, firm nod that said *as I thought*.

She put a finger to her lips — not to him as a command, but as a sharing — and motioned, small: stay. The gesture was the same one she would use tomorrow at her hut when boys surrounded and she said *you no enter* without raising voice. Gentle with him, unsentimental with them — fixed.

Then, practical, she moved deeper into the ruin, between him and the doorway, as if she herself were the wall that would now keep him and as if her small body, herbalist, elder, protector, could do the work of block and rebar.

She crouched. Up close, she smelled of the morning's work.

“Shhh,” she breathed, not shushing him — he was not making sound — but the bush, the search line that might come back, the boys who had said *nothing* too quickly. She lifted the cracked phone from his chest with the care of a woman who knows how to take a phone to charge somewhere a boy will not see it, slipped it into her wrapper, and put her hand briefly on his forearm where the gate-lifting lived.

The search line called again, closer this time, but from the road side, not the bush side. Her hut was last. They would reach it by morning, not now. That was the ledger's mercy: pause for poor visibility and postpone to next day.

Night was coming. Night, when potholes found you, when the road out at night was genuinely different from day, when manhunts paused because no streetlights made noon of bush, when a mother under a pawpaw tree would already be in a ward with a faint pulse and a curtain that had moved.

For now, at 5:40, Kwame had only her back — gentle healer, firm enough to stand off an armed search team alone — between his silence and the town's noise.

He had not yet learned, from her mouth in a hut that evening, what the town had already decided he did behind a door with a gate that had not screeched and a phone that had died not by choice but by impact.

She hummed again — two notes — and the search line, not knowing it had passed the man it sought, went to check the next uncompleted building that was not notorious for being the one.
