# Chapter 22 — Mansa Wakes

*Timed to Kwame's breaking point / near arraignment — custody hospital*

They told him at the hour he was supposed to stop hoping.

Arraignment had been written on a form that morning — a date, a court, a room where *attempted* and *murder* would be read as if they were two equal sentences. Kwame had signed the margin note about seven houses and Musah, and the officer had taken it, and the flat light had stayed flat, and the pothole line in his head — *During the day, you could see them, at night they found you* — had started to feel less like a road fact and more like a verdict.

Then the door opened midday and a nurse, not a policeman, said, “Your wife dey awake.”

No *Mansa* — *your wife* — but Kwame heard Mansa's full name in the way Mansa's voice had once said his full name, *Kwame Asante, you be elder?* The constable at the door looked to another constable, who looked to the officer who had played *Musah* — chain of looking that meant *permission needed*.

Permission came, quickly for once, because a woman waking with a head wound who could answer *who pushed you?* was a kind of evidence that even flat rooms wanted.

They walked him. Not cuffed to the table now, cuffs loosened, one hand held not by rope but by a man's hand on his elbow — the same kind of holding his Corolla's wire-fixed door had needed at Last Stop, not to restrain but to guide.

The hospital smelled as hospitals do at noon — hot, disinfectant trying to be air. The ward had curtains that were also white, like the curtain at home that had moved with a draft, but these were pulled for ceremony, not for wind. Abena stood outside one of them, not inside, as if she had been told to wait for the part where she would be needed next.

Mansa lay small.

She had always been precise — exercise book, red cover, counting — and now precision lived in bandages. Her head wrapped, her left arm splinted. A thin tube, not dramatic, just present. Her eyes, thirty-eight, were open and looking at the ceiling where a fan turned slowly enough to count.

Kwame stopped at the curtain's edge. He did not go to her with noise. He did what he had not done at 11:42 — he spoke before silence had time to become distance.

“Mansa,” he said. Quiet, not flat. The same terse, but now with the crack Mansa had once made on *answer me* — this time his.

She turned her head. Small movement. Bandage made it smaller.

“Kwame,” she said. Voice sharp still, but exposed the other way — not anger, not yet, but pain that had not decided where to put itself.

A doctor — not Nanfuri, not Chocho, just a man in a coat that had not been white for long — said, “You get five minutes. She tire. Ask am simple.” He looked at Kwame, then at the constable, and stepped slightly back, giving them the ordinary privacy that custody rarely gives.

Kwame pulled the stool close. The constable stayed at the curtain but not inside it — supervised reunion, so this was not just reported at distance.

Mansa looked at his face — beaten, healing, behind a door — and at his hands — cuffed loosely, palms still dusty from uncompleted building though washed — and her eyes filled in the way his had not at Last Stop because he had thought *switched off*.

“You fall?” he asked. He meant *you fall?* not *did he push?* — the difference the town had not made and that the Ledger had locked as *not pushed*.

She swallowed. Her voice, when it came, was not the shouted *good for nothing* of 11:42 — it was the earlier, smaller voice from behind the thin wall, the one that had said *you still think I should follow him?* to Abena and had received *if you no follow, how you go know?*

“I fall,” Mansa said. To him. Not to the doctor, not to the exercise book. To him.

She told it plain, because plain was the only volume she had left.

“You walk comot. I vex. Abena tell me —” She paused, glanced to where, beyond the curtain, Abena's small radio voice would have been if radios were allowed here. “She say follow, then I go know if you hide money or woman. I think say you go Last Stop. I no want pass gate — everybody hear door — so I take window.”

Kwame looked at the window in his memory — hand's breadth open, sill low, worn where hands gripped, pawpaw leaves big as hands — and at the sill in the ward — metal, not wood — and understood, for the first time with his body, what Abena's advice had cost when acted on quietly in a compound where quiet travels through walls.

“Sill low,” Mansa said, as if he had asked. “I slip. Curtain catch but no hold. I fall for head. Phone for hand — fall with me.” She made a small gesture with her splinted hand. “Break.”

Dead, not switched off. Impact, not choice. The flat nothing on his screen at Last Stop, at 12:18, at the empty room.

“I no push,” he said, not as a defense to a court but as a repetition for a wife who needed to hear him say it to her, not to a file.

She tried to shake her head, bandage made it a nod.

“You no push,” she whispered. “I fall. My fault. Abena —” She did not finish that either, because that fault belonged to Ch.23, to a confession that would need its own five minutes in a different room.

The doctor stepped half in: “Small.”

Mansa reached — splinted arm, clumsy, true — and touched Kwame's wrist where gate-muffling had lived and where rope had been and where cuff now was loose. Abena, beyond the curtain, made a small sound — relief or guilt, not yet speech.

Kwame, who had kept quiet at 11:42 long enough for other people's noise to die and had learned that silence can be distance, said the three sentences he had owed the hill on the dark lane and could now, at breaking point, finally afford:

“I borrow ₵300 for Madam eggs. I hide because shame. No woman.”

He had said them to a screen at Last Stop. Now he said them to the woman they were for.

Mansa's eyes filled.

“Five,” Mansa said, tiny, accountant even here. “You short five. I count. Bad Luck *Nkoaa*.”

She had counted. She had always counted. The number was not the money. The number was proof he had told true.

The constable at the curtain said, gentle, “Time.”

Kwame stood. He did not kiss her because bandage made her face a different map. He put his forehead, briefly, to her hand — splinted, careful.

Outside, Abena waited for her own chapter, where *you still think I should follow him?* would need to be said back, not through a wall but face-to-face, and where a town's second nervous system — kiosks, salons, bars — would learn that the window was not a push and that a window needs to be said as a confession to the man who had not looked beyond it.

Inside, the attempted-murder charge began to loosen its knot — not the Saaba charge, not yet, that one needed Musah and Chocho and a cemetery gate with backup — but this one, this room, this *I fall*.

Kwame walked back to the flat room with the constable's hand on his elbow, five cedis at last accounted for, gate-quiet finally broken not by a screech he had let happen on purpose but by a woman's waking voice that said *I fall* at the very minute an arraignment had waited to say otherwise.
