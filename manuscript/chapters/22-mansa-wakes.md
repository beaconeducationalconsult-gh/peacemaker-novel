# Chapter 22 — Mansa Wakes

*Timed to Kwame's breaking point / near arraignment — custody hospital — flat light*

They told him at the hour he was supposed to stop hoping.

Arraignment had been written on a form that morning — a date, a court, a room where *attempted* and *murder* would be read as if they were two equal sentences and where a man's terse *I no do am. Both.* would be made to sound thin even though it was true. Kwame had signed the margin note about *Last Stop = barrier, 7 houses to Saaba house, car steal not lent, phone dead not off, outside group* and the officer had taken it with a small nod that meant *we file but we also hear*, and the flat light — Style B's daylight, uniform outline, minimal hatching, white walls — had stayed flat, and the pothole line in his head — *During the day, you could see them coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you* — had started to feel less like a road fact he used to teach junior drivers and more like a verdict on himself: he had driven at night with silence as his headlights and been found by what his headlights did not show — a window low, a curtain draft, a phone dead from impact.

Then the door opened midday and a nurse, not a policeman, not a constable, not the *fact* officer, said, “Your wife dey awake.”

No *Mansa* — *your wife* — the hospital's generic that makes every marriage sound the same until you hear the name inside it. But Kwame heard Mansa's full name — Mansa, thirty-eight, exercise book red cover, counting — in the way Mansa's voice had once said his full name at the compound's thin wall — *Kwame Asante, you be elder? you be station master?* — and in the way thin walls had made that name travel.

The constable at the door — young, the one who had straightened the barrier pole after the Camry hit — looked to another constable, who looked to the officer who had played *Musah bring car* on a phone copy — chain of looking that meant *permission needed* and that here meant *permission wanted quickly* because a woman waking with a head wound who could answer *who pushed you? — did he push you or did you fall?* — was a kind of evidence that even flat rooms, with their uniform line weight and storybook pots, wanted more than paper.

Permission came, quickly for once, because procedure also wants a witness who was not a constable's draft.

They walked him. Not cuffed to the table ring now, cuffs loosened, one hand held not by rope or by metal to steel but by a man's hand on his elbow — the same kind of holding his Corolla's wire-fixed door handle had needed at Last Stop — not to restrain but to guide, not to prove *we tie* but to say *we walk with you*.

The hospital smelled as hospitals do at noon in Accra — hot, disinfectant trying to be air and losing, floor mopped with water that was already dust again by the door. Fans turned slowly enough to count, which Mansa would count. The ward had curtains that were also white, like the curtain at home that had moved with a cool outside-to-inside draft for Kwame at 12:18 and for a constable at mid-morning, but these curtains were pulled for ceremony, not for wind, and their hem did not move. Outside the curtains, other beds, other families, other small radios low — not Kasoa FM adding emotions, just a game.

Abena stood outside one of those white curtains, not inside, as if she had been told — correctly — to wait for the part where she would be needed next in Chapter 23, where *you still think I should follow him?* would need to be said back face-to-face. She wore a cloth too bright for a hospital, the salon's brightness, and she held a plastic bag with a sachet unused. Her eyes were red in the way *Nkonkonsa* gets when gossip has stopped being entertainment and has become a name for a debt.

Mansa lay small.

She had always been precise — exercise book, red cover, counting in a hand that made even cots look neat, pen clipped — and now precision lived in bandages. Her head wrapped — white, not storybook white but true white that showed a faint, honest seep at the temple — her left arm splinted from fall angle, a thin tube, not dramatic, just present, taped. A chart at her foot had numbers that were not hers but were about her. Her eyes, thirty-eight, were open and looking at the ceiling where a fan turned slowly enough to count — one, two, three — and where, in another building two days earlier, a curtain had moved.

Kwame stopped at the curtain's edge.

He did not go to her with noise. He did what he had not done at 11:42 — where silence had been distance and leaving had been read as flight — he spoke before silence had time to become distance again.

“Mansa,” he said. Quiet, not flat. The same terse voice that had said *leave am* at the egg seller and *I no dey go Lapaz* at gun, but now with the crack Mansa had once made on *answer me* — the crack that fear makes when fear is not allowed to be fear. His. Hers. Now his.

She turned her head. Small movement. Bandage made it smaller — the head had to turn but the cloth stayed, so the eyes turned more. She saw him — beaten face healing, lip less cracked, eye more open, cuffs loose — and her eyes did the accounting they always did, but now the accounting was not money.

“Kwame,” she said. Voice sharp still, because sharp was her voice, but exposed the other way — not anger, not yet, not performance. Pain that had not decided where to put itself, and relief that had.

A doctor — not Nanfuri, not Chocho, not an interrogator — just a man in a coat that had not been white for long and a nurse behind him, said, “You get five minutes. She tire. Ask am simple. No long story.” He looked at Kwame, then at the constable who waited at the curtain but not inside it — supervised reunion, so this was not just reported at distance, so the novel could have *you were there* rather than *you were told* — and stepped slightly back, giving them the ordinary privacy that custody rarely gives but that a ward can, because wards have learned that families heal when they are allowed to be families even for five minutes.

Kwame pulled the plastic stool close. The constable stayed at the curtain's edge, not inside it — present, not listening, the thin-wall ideal: there but not.

Mansa looked at his face — beaten, healing, behind a door and then a hut and then a rope — and at his hands — cuffed loosely, palms still holding the faint dust of the uncompleted building though washed, the same palms that had lifted a gate every night to make it not screech — and her eyes filled in the way his had not at Last Stop at midnight because at Last Stop he had thought *switched off to punish* and here he knew *dead from impact*.

“You fall?” he asked.

He meant *you fall?* not *did he push?* — the difference the town at kiosks and salons had not made, that two competing stories — *he push for other woman* and *she push him and it backfire* — had both gotten wrong the way a queue behind the wrong destination sign gets right loudly. He meant *you fall* the way the Ledger had locked as *not pushed, not pushed, not pushed*.

She swallowed. Her throat made a small sound the splint made louder.

Her voice, when it came, was not the shouted *You're just good for nothing* of 11:42 that had been fear turned into attack and that Abena's radio had clicked off on. It was the earlier, smaller voice from behind the thin wall at 8 p.m., the one that had said *you still think I should follow him?* to Abena and had received *if you no follow, how you go know? You for know, Kwame keep quiet, eehh, na so they be,* the line that was recommendation before it was canon and was advice before it was cause.

“I fall,” Mansa said. To him. Not to the doctor, not to the exercise book with its red cover, not to the chart at her foot. To him — the person the sentence was for.

She told it plain, because plain was the only volume she had left and because plain in a hospital, at noon, with a fan counting, is louder than shouting at midnight through thin walls.

“You walk comot — I vex, eehh — small vex, big fear. Abena tell me —” She paused, glanced to where, beyond the white curtain, beyond the ward's other beds, Abena's bright cloth would have been if white curtains were not ceremony. “She say follow, then I go know if you hide money or woman. She say — you know Abena — ‘you still think I should follow him?’ She say ‘if you no follow, how you go know? You for know, follow quiet so gate no screech.’”

Kwame looked at the window in his memory — hand's breadth plus two fingers, sill low, worn where hands over years had gripped, wood smooth from children, pawpaw leaves big as hands, trunk close enough that fruit knocked roof — and at the sill in the ward — metal, hospital, not wood, high, not low — and understood, for the first time with his body and not just his outline, what Abena's advice had cost when acted on quietly in a compound where quiet travels through walls and where quiet was his own chosen peace.

“Sill low,” Mansa said, as if he had asked *how* and not *did you*. She answered the *how* because *how* is what wives tell husbands when they want forgiveness to have a shape. “I put hand for sill, I think say I go step like child — we be child for window before. Curtain catch for hand but no hold — thin, good cloth but thin. I slip for edge — dust for sill — I fall for head. Phone for hand — I hold because I want torch — fall with me. Break.” She made a small gesture with her splinted left hand, not showing, indicating — a small movement that said *phone on floor beside me, cracked as his was, dead not off*.

Dead, not switched off. Impact, not choice. The flat nothing on his screen at Last Stop at 11:47, at 12:18 in the empty room that he had read as *she punish*, not *she hit*.

“I no push,” he said, not as a defense to a court where *I no do am. Both.* had been filed, not to a file, but as a repetition for a wife who needed to hear him say it to her while looking at her bandage, not to a stamp.

She tried to shake her head — bandage made the shake a nod, the way thin walls make slammed door slammed when it was just closed — and then let the nod be.

“You no push,” she whispered, and her voice did the thing his had done at Last Stop wall — cracked, then held. “I fall. My fault. Abena — she talk, but I do. I do. Not you. I shame.”

Abena's name — not as blame, but as thread — belonged to the next chapter, where confession would need its own five minutes in a different room with officers listening and a plastic bag of water unused. Mansa did not finish that fault, because that fault was not hers to confess for another and because five minutes needs keeping.

The doctor stepped half in, hand raised, not to take but to mark: “Small. We need rest.”

Mansa reached — splinted arm, clumsy, true, not storybook — and touched Kwame's wrist where gate-muffling had lived for years with a palm flat and a lift, where rope had been at Nene's hut, where handcuff was now loose, where Nene's hand had been at 5:40 with water first. She touched not to hold but to place.

Abena, beyond the curtain's edge, made a small sound — relief or guilt, not yet speech, not yet the salon's *eehh* — just breath through a nose that had been crying quietly where no transcript records.

Kwame, who had kept quiet at 11:42 long enough for other people's noise to die down and had learned at the station that silence can be peace and at home that silence can be distance and in custody that silence can be filed as guilt, said now the three sentences he had owed the hill on the dark lane and could now, at breaking point, finally afford the way a man affords a debt when the debt has been counted and found to be ₵5 short:

“I borrow ₵300 for Madam eggs. Thirty eggs, Kofi elbow, crowd. I hide because shame, not because woman. Not hidden woman. I get ₵295 that day, short five.”

He had said them to a cracked screen at Last Stop — *I borrow for Madam eggs, I go pay tomorrow* — and the screen had not answered. Now he said them to the woman they were for, in a ward where counting matters.

Mansa's eyes filled fully, the way Kwame's eye at last had opened.

“Five,” Mansa said, tiny, accountant even here, even with splint, even with tube, even with bandage white not storybook. “You short five. I count yesterday? I no count — I vex. Today I count. You short five. Bad Luck *Nkoaa* — not bad wife.”

She had counted, or rather, she had just counted, and counted implies trust that numbers will add. She had always counted. The number was not the money. The number was proof he had told true now, at last, with the gate not screeching but speaking.

“You no be good for nothing,” she whispered, unsaying. Not as apology performed for officers, but as unsaying performed for a man in a ward. “You be — you dey — you for tell me, eehh. You for talk.”

The constable at the curtain said, gentle, not unkind, in the voice he used for *make way* at Nene's but softer: “Time. We go bring am again tomorrow. Doctor say rest.”

Kwame stood. He did not kiss her because bandage had made her face a different map where a kiss would need permission lips could not give. He did what Nene had done at 5:40 — practical, gentle — put his forehead, briefly, lightly, to her splinted hand's knuckles — careful of splint, careful of tube — and let his breath be the thing that touched.

Outside the curtain, Abena waited for her own chapter, where *you still think I should follow him?* would need to be said back, not through a wall but face-to-face to the man who had not looked beyond a window, and where a town's second nervous system — kiosks, salons, bars, food joints — would learn that the window was not a push and that a window needs to be said as confession to the man who had not looked beyond it, and to the woman who had.

Inside the curtain, the attempted-murder charge began to loosen its knot — not the Saaba charge, not yet, that one needed Musah the driver and Chocho's *not my boy — outside group* and a daylight arrest at a cemetery gate with backup and a pale wedge of sun that finally showed cleared paths — but this one, this room, this *I fall* said at noon while a fan turned slowly enough to count, said at the very minute an arraignment form had waited to say *arrain him* — loosened.

Kwame walked back to the flat room with the constable's hand on his elbow — not rope, not ring, just hand — five cedis at last accounted for, ₵300 at last spoken, gate-quiet finally broken not by a screech he had let happen on purpose in Chapter 26 but by a woman's waking voice that said *I fall — my fault, Abena talk but I do* at the hour he was supposed to stop hoping.

Behind him, the ward's white curtains — like the curtain at home but ceremony not wind — stayed pulled, and Abena's bright cloth stayed outside, waiting.
