# Chapter 6 — The Empty Room

*Day 0, just after midnight — minutes after 11:42 p.m.*

The gate needed his palm again, and he gave it.

Lift, push. No screech. He had hoped, in the short walk from Last Stop, that the house would be warm, that Mansa would be at the table with the exercise book open to make him say the thing he had not said. He had rehearsed it without moving his lips on the dark lane: *I borrow ₵300 for Madam eggs, I be short, I go pay tomorrow.* Three sentences. He could say three sentences.

The yard was dark except for the thin light from Abena's doorway, where her radio glowed. The pawpaw tree beyond their window was a black shape that sometimes, in wind, brushed the roofing sheet and sounded like knuckles. Tonight there was no wind.

He opened their door.

The room was empty and dark.

Not the dark of someone sleeping — that dark has a shape, a breathing hill under cloth — but the flat dark of a room that no one is in. The bed was made in the way Mansa made it when she was not going to sleep in it — too neat, corners tucked as if to show a point. The table where the sales pouch had been — ₵295 — no, he had left the pouch in the car? He had taken it. The table was bare except for the exercise book, closed, red cover catching no light.

“Mansa?”

His voice went into the room and came back without an answer. Thin walls made you hear your own neighbour's whispering; they also made a room sound very empty when the person who belonged in it was not there.

He switched the light. The bulb, economical, took a second to decide.

Empty.

He called her phone first before he searched the house. That was the order his mind chose: the nearest reach. It rang — or did not ring — the same flat nothing he had already heard at Last Stop. Once, twice, then nothing. Not switched off, the network said. Unreachable. Dead.

He told himself again she had switched it off. It was the easier translation: anger, not impact. Phones died when you let them, not when you fell.

He searched the house the way a man searches a house that is only four spaces — kitchen, washroom, main room, compound yard — and therefore cannot hide a person well but can hide exactly one.

Kitchen: plates from the evening's rice, washed, stacked. Stew covered. No note.

Washroom: bucket half-full, towel damp where he had left it.

Main room: Bible still on the stool, ribbon still at Matthew 5. No bag missing he could see, no slippers out of line.

Yard: he stepped out and looked, but not down. He looked outward, toward the lane, toward the gate, toward the road to Last Stop as if Mansa might be walking it because she had decided to prove something by walking at midnight. He called again.

“Mansa!” Louder this time, enough that Abena's doorway light shifted — someone moving inside, then deciding not to come out.

The curtain moved.

It had been moving all evening in small drafts, but now, with the door open behind him, the draft was cooler, more definite. The white curtain lifted and bellied inward, then settled. Cold air touched his ankle.

Kwame looked at the window.

The window was open a hand's breadth — as he had left it when he washed his hands earlier, to let heat out. Or a little more. It was hard to tell in that light. The sill was low, the kind built for sitting on, the kind children used to climb in and out of when they were small. The wood was worn where hands had gripped it.

He put his hand out to close it. His fingers touched sill. The curtain brushed his wrist, cool.

He did not lean out. He did not put his head beyond the frame and look down.

Why would he? She had left through a door — he had heard the door — and when people left to make a point, they used doors so the compound would hear. They did not use windows like thieves. The window was for watching him leave, perhaps. For air. For curtains.

The pawpaw tree was just outside, leaves big as hands, fruit dark. At night it made small sounds on the roof that you could mistake for anything. It did not occur to him, in that moment, to step around the outside wall and look down at the base where a body lay in a shape the night made invisible, where a phone with a cracked screen lay dead not because it was switched off but because it had hit the ground after a fall that was also quiet enough to be called *bang* and then ignored.

He did not check outside the window.

In his account, later, he would say this plainly to a policeman who would write it down as if it were a choice, as if he had decided not to look. He would say: *I search room, I search kitchen, I call phone — dead. I no check window.* And the policeman would underline *window*.

Now he stepped back, pulled the curtain inward with a small, irritated tug — as if the draft were the problem — and left the window as it was, open a hand's breadth, curtain still moving.

His phone said 12:18 a.m.

Yaw's voice still waited in his ear from Last Stop: *drivers stranded for Last Stop side, they need car.* Duty. The same duty that had made him pay for eggs, that had made him sit at Last Stop in the first place.

He took his car keys from the nail. The sales pouch was in the car, not on the table. That mattered because the car was the only thing of value that could move quickly at night, the only thing that could make a short debt short by making a small fare.

He put his palm on the table once, flat, as if steadying something that was not a gate.

“Mansa, if you dey hear me — I dey come back,” he said into empty, thin-walled air that eight rooms could hear.

No answer came, because answers, at that moment, lay outside the window where he had not looked, breathing very shallowly under the pawpaw tree, phone dead beside a hand that had gripped a sill with advice — *you still think I should follow him?* — still warm in the ear.

Kwame closed the door behind him — not slammed — and went to get the Corolla. The gate needed his palm again. He gave it, lift, push, quiet.

He drove the lane with headlights that made that pale wedge, picking his line, swerving when he saw the holes, missing them when he could. The compound, gate, curtain, and tree receded in his mirror and became, quickly, the same darker dark as the cemetery beyond.

He did not know that, in the morning, a cool draft would move that same curtain for a constable, that the constable would look through that window, that through it he would see what Kwame had not — Mansa, faint pulse, pawpaw tree — and that the rope barricade he would put up would make the whole town arrive in an hour.

He knew only that the room was empty, the phone was dead, and Last Stop was waiting with passengers who needed a peacemaker to drive them somewhere while the potholes found him.
