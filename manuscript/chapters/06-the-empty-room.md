# Chapter 6 — The Empty Room

*Day 0, just after midnight — minutes after 11:42 p.m. — the hill house Third from gate*

The gate needed his palm again, and he gave it without thinking, which is also a way of thinking.

Lift, push. No screech. The hinge took his weight the way it had at 4 a.m. when he left for the station, the way it had at 11:44 when he left after *good for nothing*, the way it would need to be handled any night he wanted eight doors not to open and eight women not to look. He had hoped, in the short walk from Last Stop where the barrier pole stood up and a single bulb buzzed and Saaba's light glowed seven houses away, that the house would be warm when he returned for his car — that Mansa would be at the table with the exercise book open, red cover, counting, to make him finally say the three sentences he had rehearsed without moving his lips on the dark lane: *I borrow ₵300 for Madam eggs, I be short five, I go pay tomorrow.* Three sentences. He could say three sentences to a wife, even if he had needed a wall at Last Stop to find them.

The yard was dark except for the thin, tired light from Abena's doorway, where her small transistor glowed even when off — that little red dot — and where her radio's earlier *if you no follow how you go know?* had been said and heard through concrete at 8 p.m. The pawpaw tree beyond their window was a black shape that sometimes, in wind, brushed the roofing sheet and sounded like knuckles asking to be let in. Tonight there was no wind, so the leaves were still, big as hands, fruit dark as stones. The cemetery east, beyond the compound's low wall, was just a darker dark where the iron gate's crooked post kept a cleared path and a stash point that would later take a daylight arrest with backup, but from here it was only east.

He opened their door.

The room was empty and dark.

Not the dark of someone sleeping — that dark has a shape, a breathing hill under cloth that rises and falls even when you hold your breath to hear it. The sleeping dark has a scent — oil, cloth, the faint salt of a body that has been in the day. This dark was flat, the flat dark of a room that no one is in, that has been arranged to look empty rather than left empty. The bed was made in the way Mansa made it when she was not going to sleep in it — too neat, corners tucked as if to show a point, pillow punched not slept. The table where the sales pouch had been — ₵295, or ₵295 plus ₵20 parish money he meant for Sunday — no, he had left the pouch in the car? He had taken it to Last Stop, left it on passenger seat while he sat. The table was bare except for the exercise book, closed, red cover catching no light, pen clipped, numbers not yet written for today because *how be market?* had not been answered with a number.

“Mansa?”

His voice went into the room and came back without an answer the way a man's voice comes back in a room with thin walls — everybody hears that it came back, nobody answers separately. Thin walls had made you hear your own neighbour's whispering at 8 p.m. — *you still think I should follow him?* — and now they made a room sound very empty when the person who belonged in it was not there to fill the small air between wall and curtain.

He switched the light. The bulb, economical — the kind you buy because it uses little and therefore is also slow — took a second to decide, then threw a flat, even light that the Style Guide would call B: white background, uniform outline, minimal hatching on the table's edge, storybook.

Empty.

He called her phone first before he searched the house. That was the order his mind chose when a mind has been given two impossible translations and must pick one: the nearest reach.

It rang — or did not ring — the same flat nothing he had already heard at Last Stop at 11:47, at 11:53, at his wall at midnight. Once, twice, then that recorded voice that is not a woman's: *the number you have dialed is not reachable*. Not *switched off*. Unreachable. Dead. Not *vex*. Not *punish*. Dead.

He told himself again she had switched it off. It was the easier translation, the way *Bad Luck Nkoaa* is easier than *you borrowed and not yet repaid*, the way *phone off* is easier than *phone dead from impact because window*. Phones died when you let them die — battery — not when you fell with them against a pawpaw tree.

He told himself and, telling, believed enough to act.

He searched the house the way a man searches a house that is only four spaces — kitchen, washroom, main room, compound yard — and therefore cannot hide a person well but can hide exactly one person exactly where you do not look.

Kitchen: plates from the evening's rice, washed, stacked, stew covered with a plate that had a chip at the rim. No note. She would have left a note if she had left through the door to be heard, he thought, because Mansa's notes were also accounting — *I go Abena* — small, practical.

Washroom: bucket half-full, towel damp where his own hands had left it after washing at 8 p.m. to let heat out, window open a hand, curtain not here, just concrete and a small high window that no one used to leave, only to let steam leave.

Main room: Bible still on the stool, ribbon still at Matthew 5 where he had left it that morning before checking a preacher's verse, not arguing. No bag missing he could see, no slippers out of line at the doorway — but slippers for a quiet exit would not be at the doorway, they would be at the window, and slippers at the window would be bare feet on sill.

Yard: he stepped out and looked, but not down. That sentence must be kept exact, because the policeman would underline it. He looked outward, toward the lane, toward the gate that had needed lift and had not screeched, toward the road to Last Stop as if Mansa might be walking it because she had decided to prove something by walking at midnight the way women sometimes prove things by walking where they can be seen. He called again, louder now, enough that thin walls made his loud become compound property.

“Mansa!”

Abena's doorway light shifted — someone moving inside, deciding whether to come out and be the neighbour who hears, then deciding not to, because *good for nothing* at 11:42 had been heard and hearing that makes you careful about hearing next.

He came back inside, frustrated not at Mansa — not yet — at the room for being empty in a way that did not explain itself.

The curtain moved.

It had been moving all evening in small drafts, hand's breadth open since 8 p.m., but now, with the door open behind him creating a second mouth, the draft was cooler, more definite, more outside than inside. The white curtain — hemmed by Mansa, thin enough to be called *good cloth* at the station's salon — lifted and bellied inward, showing its own light, then settled, showing cold air on the ankle that had earlier been warm. Style B's flat daylight made such curtains look storybook, careful outline on ordinary cloth; Style A's wedge would make this same curtain a pale wedge cutting black, but now it was just cool.

Kwame looked at the window.

The window was open a hand's breadth — as he had left it when he washed his hands earlier, to let heat out. Or a little more — a hand and two fingers. In the flat light it was hard to tell, and in a life where windows are for air and for watching a husband leave, not for following a husband who left without answering, *a little more* does not register as evidence until it is evidence.

The sill was low, the kind built for sitting on, the kind children in Mallam used to climb in and out of when they were small before they were told not to. The wood was worn where hands had gripped it — not from Mansa, from years, from everyone, from children, from cooking smoke making the wood smooth. The kind of sill that, during the day, you could see the drop beyond and not slip because you saw. At night, the drop found you.

He put his hand out to close it. It was the reflexive peacemaking — stop the draft — same category as lifting a gate to stop a screech.

His fingers touched sill. Cool, faintly dusty. The curtain brushed his wrist, cool.

He did not lean out. He did not put his head beyond the frame and look down.

This must be said plain, because the ledger made it the big plausibility piece and because single POV makes it not privileged but noticed:

Why would he? She had left through a door — he had heard the door at 11:42 include that small, slammed-but-not-slammed sound thin walls make slammed — and when people leave to make a point in eight rooms, they use doors so the compound will hear exactly what happened and will repeat it in the morning as *argument, she call am useless, he walk comot*. They do not use windows like thieves, like children, like a woman who had been told *you for know, follow quiet so gate no screech*. The window was for watching him leave, perhaps. For air. For curtains. For the pawpaw tree that sometimes knocked fruit on roofing at night and made you think someone threw a stone.

The pawpaw tree was just outside, leaves big as hands, fruit dark, trunk close enough that a child could use it to climb though an adult would not. At night it made small sounds on the roof that you could mistake for anything — fruit, wind, a bang ignored. It did not occur to him, in that moment, with a phone dead in his memory as off and a gate quiet in his memory as peace, to step around the outside wall — three steps — and look down at the base where a body lay in a shape the night made invisible by not lighting, where a phone with a cracked screen lay dead not because it was switched off but because it had hit the ground after a fall that was also quiet enough to be called *bang* by one unnamed witness and then ignored as *where it come from?*

He did not check outside the window.

In his account, later, in the flat room with handcuffs and a pen, he would say this plainly to a policeman who would write it down as if it were a choice, as if he had decided not to look, as if *I no check* were motive. He would say: *I search room, I search kitchen, I call phone — dead. I no check window — I think she walk comot.* And the policeman would underline *window* with the same small sound a man makes when he taps a destination sign.

Now he stepped back, not forward, pulled the curtain inward with a small, irritated tug — as if the draft were the problem and not the hand's breadth — and left the window as it was, open a hand and two fingers, curtain still moving, cold on ankle.

His phone said 12:18 a.m. The time mattered because the ledger protected 4:15 vs 4:30 and because the fall was immediately after 11:42, before his search, not during.

Yaw's voice still waited in his ear from Last Stop: *drivers stranded for Last Stop side, they need car — you dey come?* Duty. The same duty that had made him pay for a Madam's thirty eggs at seven a.m. with borrowed ₵300, that had made him sit at Last Stop at 11:52 to let quiet die down, that had made him refuse extra ₵50 at gun because he was looking for his wife, not a fare.

He took his car keys from the nail where they had hung since he had first hung them as station master twelve years ago. The nail was shiny where keys had touched it thousands of times. The sales pouch was in the car itself — ₵295, not on the table — that mattered because the car, fourteen years, washed every Sunday, was the only thing of value that could move quickly at night and that could make a short debt short by making a small fare from Last Stop to station and back before Mansa — before the empty — before —

He put his palm on the table once, flat, as if steadying something that was not a gate but needed the same lift. The red exercise book's cover dented under his palm, then sprang back.

“Mansa, if you dey hear me — I dey come back,” he said into empty, thin-walled air that eight rooms could hear and would remember as *he shout her name after empty*.

No answer came, because answers, at that moment, lay outside the window where he had not looked, breathing very shallowly under the pawpaw tree that had sometimes made roof knocks, phone dead beside a hand that had gripped a sill with advice — *you still think I should follow him? if you no follow how you go know?* — still warm in the ear and now cooling with the draft that moved the curtain a third time.

Kwame closed the door behind him — not slammed, though thin walls would call it slammed in the morning — and went to get the Corolla under the pawpaw tree whose leaves he did not look beyond. The gate needed his palm again. He gave it, lift, push, quiet, still practicing the small, practiced movement that, in a different chapter, he would once break on purpose to let it screech and show he had learned what quiet costs.

He drove the lane with headlights that made that pale wedge — a single cone cutting dark, dense hatching where it hit, flat black where it did not — picking his line, swerving when he saw the holes, missing them when he could, failing when night found him. The compound, gate, curtain with its hand-and-two-fingers, and tree receded in his mirror and became, quickly, the same darker dark as the cemetery beyond, where a crooked iron gate kept a cleared path and a stash point waiting for daylight with backup, and where an uncompleted building, distinct — not near — waited with sand and half-wall for a man who would wake at 4:15 to a wedge that had moved.

He did not know that, in the morning, a cool draft — same outside-not-inside draft, same white curtain — would move that curtain for a constable who had come with two officers to follow drivers twenty-six minutes from the station, that the constable would look through that window not because he was clever but because a draft made him, that through it he would see what Kwame had not — Mansa, faint pulse, pawpaw tree base, curtain — and that the rope barricade the constable would put up would make the whole town arrive in an hour and start two stories where one fall waited to be heard.

He knew only that the room was empty, the phone was dead, and Last Stop was waiting with passengers who, the ledger would clarify, were not passengers and with a car that would be taken seven houses to a light still warm where a daughter's phone on speaker waited to let slip a name.
