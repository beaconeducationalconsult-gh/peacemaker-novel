# Chapter 15 — The Last House

*Day 2, daytime — Nene's hut, last house*

They came at first light the way they had been told to — house to house, along the Mallam road, starting again at the uncompleted side where the sand still held yesterday's footprints, where the sachet bags had been kicked by boys who had looked quickly because notorious places make you look quickly, and where Kwame had not been when they looked because Nene's body had been wall and her hum had been shush.

Kwame heard them from inside Nene's hut, which smelled of dried leaves, small fires, and a kind of order that tin walls rarely keep but that Nene's kept — herbs tied in bunches, leaves sorted by use, mortar still holding yesterday's bitter paste for another man's pain.

He was behind the door.

That was not a plan so much as the only place left when a hut is one room and the one room is expected to be searched. The hut was one room with a window shuttered and barred from inside, a bed low, pots near the fire, stools, baskets — all given, in Style B's daylight, the same careful economical linework as people, because ordinary objects are lived-in. The door, when open, left a narrow dark behind it — ninety centimetres of flat black the wedge would not reach until it was opened too far — where a man could stand if he stood very still and did not breathe the way breathing wants to be done when ribs are on a list.

Nene had told him at 5 a.m., while she ground something that smelled bitter and that made his tooth, still mobile, answer with a small ache, “If they come, you no talk. I go talk. You hear? Tell me you hear.” She said it like an instruction for taking medicine — not loud, not to be argued, two sentences, terse as his own.

“You hear?”

He had nodded, which hurt the back of his head where sand-blood mat still held dust.

Now they came — the second day's line, not the first day's half-hearted *nothing* at the uncompleted wedge.

A young man's voice, trying for authority and landing on eagerness, the way young men do when given a rope and a list:

“Nene! Ag — Agooo — Nene, open —”

Nene's voice cut him — gentle and patient with Kwame inside, practical and unsentimental with the search team outside — exactly as the dossier had promised and exactly as the Theme Tracker said the fixed anchor should be: no arc, steady, healer firm.

“You no enter,” she said. She was outside on her packed earth, and Kwame could hear her slippers — not quiet now, placed deliberately, each step a small statement — and the soft sound of her wrapper as she crossed her arms.

“Madam, we dey search every house,” the young man said, more voices behind him agreeing low, making the line wider. “Last house. Make we check inside small. All houses we check.”

“Last house na my house,” Nene said. Even behind the door Kwame could hear the smile that was not a smile — the same not-smile she had given the search at 4:40 when slippers said *nothing* and she hummed two notes. “We don check uncompleted. You find nothing. You leave bush man for there? My house you want check like storeroom? I dey keep headache herb for you, you want search am like thief house?”

“Mammy, no be so,” another voice — older, tired, probably the constable who had manned the barrier that afternoon the Camry broke it with hollow *thwack* and who had been given a list and a rope and a hat that was not Nanfuri's. “Just open, make we see. We no spoil.”

Young men, not policemen, surrounded the house.

Kwame heard it without seeing — the soft surround that mobs do before they call themselves mobs, because *mob* is a word reports use later while the men making the ring think they are making a queue. Exits blocked by shifting feet — eight, ten, twelve — making a circle the way queue signs make a line. The hut's one window was shuttered. A hand tested its bar. Nene had barred it from inside with a stick that was also a pestle.

“You no go see nothing,” Nene said. Her voice did not rise. That was its power — she did not need to be loud to be heard over boys, because small that never needs to ask for space is heard. She did not need to be loud the way the preacher's PA needed. She was the natural PA the first chapter named.

“You go see my pots,” she went on, even, counting her inventory as if inventory were argument. “You go see my herbs for Mansa head. You go see stool. You want herb for headache? I give you. House? No. You no pay for house entry.”

A constable's voice, clipped, from further back where the pickup waited — not Nanfuri, Nanfuri was at the post where a recording from a daughter's phone waited to be heard and where Chocho would be brought to say *Musah outside* — said, “Mammy, we get order. Open. We look, we comot.”

Nene laughed, short, not mirth, the short laugh of a woman who has delivered babies and buried men and knows order is a paper.

“Order from who? For my house you need my order. Small, I be chief for my own door. I say you no enter. You try, you explain for chief for Weija. Not my chief — true chief.”

That name — *chief* — moved the circle a little, the way Chocho's name at noon the previous day moved drivers a few inches without being asked, the way *Nanfuri* would later move a cemetery search with backup. Nene was small, but the kind of small that for thirty years had pulled teeth, stopped fevers, told a boy his love potion was water and do not pay for water — accumulated authority without uniform.

A boy, bolder because the sun made him feel seen, said, “We go check by force! Last house no for hide man!” and his foot — cheap slipper, strap already worn — hit the door's lower edge, not hard, testing — the way Kofi had tested an elbow at the egg spill and found Kwame's forearm.

The door shuddered. Behind it, Kwame put his palm flat where wood met his spine and held the weight off the lower hinge the way he held his own gate at home every night — lift, push — but this time to keep it shut, not quiet. The hinge took his weight and made no screech, because some hinges, when held, keep peace, but the bar — a length of acacia — groaned, the way wood groans when it is asked to be wall.

“Touch that door again,” Nene said, and now, for the first time since 8 p.m. two days earlier when she had said *water first*, her voice had that firmness that could stand off an armed search team alone without needing to stand off anyone armed — just boys with rope and a policeman with a list. “And I go slap your face reach barrier for Last Stop and police go watch and write say you deserve am.”

Silence then, small. The kind that comes before a crowd decides whether to become a mob or a line.

Chocho was not there.

That mattered and the prose should say so, because if he had been, a nod would have decided it the other way — the way a nod moves a queue — and the ledger's red herring would have been one nod earlier. Without him, the line had only a policeman with a list and a rope, and a mammy who did not move and who, because she was fixed anchor, did not need to.

The policeman — older, tired, barrier man — said, softer, trying the word that matters to elders: “Madam, please. Open small. We no touch your things. We just — see. Please, Mami Nene.”

Nene did not answer at once. She let the silence do the work quiet does when it is chosen, not when it is absence. Then she opened.

She opened not because she was told, but because she chose the moment the way she chooses which leaf for which pain. She lifted the bar herself — two hands, not one, deliberate — stepped inside just enough to be between the doorway and the boys, so any entry would have to pass her and therefore be seen to pass an elder, and opened the door just enough that light fell in that same geometry the book had used at midnight: headlights, search wedge — a pale cone cutting too-solid black, dense cross-hatching on the hut's woven wall and on the dust where light hit, flat black in the corner where a man stood behind a door.

The boy who had kicked stepped his head in first, eager to be first to say *we catch am*.

He looked left, where the pots were, where the herbs hung in bunches tied with string that Nene reused. He looked right, where the low bed was with a cloth folded at its foot. He looked — because people, even policemen, look at doors last, because doors are supposed to be the entrance not the hiding place — at the dark behind the door where Kwame stood with his back to block and his hands not raised because raising hands would have touched wood and moved it and because breathing was still a negotiation.

The boy saw him.

His eyes did a thing Kwame had seen that morning with the okada rider — the widening where a crowd becomes a single thing quicker than any individual meant it to. The iris showed more white. The mouth opened before the word found its shape. He did not speak for half a second that felt, behind the door, like a whole noon.

Then he shouted — not a sentence, not *Station master we find you*, but a sound — a burst that had no language and therefore every language —

“HE DEY!!”

Light, which had been a wedge, became hands.

They did not pull Nene — they went around her, as if she were a post that could not be moved but could be passed, as if she were the wall she had been at 4:40. Four hands on the door edge, yanking it open, flat black gone, Kwame suddenly lit, beaten face visible — lip, eye, tooth line — to the road where more boys now shouted and ran with the specific joy of a search that had found its man at its last house and could stop searching.

“Station master! — Paa Kwame! — He hide! — For Nene house! — Make we tie am!! — Ehee!”

Nene stepped back — not away, but to be against the wall, so she could see and so they would have to look at her while they did what they were already doing, so the act would have a witness who was not the mob. Her face did what fixed anchors do when the thing they have held steady is taken: nothing dramatic, no wail, just a tightening around the eyes that said *this was expected and still it is wrong* and that the herbs still hung.

Hands tied — not with handcuffs first, with a rope someone had brought for a roof tie — rough, hemp, not flat police metal. Not tight enough to cut immediately, tight enough to be shown to the crowd that had gathered at the line's call and that needed to see *tied* to believe *found*. Kwame did not resist. His ribs would not let him, and also, dimly, he understood that struggling while tied reads as guilt the way silence while married reads as distance and the way quiet while accused reads as consent.

They brought him out.

Daylight at Nene's — Style A's darkness after a morning of Style B's flatness — was that even light the illustration guide said to render as uniform line weight, minimal sparse hatching used only for shading, no solid black fills, flat daylight with no directional shadow, storybook coloring-book care on everyday objects — pots, stools, baskets, pestle — that now lay kicked over because ordinary objects are the first to be forgotten when a man is found and because a wedge that had been dust is now a crowd.

Jubilation went up. Not cheers for justice as procedure. Cheers for ending, for the road exhaling. The search that had started at 4:30 on Day 1 with *uncompleted side, not just eye* had reached its last house on Day 2 and found its man behind its door, and the Mallam road itself, building to building, seemed to say *finish*.

Kwame stood in the circle, rope at wrists, beaten face newly lit, dust on his lip again mixed with sweat, eye half-open. Nene, behind him in her doorway, said nothing else. She had protected him overnight — *water first, you can hear?* — and had told him everything so he would not hear it piece piece from town; now protection meant not adding noise to a crowd that had waited to make noise.

He looked at her once — quick, not long, because long would have been another sentence — and saw she had not moved her herbs. They still hung. That steadiness was the only gift she could give that was not taken and that did not need to be taken to be given.

A constable — not Nanfuri, Nanfuri was not on this line; he was at the post where a recording from a daughter's phone waited to be heard and where Chocho would be brought — put a hand on Kwame's shoulder, more to steady than to arrest, fingers firm, and said the formal words for the second time in two days, but this time they had weight, because the crowd was the witness that would later testify it heard them said correctly:

“Mr. Kwame Asante, 44, driver, station master — you be under arrest for suspicion —”

The rest — *attempt, murder, rights* — was dust and hands and the walk back toward the road, building to building, last house no longer last, the gate of the cemetery east of his hill house still waiting with its crooked post and its cleared paths and its stash point for a different arrest, later, with backup, that would close the loop where a Camry's tyre track ended inside and where *Carefully* had been said.

Behind the door that had kept him a night — ninety centimetres of flat black — Nene's hut was empty again, pots, stools, baskets receiving the same outline care as people — storybook linework, quiet, observational, figures often from behind or in profile — while the road took its man and while a small pestle that had been a bar lay on the floor where it had groaned.
