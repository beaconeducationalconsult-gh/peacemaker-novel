# Chapter 15 — The Last House

*Day 2, daytime — Nene's hut*

They came at first light the way they had been told to — house to house, along the Mallam road, starting again at the uncompleted side where the sand still held yesterday's footprints and where Kwame had not been when they looked quickly.

Kwame heard them from inside Nene's hut, which smelled of dried leaves and small fires and a kind of order that tin walls rarely keep. He was behind the door. That was not a plan so much as the only place left. The hut was one room. The door, when open, left a narrow dark behind it where a man could stand if he stood very still and did not breathe the way breathing wants to be done.

Nene had told him at 5 a.m., while she ground something that smelled bitter, “If they come, you no talk. I go talk.” She said it like an instruction for taking medicine — not loud, not to be argued.

Now they came.

A young man's voice, trying for authority and landing on eagerness: “Nene! Ag — ”

Nene's voice cut him, gentle and patient with Kwame but practical and unsentimental with the search team — exactly as the dossier had promised.

“You no enter,” she said. She was outside. Kwame could hear her slippers on the packed earth, not quiet now, placed deliberately.

“Madam, we dey search every house,” the young man said. More voices behind him, low, agreeing. “Last house. Make we check inside small.”

“Last house na my house,” Nene said. Even behind the door Kwame could hear the smile that was not a smile. “We don check uncompleted. You find nothing. You leave bush man for there? My house you want check like storeroom?”

“Mammy, no be so,” another voice — older, tired, probably the policeman who had manned the barrier that afternoon the Camry broke it, now given a list and a rope. “Just open, make we see.”

Young men surrounded the house. Kwame heard it without seeing — the soft surround that mobs do before they call themselves mobs, exits blocked, eight feet shifting to make a circle. The hut's one window was shuttered. A hand tested it. Nene had barred it from inside.

“You no go see nothing,” Nene said. Her voice did not rise. That was its power — she did not need to be loud to be heard over boys. “You go see my pots. You go see my herbs. You want herb for headache? I give you. House? No.”

A constable's voice, clipped, from further back: “Mammy, we get order. Open.”

Nene laughed, short.

“Order from who? For my house you need my order. I say you no enter. You try, you explain for chief.”

That name — *chief* — moved the circle a little, the way Chocho's name moved drivers a few inches without being asked. Nene was small, but the kind of small that never needed to ask for space, and the boys knew her. She had pulled a tooth for one, stopped a child's fever for another, told a third that his love potion was not medicine but water and not to pay for water.

A boy, bolder, said, “We go check by force!” and his foot hit the door's lower edge, not hard, testing.

The door shuddered. Behind it, Kwame put his palm flat where the wood met his spine and held the weight off the lower hinge the way he held the gate at home — lift, push — but this time to keep it shut, not quiet. The hinge took his weight and made no screech, but the bar that Nene had placed groaned.

“Touch that door again,” Nene said, and now, for the first time, her voice had that firmness that could stand off an armed search team alone, “and I go slap you and police go watch.”

Silence then, small. The kind that comes before a crowd decides.

Chocho was not there. That mattered. If he had been, a nod would have decided it the other way. Without him, the line had only a policeman and a mammy who did not move.

The policeman said, tired, “Madam, please. Open small. We no touch your things. We just — ”

Nene opened.

She opened not because she was told, but because she chose the moment. She lifted the bar herself, stepped inside just enough to be between the doorway and the boys, and opened the door just enough that light fell in that same pale wedge — headlights at midnight, daylight now, same geometry: a cone cutting too-solid black, cross-hatching on the hut's woven wall where light hit, flat black in the corner where a man stood.

The boy who had kicked stepped his head in first, eager to be first.

He looked left, where the pots were, where the herbs hung. He looked right, where the bed was. He looked — because people look at doors last — at the dark behind the door where Kwame stood with his back to block and his hands not raised because raising hands would have touched wood and moved it.

The boy saw him.

His eyes did a thing Kwame had seen that morning with the okada rider — the widening where a crowd becomes a single thing. He did not speak for half a second. Then he shouted — not a sentence, a sound — “HE DEY!”

Light, which had been a wedge, became hands.

They did not pull Nene — they went around her, as if she were a post that could not be moved but could be passed. Four hands on the door, yanking it open, flat black gone, Kwame suddenly lit, beaten face visible to the road where more boys now shouted and ran.

“Station master!”

“Make we tie am!”

“Ehee! He hide for Nene house!”

Nene stepped back — not away, but to be against the wall, so she could see and so they would have to look at her while they did what they were already doing. Her face did what fixed anchors do when the thing they have held steady is taken: nothing dramatic, just a tightening around the eyes that said *this was expected and still it is wrong.*

Hands tied — not with handcuffs first, with a rope someone had brought for a roof. Not tight enough to cut, tight enough to be shown to the crowd that gathered at the line's call. Kwame did not resist. His ribs would not let him, and also, he understood dimly that struggling while tied reads as guilt the way silence while married reads as distance.

They brought him out.

Daylight at Nene's — Style A's darkness after a morning of Style B — was that flat even light that uniform line illustrations use: no beam, just white ground, careful economical linework on ordinary objects — pots, stools, baskets — that now lay kicked over because ordinary objects are the first to be forgotten when a man is found.

Jubilation went up. Not cheers for justice. Cheers for ending. The search that had started at 4:30 yesterday with *uncompleted side* had reached its last house and found its man, and the road itself seemed to exhale.

Kwame stood in the circle, rope at wrists, beaten face newly lit, dust on his lip again. Nene, behind him in her doorway, said nothing else. She had protected him overnight; now protection meant not adding noise. He looked at her once — quick — and saw she had not moved her herbs. They still hung. That steadiness was the only gift she could give that was not taken.

A constable, not Nanfuri — Nanfuri was not on this line; he was at the post where a recording from a daughter's phone waited to be heard — put a hand on Kwame's shoulder, more to steady than to arrest, and said the formal words for the second time in two days, but this time they had weight, because the crowd was the witness.

“Mr. Kwame Asante, you be under arrest —”

The rest was dust and hands and the walk back toward the road, building to building, last house no longer last, the gate of the cemetery east of his hill house still waiting with its crooked post and its cleared paths for a different arrest with backup, later, that would close the loop where a Camry's tyre track ended inside.

Behind the door that had kept him a night, Nene's hut was empty again, pots, stools, baskets receiving the same outline care as people — storybook linework, quiet, observational — while the road took its man.
