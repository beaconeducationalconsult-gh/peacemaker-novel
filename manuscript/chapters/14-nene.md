# Chapter 14 — Nene

*Day 1, evening — the uncompleted building, then her hut*

She did not move him until the calling line had passed.

Nene crouched. Up close she smelled of leaves that had been crushed for other people's pains — bitter, green — and of woodsmoke. She was small, but the kind of small that never needed to ask for space.

“Water first,” she said, practical.

She had a bottle — not sachet, bottle — with a cap that fit tightly. She opened it for him, held it steady because his right hand was not steady. He drank. Pain made him careful, not slow — a difference. Blood and dust and the taste of the building moved, then settled. She gave him bread from a cloth — dry, unsweet — and watched him chew one bite, swallow, breathe, before she let him have another.

“You can hear?” she asked, gentle. Not *can you speak* — that came later — but *can you hear*, as if hearing were the more urgent medical function.

Kwame nodded. The nod hurt the back of his head. He did it again, smaller.

She put her hand, briefly, on his forearm — not where Kofi's elbow had hit that morning, not where the boot had hit at Last Stop, but on the wrist that had taken the weight of the gate for years. A small, checking touch.

“Then listen once,” she said, still unsentimental in the way healers are when they have bad news to give and no other way to give it except plain. “I go tell you everything at once so you no go hear am piece piece from town. You hear? Tell me you hear.”

He heard.

She told him.

• • •

*TRAGEDY — what they found at his house.*

“It was morning,” Nene said. She spoke as if counting herbs, even. “Kasoa FM say at six, daughter of police man — fine new house for Last Stop, seven houses away — robbers enter night. Kill am. Saaba. Chief Inspector Nanfuri pikin.”

Kwame, who had seen that house's light at six p.m. warm and ordinary, who had seen the Camry pass it without thinking, made a small sound that was not a word. Saaba. The name the barber had used at midday: *fine girl, father be police man, big one.* That house. The newest, freshest build.

“She dey house night, dey phone with father,” Nene went on, and her voice, for the first time, softened a fraction. “Ordinary call. ‘Daddy, you lock?’ ‘I dey come.’ That kind talk. Robbers break. Her phone dey record. Father hear something start for other side, then line cut. Police go there morning, find am — and the recording.”

Kwame stared. He was on a floor in a half-wall's shadow, blood on his lip, and a girl he had seen once buying airtime was now a name that had been on a call with her father when men broke a window seven houses from where he himself had been beaten.

“She —” he tried. The tooth that moved made the word careful.

“Eehh,” Nene said, understanding the unfinished question without needing it finished. “Nobody see. Nobody see because robbers use car.”

She looked at him then. The look was not accusing. It was measuring.

“Your car, Kwame.”

His car. Fourteen years, washed every Sunday, wire on the handle he had fixed that morning. Taken at Last Stop where he had refused to carry.

“They say numbers — car see for that seven-house house. People know say na your car. So story make.”

*Story make.* It was exactly how she would put it.

*WHERE WAS MANSA? — what they found at the pawpaw tree.*

“They bring CID for station,” Nene said. No hurry. No leaving gaps for him to fill and get wrong. “Two officers. They follow drivers come your house. Twenty-six minutes. Abena dey there. They ask her.”

Abena. *Nkonkonsa*. Neighbor who had said *you still think I should follow him?* and whose radio had clicked off when Mansa said *good for nothing*. Kwame would not yet know that advice sat underneath this next part.

“Police see curtain,” Nene said. “White curtain blow with draft. Through window—” She made a small gesture with her hand, the same hand she had used to brush leaves, miming a curtain being moved by cold air from outside, not inside. “Officer look. Through window see am.”

Kwame's throat made a shape.

“Your wife. For pawpaw tree base. Fall. Not on bed. For outside.”

He did not speak. He could see the curtain — hand's breadth open — where he had put his fingers, where he had tugged it inward irritatedly at the draft, where he had not leaned out, where he had not looked down.

“Alive,” Nene said quickly, because she saw his face. “Pulse small, but dey. They do rope. Ambulance carry am. Whole compound talk say argument last night — she call you useless, whole house hear — so people start talk.”

• • •

*THE STORY MALLAM TOWN TELLS — what the town says now.*

Nene shifted, because this part was longer and needed sitting on a block, not crouching. She eased him so his back was not on half-wall but against full wall, where he could see the doorway wedge and still hear her without turning.

“By midday, two stories waka,” she said, almost weary with how predictable people were. “Kiosk, salon, barber, station. Every mouth talk.”

She held up one finger, chipped nail.

“Story one: You push am because of other woman. You get other woman for that seven-house new house, or for station, and Mansa find out, and you push am for window.”

Finger two.

“Story two: You and Mansa fight for money, you no hit but she push you to show you be man and she fall. Or she try to shame you small and e fall pass. Same, but different face.”

She watched him.

“Both say: Kwame run, so Kwame guilty. Because you no dey. Phone off, car no dey, house empty. If you no do bad, why you run?”

Kwame closed his eye that could close fully. He had not run. He had walked to Last Stop to let quiet do work, sat where the barrier had been broken, been told *you still think I should follow him?* without knowing it, been asked to carry, refused even at gunpoint for his wife's sake, been dragged, dumped, and now lay where searchers called *notorious* and thought it proved something.

“I no —” he started. The rest — *push* — would not come.

“I know,” Nene said. Simple. Not because she knew facts — she did not have facts until tomorrow when a doctor would say *fall, not push, angle wrong* — but because she had doubted from the start the way a fixed anchor doubts: by not moving when the line moves.

“Search start 4:30,” she went on, finishing the day for him. “Police bring Chocho people to help. Some say Chocho be land guard so search be suspect. I tell them no. Chocho watch new houses — but this car take from you go that seven-house house — not his people. Later they go bring him to hear recording background voice and he go say *outside group, not Mallam.* But that be later. Today, they just waka house to house. They pause for night — no lights, poor eye — postpone to tomorrow. That be why I fit bring you.”

She stood. She was done. She had given him — as she had to, as Nene narrating to Kwame was literally already how his own draft handled what a single-POV man cannot witness — three chapters at once: Saaba on a call with her father, Mansa under the pawpaw tree, the town speaking over his silence.

He had heard it all without moving his body, which hurt less that way. His tongue found the moved tooth again.

“Saaba —” he said again, because of the girl, because of the father who had said *Carefully* at the station and withdrawn at a cemetery gate rather than go alone against men who shot tyres, and who later would hear his daughter's last call on a recording that was also ordinary.

Nene nodded once. She understood which loss was not his but had made his loss larger.

“You go wake, they go think you kill two,” she said, naming the two charges without using the word *charge*. “One for window fall, one for seven-house house. But fall be fall, not push — and car be stolen, not lent. You hear?”

He heard.

She bent and, practical again, took his cracked phone from his chest where the tall man had placed it.

“I go charge,” she said. “Make we no talk this for here. Tomorrow they go search last house — my own. They go surround. I go stand. Tonight we go my hut small small for darkness.”

She helped him up. It took a long time. He was forty-four, not old, but beaten bodies pretend to be old. She put his arm over her shoulder — she was small, but firm enough to stand off a search team alone — and guided him deeper through the bush path she knew from gathering, away from the doorway wedge where slippers had paused at 4:40 and said *nothing*, toward the hut that would be last.

He walked limping, leaning, dust settling on his lip again. In her hand, his dead phone.

Behind them, the uncompleted building kept its half-wall and its flat black and its sachets as if no one had been there. East of his hill house, the cemetery kept its cleared paths and its stash point behind a crooked post, waiting for backup and for a daylight arrest.

And in Mansa's room, the curtain — where a constable's draft had found a body — still moved, though the body was now gone, waiting for a wake that would come at Kwame's breaking point, near arraignment, when confession would matter more than gossip.
