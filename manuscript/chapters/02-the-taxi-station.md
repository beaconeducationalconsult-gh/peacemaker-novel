# Chapter 2 — The Taxi Station

*Day 0, 10:00 a.m. – 2:00 p.m.*

By 10:30 the station existed in its other form.

If you only knew Mallam at six to ten, you would think it was a market that sold noise. If you knew it at eleven, you knew it was also a waiting room. The queues thinned to a handful of patient traders, nursing mothers adjusting cloths over shoulders, schoolboys with backpacks bigger than their backs. The seven hand-painted destination signs leaned at tired angles. Drivers sat on their bumpers or on low stools, phones bright in their palms, not because they were needed but because mid-morning was when you charged them.

Kwame sat on the bench behind his own car — a Toyota Corolla, fourteen years old, washed every Sunday whether it needed it or not — and counted.

He did it without taking the money out. His sales bag was a small leather pouch with a zip that caught if you pulled too fast. Inside was what four hours had made: ₵180.

He knew, without unfolding, what each note was. Two fifties from the early Adentan runs, a twenty, a folded fifty that a woman had given him for Kasoa when she only had fifty and told him to keep the change, eehh — which he had — and a thin stack of tens and coins for change that felt more like weight than money.

He needed ₵300 by nightfall. Not to eat. To repay.

Ebo's ₵150 and Kpakpo's ₵150 lay heavier in his memory than the ₵180 lay in his hand. The Madam had walked away with the eggs' price, yolk still on her wrapper, blessing him twice. The two drivers had not asked when. That made it worse. In the station, a debt you named was a transaction. A debt you did not name was a trust.

Yaw dropped onto the bench beside him with a sigh that came from his knees.

“Station master, you still counting bad-luck money?”

Kwame zipped the pouch, unzipped it, zipped it again. The zip caught.

“Money be money,” he said.

Yaw grinned. Yaw was twenty-six and drove as if the road were a personal argument he intended to win. He had been the first to laugh after the egg incident, and the first to say, too loud, that Station master had paid for a boy who would have paid him nothing in return.

“Bad Luck *Nkoaa*,” Yaw said now, testing the phrase that had started somewhere between the kiosk and the barber's chair after ten. “That is what they are saying. Morning spoil because of eggs. So afternoon nobody dey travel. You see? The eggs were juju.”

Kwame did not answer. He was listening to the transistor still on the barber's stool. The Kasoa FM presenter had moved on from market prices to something else — land.

“…reminding all who have completed or are completing new buildings to visit the lands commission, especially in the new stretches toward Last Stop. We have had reports of land guard activity, disturbances — you know how it is. Protect your documents, protect your house…”

The presenter added that little thrill again to *disturbances*, making two syllables last three.

Chocho walked past while the radio said it.

Kwame did not see him arrive. No one ever quite did. He was simply there, on the edge of the station where the tar ended and the packed earth began toward Last Stop, with two boys behind him who did not need to be told to stand a little back.

Chocho was not large. That was the first thing people got wrong when they described him later. He was not tall, not muscled in the way drivers admired. He wore a clean white Lacoste whose collar sat flat, dark trousers, slippers that made no sound in dust. His hair was cut low and exact. He looked, at a distance, like a senior driver who had retired early and kept his car clean.

At a closer distance, you understood why drivers moved their cars a few inches when he passed, even though he had asked for nothing.

“Station master,” Chocho said. Not loud.

Kwame stood. It was a small, automatic respect. Not fear — habit from dealing with any man who could settle a quarrel with a word and enforce it with ten more men you would not see.

“Chocho.”

“How be station?” Chocho asked. He looked at the queues, the empty spaces, the drivers on their phones. His eyes stopped on Kwame's car, whose door handle Kwame had fixed with wire that morning before dawn.

“Slow,” Kwame said.

“Morning too much dust,” Chocho said, and smiled a little, as if they were agreeing about weather. “Boys say nobody want enter car when eggs break. Say spiritual.”

Yaw, who had straightened when Chocho approached, laughed too quickly. “Na lie! But — small slow, yes.”

Chocho nodded once. He did not look at Yaw. His attention was on Kwame, and it was transactional in a way that was not unfriendly, just precise.

“You get new houses for this side,” Chocho said, flicking his chin toward Last Stop, toward the short stretch of road where, seven houses down, the freshest building in the community sat with paint still too bright and a low wall still unrendered. Kwame knew it without counting. Everyone knew it. Saaba's house, though Kwame only knew it as the fine one the mason's boys had finished last month, with glass windows that caught the sun.

“Fresh paint,” Kwame said.

“Fresh paper,” Chocho said, meaning documents, meaning registration. He said it the way a man says *water* when he knows there is not enough. “Some people build, no pay land cost. Some pay one family, another family come say na their own. You know.”

Kwame knew enough. In Mallam Town land was not land until a paper said it was land, and papers multiplied like gossip. The story was always the same: a man built quickly, nicely, on a plot bought from an uncle who had bought it from an uncle. Then a boy came with a shovel at midnight and said his master owned it, and the man paid, or fought, or called a policeman who knew the boy's master and advised settlement. Chocho's name lived in those settlements. Not always in the house itself — sometimes just in the phone call that stopped the shovel.

Whether Chocho protected or collected was a question people answered differently depending on whether they had built recently.

“You dey watch that side?” Kwame asked. He meant, are you watching Last Stop.

Chocho's smile did not move.

“We dey watch peace,” he said. “Same like you.”

That was the whole conversation. Chocho tapped his slippers once in the dust — a small, unnecessary gesture, as if brushing something off — and continued past the queues toward the kiosks where, by midday, the town's second nervous system operated.

Kwame sat again. Yaw exhaled.

“Charlie, that man,” Yaw whispered. “Even his slippers quiet.”

Kwame said nothing. He was doing math that did not need a calculator. ₵180 in the pouch. ₵300 owed. Evening rush from three to five could, on a good day, make ₵250 if he stayed late and took the last Circle run that no one wanted because returning empty wasted fuel. On a bad-luck day, ₵80.

Mansa would ask tonight. She always asked, not because she did not trust him, but because she kept the books for the compound's rent, for the children's school things for her sister's children who sometimes stayed with them, for the future they described as “small small.” She asked the way a nurse checks a pulse — not accusing, just confirming the heart still beat.

He had not told her about the ₵300. It was not a lie yet. It was a silence. And silence, in his experience, let things cool and die down. If he told her at 8 p.m. when he arrived home already tired, she would count it as a loss. If he told her after he had repaid it, it would be a story about a boy on an okada and not about money at all.

That was how he reasoned it. It felt, at noon, like peace.

At the barber's kiosk, the gossip had already moved past eggs.

Kwame could hear it without meaning to — thin walls had trained him to hear through things. The barber, a man called Sule who cut hair as if negotiating with it, was holding his clippers still while he spoke. Two women waited under dryers that were not on, their hair wrapped, their ears open.

“Have you seen that seven-house house?” one woman said — Kwame recognized the voice of the seamstress who made Mansa's church dresses. “The one near Last Stop. Glass windows, eehh. The girl there, she fine. Her father is police man. Big one.”

“Which police?”

“Chief Inspector. That tall one who come station before. Nanfuri.”

“Aahh. Him. He be strict. That house — fresh building, fresh girl. That kind house, land guard go see quick.”

Sule the barber resumed clipping. “You talk true. New house, no wall finish, no paper — na invitation.”

Kwame felt a small, unimportant prick of interest and let it go. New houses were for other people's arguments. His station was old houses and old quarrels. Moreover, the girl — Saaba — Kwame had never spoken to. He had seen her once buying airtime at the kiosk, quick, polite, the way children of strict fathers are. She was not his.

He checked his phone — 11:47 a.m. — and saw nothing from Mansa. That was normal. She would not message until she needed something.

He stood to stretch, and Kpakpo came over with a plastic bag of water and two boiled eggs he had peeled with his thumbs.

“Chop,” Kpakpo said. It was not a question.

Kwame took one egg. Kpakpo took the other, sprinkled salt from a tiny folded paper. They ate standing up, looking at the road, not at each other. Ebo joined them without being called. That was part of the station too — the ad hoc council that formed around a small need and dissolved when it was met.

“We go do evening,” Ebo said, chewing. “You go drive Circle, I go drive Kasoa. Make we clear your bad luck.”

Kwame swallowed. “No need.”

“Need there,” Ebo said, and looked at him steadily. “You stand for boy, we stand for you. That be station.”

Kwame wanted to say *thank you* and could not find a larger way to say it without making it noise. He nodded once, the same small wai with his whole head that he had used with the Madam.

At 12:30 the sun was directly above the station, flattening shadows to nothing, making the dust visible only when a car moved. The preacher's speaker still leaned against the bicycle where he had left it, its cord coiled. The transistor had been turned down so the barber could argue.

Kwame walked to the edge where the tar met the packed earth and looked toward Last Stop. The barrier pole was up. The policeman was now awake and fanning himself with a notebook. Beyond him, seven houses down, Saaba's new house caught the sun so hard its white walls hurt the eyes. A dark tinted Camry — not the one from later, just a taxi driver's cousin's car — pulled away from its front and turned toward the junction, normal, ordinary.

Everything dangerous that afternoon was still on the other side of ordinary. It had not yet chosen its vehicle. It had not yet reached for a phone to call a father.

Kwame turned back to the station, because the station was his and it was, for two more hours, safe. The potholes were visible. He could see them. He could swerve.

He zipped his sales pouch again. ₵180. He would make it ₵300 if the evening was kind, if the road was kind, if the town kept talking about other people's new houses and not about him.

At 1:15 p.m., he moved his car forward one length to cover Kpakpo's spot while Kpakpo went to piss behind the kiosk. It was a small, automatic peacemaking — keeping the queue tight, keeping order without a word.

The day held. That was the danger.
