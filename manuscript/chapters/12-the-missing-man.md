# Chapter 12 — The Missing Man

*Day 1, ~4:15 p.m. — the uncompleted building*

He woke to dust.

Not the dust of the station at six a.m., which was busy and low and human, but the other dust — that which lives in uncompleted buildings where sachet bags gather and no one sweeps. It had a taste. It had settled on his lip, on the blood that had dried there and cracked when he breathed.

Kwame opened one eye.

The other did not open where he expected. He tried his hands. The left answered. The right answered later, as if it had to be called twice.

He was behind a half-wall. That explained the shadow that cut his face. White linework on black, dense where foliage should be — but there was no foliage here, just block and rebar, a single wedge of daylight coming through the doorway that had no door, making the floor's sand visible grain by grain. Outside that wedge, flat black. Style A's noir was not dramatic here; it was simply what an uncompleted building looks like at four in the afternoon when you are on its floor.

He tried to remember. Last Stop. Three people. *You go Lapaz or no?* The hand reaching past his chest to kill the ignition, wire snapping, gravel on his cheek at the junction where he had earlier counted ₵285 and later ₵295 — the same junction where at four p.m. a Camry had broken a barrier and where a commander had said *Carefully*. The car — his Corolla, fourteen years — starting on the second try without him.

He tried to sit.

The building told him not to.

Pain came not as one thing but as a list, precise as a queue at the station: ribs on the left, a tooth that moved when he touched it with his tongue, the forearm where Kofi's elbow had hit him yesterday morning and where a boot had hit him last night — same place, layered — the back of his head where sand had stuck to blood.

He lay back down. The pouch was gone, of course. His phone was on his chest where the tall man had placed it, screen cracked in a spider that caught light. Dead. Not off. Dead — the same flat nothing Mansa's phone had given him at 11:47 p.m., at 12:18 a.m., at last Stop at midnight. He understood that nothing now in a way he had not before: a phone could die from impact, not choice.

*Impact.* The word brought the window back — white curtain, hand's breadth, cold on ankle, pawpaw leaves — and with it, Mansa's empty room. He had told that room *I dey come back* into thin-walled air that eight rooms could hear. The room had not answered because answers were outside the window where he had not looked.

How long?

Light said afternoon. Shadow said long. He had been on this floor overnight, through Day 1 morning when Kasoa FM would have read Saaba's name and the station would have sent two officers to his hill house — a house that was twenty-six minutes from the station and seven houses from Saaba's, a house whose curtain would move for a constable at mid-morning.

He did not know any of that yet. He knew only what his body knew: overnight until ~4:15 p.m.

He turned his head, small.

Outside the doorway wedge, bush moved. Not wind — something gathering. He had heard this at 4:30 yesterday described, later, as a manhunt building to building along the Mallam road, starting with uncompleted buildings, police and Chocho's men. That description would reach him through Nene. Now, on his floor, he heard only a distant voice calling, too far to be about him, and a response, and a man's laugh that was not cruel, just working.

He tried again to sit. This time his left arm helped.

He got to one elbow, then to sitting with his back against the half-wall. The world narrowed to that white wedge and the black beyond, to the taste of dust and old blood, to a fly that found his lip and left, to the cracked phone that said nothing, to the knowledge that he was missing and that missing, combined with an argument the compound had heard, reads as flight.

The station would have told the officers. The officers would have found the window. Mansa would be —

He could not finish that. He was Kwame Asante, forty-four, station master, elder who checked verses silently rather than arguing, a man who kept quiet long enough for noise to die. Silence-as-peace had been his way of loving a wife who needed answers. Now the town's noise would fill the vacuum he had left by being absent, and it would get it wrong.

He breathed, small, ribs protesting.

The uncompleted building kept him as quietly as he had kept the gate.

Outside, the search was starting.
