# Chapter 12 — The Missing Man

*Day 1, ~4:15 p.m. — the uncompleted building*

He woke to dust.

Not the dust of the station at six a.m., which was busy and low and human, kicked up by foot and tyre and then settled on sachet bags and on the preacher's speaker cord until someone flicked water. This dust was the other kind — the dust that lives in uncompleted buildings where no one sweeps, where cement has been half-mixed and left to drink the air, where the floor is sand and the sand is mixed with broken sachet water bags, cigarette foils, and the small, waxy leaves that fall from bush no one prunes. It had a taste. It had settled on his lip, on the blood that had dried there and cracked when he breathed, on the eyelash of the eye that would not open properly.

Kwame opened one eye.

The other did not open where he expected. He tried to lift the brow and felt a pulling, as if skin had been glued and then glued again badly. His mouth opened before his eye did. A small, dry click at the hinge of the jaw.

He tried his hands.

The left answered. He could see it, dusty, knuckles scraped where gravel had taken them at Last Stop — same junction where at four p.m. a Camry broke the barrier pole and where at seven a.m. he had taken an elbow for a boy. The right answered later, as if it had to be called twice and then came embarrassed. He flexed it. Pain answered, neat.

He was behind a half-wall.

That explained the shadow that cut his face. White linework on black, dense where foliage should be — but there was no foliage here, just block and rebar, three courses high, unplastered, the iron fingers of the rebar rusted and bent where the builders had stopped and not returned. A single wedge of daylight came through the doorway that had no door, making the floor's sand visible grain by grain, each grain a short parallel hatch mark in the storybook light, while outside that wedge, flat black. Style A's noir was not dramatic here; it was simply what an uncompleted building looks like at four in the afternoon when you are on its floor and the sun has become a cone rather than a wash. The building's flat black sky had no stars. Its light was that pale wedge, moonlit or flashlight, now sun at a low angle, cutting through darkness it had made for itself.

He tried to remember by moving his tongue.

The tooth. It moved when he touched it, a small sideways give that made him stop touching it. He tasted salt and a coin's metallic lie.

Last Stop. Three people standing where the tar thinned to gravel at the bush edge nearest the station — two men and a woman with a small bag. *You go Lapaz or no?* The woman's almost apologetic *We go pay extra, add ₵50*. His own flat: *I dey look for my wife. I no dey go.* Second refusal, even when the extra would have made his pouch ₵345 and therefore made the three sentences about ₵300 affordable. The short, thick leader's hand to waist, the small black flat thing that swallowed light. The hand reaching past his chest — across the same chest where, twelve years, an elder's Bible had rested at Matthew 5 — to kill the ignition. The wire snapping that had fixed his Corolla door that morning. Gravel on cheek at the junction where he had earlier counted ₵285 that afternoon and later ₵295 before walking to the same junction at 11:47 to let quiet do the work of speech.

The car — fourteen years, washed every Sunday whether it needed it or not — starting on the second try without him. Turning not toward Lapaz but, seven houses along the same road, toward the newest house with glass windows catching sun. Saaba's house, though he only knew it as the fine one.

He tried to sit.

The building told him not to.

Pain came not as one thing but as a list, precise as a queue at the station where seven destination signs make seven different waits. Ribs on the left where the leader's foot had landed, not hard enough to be called stabbing, hard enough to be called *breathing is now a negotiation*. The forearm where Kofi's elbow had hit him yesterday morning at the egg spill — same spot, layered, now a boot — the body remembering locations the way roads remember potholes: the same hole found you twice. The back of his head where sand had stuck to blood and blood to sand in a small, gritty mat. The knee, not cut but scraped through trousers to the skin that knew the trousers were torn.

He lay back down. The relief was immediate and humiliating.

The pouch was gone, of course. He had known it would be without needing to look. Sales pouch on the passenger seat, ₵295 plus the ₵20 parish offering he had folded for Sunday — not much, but his — now just money for men who thought houses without papers were houses without owners. His phone was on his chest where the tall man had placed it after looking, cracked in a spider that caught light and also made light fail, screen lit once then not. He lifted it with the left hand, thumb smearing dust across the crack.

Dead. Not off.

The same flat nothing Mansa's phone had given him at 11:47 p.m. at the wall, at 12:18 a.m. at the empty room, at Last Stop at midnight. Not switched off to punish. Not unreachable because she was angry. Dead — the way a thing dies when it hits the ground with the hand that holds it.

*Impact.*

The word brought the window back — white curtain, hand's breadth, cold on ankle, pawpaw leaves big as hands — and with it, Mansa's empty room, the kitchen plates washed and stacked, the Bible on the stool, his own voice saying *Mansa, if you dey hear me — I dey come back* into thin-walled air that eight rooms could hear. The room had not answered because answers were outside the window where he had not looked, breathing very shallowly under the pawpaw tree, phone dead beside a hand that had gripped a sill low, worn where hands grip, on advice that had been given through a wall.

How long?

Light said afternoon. Shadow said long. The wedge had moved since he first saw it — at first it hit sand, now it hit block, which meant the sun had moved while he was not moving. He had been on this floor overnight, through Day 1 morning when Kasoa FM would have read, with that emotional voice that added thrill to *disturbances*, a name — Saaba — and the station would have sent two officers to his hill house — twenty-six minutes from the station and seven houses from Saaba's, a house whose curtain would move for a constable at mid-morning because a draft moved it for Kwame at 12:18. He did not know any of that yet the way facts are known. He knew it the way bodies know time: by thirst, by bladder, by the particular way blood dries in a line from nostril to lip and then stops.

He knew only what his body knew: overnight until ~4:15 p.m. — the ledger's margin that had to be protected, not widened or narrowed in later drafts.

He turned his head, small. Movement made sand speak.

Outside the doorway wedge, bush moved. Not wind — something gathering, making the dust that is busy dust begin to rise where it had been other dust. He had heard this described, later, as a manhunt building to building along the Mallam road, starting with uncompleted buildings — the notorious sites, bush-surrounded — police and Chocho's men interspersed, Chocho not shouting but nodding a line correct. That description would reach him through Nene. Now, on his floor, he heard only the beginning: a distant whistle — not police, a boy's — then a man's voice, then another, overlapping until the road's line became a calling line, and a response, and a man's laugh that was not cruel, just working, the laugh of men doing a job they had been given.

“Make we start for uncompleted side! — That one done! — Check that one!”

He pressed his mouth shut. He did not know whether they looked properly — the boy at 4:40 who would say *nothing* after a quick lean — or whether, at night, they would pause for poor visibility and postpone to next day. That pause, in the ledger, was the mercy that let Nene guide him to her hut.

He tried again to sit.

This time his left arm helped.

He got to one elbow. The wedge made his forearm's hairs visible. Dust on them, blood under a nail, the small scar from a station cut ten years ago. He got to sitting with his back against the half-wall, rebar at his shoulder like fingers not yet finished. The world narrowed to that white wedge and the black beyond, to the taste of dust and old blood, to a fly that found his lip and left, finding better somewhere, to the cracked phone that said nothing on his chest, to the knowledge that he was missing and that missing, combined with an argument the compound had heard at 11:42 — *good for nothing* — reads as flight.

The station would have told the officers. The officers would have found the window. The curtain would have moved for them. Through it they would have seen — what he had not — Mansa, faint pulse, pawpaw tree, rope barricade. Mansa would be — he could not finish that with the tooth that moved. He was Kwame Asante, forty-four, station master, elder who checked verses silently rather than arguing, a man who had kept quiet long enough for noise to die and had learned that silence can be distance. Silence-as-peace had been his way of loving a wife who needed answers. Now the town's second nervous system — kiosks, barbering salons, hairdressing salons, apketeshie bars, food joints — would fill the vacuum he had left by being absent, and it would get it wrong in two competed stories: *he pushed her over another woman* and *she pushed him and it backfired*. Both read flight as guilt.

He breathed, small, ribs protesting, and let the building keep him as quietly as he had kept the gate every night with a palm lift, no screech. Outside, the calling line came closer by one house, then by another.

The search was starting, building to building, last house being Nene's near the bush where herbs grew best.

He did not yet know the woman who would soon hum while gathering — fixed anchor, no arc — would put a bottle to his lips and say *water first* and then *you can hear?* and then tell him everything at once so he would not hear it piece piece from town.

He knew only that the light had moved, the phone was dead not off, the window was open a hand where he had not leaned, and the road out at night finds you even when you are not on it.
