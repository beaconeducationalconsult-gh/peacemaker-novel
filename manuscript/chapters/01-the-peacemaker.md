# Chapter 1 — The Peacemaker

*Day 0, 4:00 a.m. – 10:00 a.m.*

The roosters started before the muezzin, before the first trotro horn, before Kwame's alarm.

That was how you knew they were serious. Nature's most reliable PA system, the same every morning — no fundraising appeal at the end, no battery to die halfway through a verse.

Kwame Asante lay still in the dark and listened to them go. One near the cemetery fence, one behind the row of eight single-room self-contained apartments that made up their compound on the hill, one further down toward the low houses where the road flattened. They did not harmonize. They competed.

Peace, in Kwame's life, meant keeping quiet long enough for other people's noise to die down.

He had been practicing it for forty-four years.

Mansa breathed beside him, steady and turned away. The compound was still thin-walled enough that you could hear Abena's small radio when she left it on overnight, and tonight she had. Kasoa FM murmured somewhere below, too low to catch words, just that voice the presenter used — the one the drivers mocked affectionately for adding emotions to text so well. Even at this hour, someone was awake and being told how to feel about the news.

Kwame sat up without switching the light. He did not need it. He knew where his folded shirt was, where his belt hung from the nail, where the Bible rested on the stool with the ribbon holding Matthew 5. Elder at the Church of Christ did not mean he was the oldest — he was not — but it meant that on Sundays they asked him to pray where people could hear him, and that his congregation had voted him station master twelve years ago for the same reason. He didn't shout. People listened anyway.

At the gate he did what he did every morning. He put his palm flat against the metal and lifted slightly as he pushed, taking the weight off the lower hinge so it would not screech. It was a small, practiced movement. If he did it right, the compound kept sleeping. If he did it wrong, eight doors would open, eight women would look out, and by six a.m. the whole hill would know what time Kwame Asante left his house and whether his wife had waved him off at the door.

This morning he did it right. The gate shivered but made no sound. He stood on the lane for a second with his hand still on the warm metal, listening to the quiet he had kept.

During the day, you could see the potholes coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you.

He said it sometimes to the junior drivers when they complained about night driving — not as a warning, really, as a fact. In daylight the road out of Mallam Town was manageable if you paid attention. You picked your line early, you let the left tyre kiss the good tar, you missed the hole that had been there since the rains. At night, same road, same holes, but you drove by memory and hope, and the hole chose you.

He had been saying it for years without thinking it meant anything more than what it said.

• • •

Mallam Taxi Station at 5:20 a.m. was still a promise, not yet a place.

Kwame's car was cold. He drove the twenty-six minutes down the hill with his headlights cutting the dark in that pale wedge that made the dust hang visible. No one flagged him. At that hour the road belonged to goats and to the okada boys who rode without helmets and without lights, as if darkness were a suggestion.

By 5:45 the station was awake in layers.

First the cleaners, flicking water from buckets to keep the dust down, though the dust never listened. Then the food women, setting coal pots, yams hissing. Then the drivers, arriving in threes and fours, some pushing their doors open with their shoulders because the handles were broken, all of them finding their stations.

Seven destination queues by 6 a.m. — Kwame counted without meaning to. Kasoa, Lapaz, Circle, Madina, Ashaiman, Tema Station, Adentan. The signs were hand-painted and propped on sticks. Passengers learned to stand behind the one they needed and wait. If they stood in the wrong place, someone would correct them, not unkindly.

Kwame did not need to shout. He never had. Two of the senior drivers — Ebo, who had been driving since before Kwame learned to shave, and small, sharp-eyed Kpakpo — kept the order with him. That was the informal part people who did not use the station never understood. Station master was not a uniform. It was a vote, and after twelve years, it was a habit of deference. When there was a dispute about who had arrived first, about a driver trying to jump queue, about a passenger who refused to pay the station's small loading fee, they looked to Kwame. He listened, nodded, and gave a short answer. The answer usually held.

By 6:30 the station buzzed the way it always did between six and ten — a particular noise that was not one noise at all but many competing ones deciding to be loud at the same time. Horns short and long. Conductors calling destinations with the ends stretched. A preacher had set up at the edge near the junction with a PA system that crackled every time he said *holy*. He was new. Kwame had not seen him before.

“Brothers and sisters! The road is narrow! The road is narrow eehh!”

The driver beside Kwame, Yaw, rolled his eyes with affection.

“Every road is narrow before they do fundraising,” Yaw murmured.

Kwame did not answer at first. He listened to check the verse.

Matthew 7:13. The preacher was close enough. Kwame gave him that. He always checked the verses silently rather than arguing. Arguing was another kind of noise.

“These preachers,” Yaw said, louder, hoping Kwame would join him. “They will take your ₵20 and tell you God kept you from potholes.”

“They are hot,” Kwame said, which was not an argument. It was a terse, observed fact. It was also as far as he usually went.

Yaw laughed, satisfied. That was the thing about Kwame — if he said two sentences before nine a.m., you had done well.

By 7:00 a.m. the station was full chaos — dust up to the knees when a car pulled out too fast, the smell of exhaust and fried plantain, the preachers going hoarse, the queues bending and re-forming as people pushed with bags on their heads. Kwame stood near the junction end, where the police post and barrier sat at what everyone called Last Stop. The barrier was a striped pole on a hinge, not much to look at, and the policeman manning it at that hour was half-asleep at his post. This was Last Stop because it was where the town ended and the tarred road thinned, and also where Kwame often stood at night when he needed to think and not be heard. He did not know, that morning, that he would be sitting at that exact spot again after midnight, unable to sleep, because of an argument that had not yet happened.

At 7:18, the egg seller crossed.

She was a Madam in her fifties, broad and careful, carrying a broad flat crate of eggs on her head with a cloth ring underneath, another crate balanced against her hip. She had been crossing the road in front of the station at this hour for as long as Kwame could remember. Everyone knew to wait.

The okada rider did not wait.

He came from the Last Stop side, fast, leaning, trying to cut between a taxi pulling out and the pedestrian opening. Perhaps he did not see her crate at that angle. Perhaps he thought she would stop. She did not stop. She could not, easily, with that weight.

The bike clipped the lower crate.

What happened next was very fast and then very slow.

Eggs — thirty at least — fell and broke on the tar, yolk bright as paint. The Madam spun with her hands empty. The rider skidded, caught himself with a foot, and for a half-second it looked like he would ride on.

Someone shouted “Agoo!” — not as a greeting. As a warning, as an accusation.

Then the crowd moved.

Kwame had seen this before. A crowd in Mallam Town could become a single thing quicker than any individual meant it to. The men nearest the spill — passengers, drivers off-duty, two boys who had been sleeping by the kiosk — closed around the okada rider. One reached for his mirror and snapped it forward. Another grabbed his shoulder.

“Whaaaaaat! You want to kill Madam eehh!”

“Stooooop! You be fool!”

The rider, a boy no older than twenty, with a faded Lacoste shirt, tried to lift his hands. He said, “Madam, sorry, Madam — ” but his voice was underneath.

Another hand went for his collar. Another man — not even looking at the Madam anymore, looking at the crowd looking at him — raised his elbow.

Kwame moved.

He did not hurry. That was what people would remember later and get wrong, calling it slow. It was not slow. It was unswerving.

He stepped between the elbow and the boy's face and put his left arm up, not high, just enough. The elbow hit his forearm. The sound of it was dull, not loud. The crowd made a small sound of surprise, as if they had expected him to speak first.

“Leave am,” Kwame said. Quietly.

The man with the elbow — Kofi, a driver who owed Kwame money and therefore liked him less when sober — blinked.

“Station master, he spoil Madam market! He go run!”

“I say leave am.”

Same volume. Same flat. Kwame did not look at Kofi to give the words weight. He looked at the boy on the bike, then at the Madam, who was now standing with both hands on her head, looking at her yolk-covered road as if it were a small death.

“Madam,” Kwame said. “How much?”

She shook her head. Not refusing. Calculating, through shock. “My eggs. Thirty. Maybe thirty-five. Fresh from Ashaiman. My money — ”

“Thirty,” Kwame said. He knew she would say thirty-five if she could, and that it would be true. He also knew the crowd wanted a number to stop being angry about.

He turned his head, not his body. “Ebo. Kpakpo. Come.”

The senior drivers came because Kwame never called them to watch him shout. They came to listen. Ebo was already frowning at Kofi's hand still on the rider's collar.

“Leave am,” Ebo echoed, this time with weight, and Kofi let go.

Kwame looked at the rider. “You get money?”

The boy's eyes filled. “Charlie, I get ₵20 — ”

That got a bitter laugh from the edge of the crowd, which was how Kwame knew the mood was still bad. ₵20 would not buy ten eggs, let alone thirty.

Kwame nodded once, as if the boy had said something sensible. He did not explain himself. He never did. He turned to the drivers nearest — not the crowd, the drivers — and said, “We go settle Madam.”

Yaw understood first. “Station master — ”

“I go borrow,” Kwame said. Which was not asking. And also, in Kwame's style, was asking completely.

A small silence fell where the crowd had been loud. This was familiar too. Kwame asking for anything was rare enough that refusing him felt heavier than giving. Kpakpo reached into his trousers pocket and pulled notes without counting.

“₵150,” Kpakpo said. “Take.”

Ebo added ₵150 without comment. Three hundred cedis.

Kwame took it with both hands — a small, deliberate wai that meant thank you and also meant this was now his debt, not the station's, not the riders', his — and counted it in front of the Madam without hurry. He separated what he gave her into her palm, note by note, so the crowd could see each one land.

“Madam, thirty eggs. ₵300. You fit use am buy new crate. You go get change small for trotro.”

She looked at the money, then at him, and her mouth did something complicated. Not quite a smile. Relief under shame.

“God bless you, Station master. God bless you eehh.”

“Go,” Kwame told the rider, still quietly. “Go. Next time you see Madam crate, you wait eehh. You hear me?”

The boy nodded so hard his helmet, which had been hanging from his handle, swung. He righted his bike with shaking hands. Kofi made to say something else and Ebo's look stopped him.

The crowd, deprived of a beating, found it needed to do something else. It dispersed into small, muttering groups. Yaw, ever helpful, began directing traffic with large arm movements no one had asked for. The preacher, who had watched the whole thing with his PA briefly lowered, lifted it again and tried to attach the moral:

“Brothers! See how the peacemaker — ”

Kwame walked away before he could finish the verse. He did not need verses applied to him out loud.

• • •

By 10:00 a.m. the station quieted in the way it always did, mid-morning to early afternoon — as if the town had spent its noise and needed to gather more. The food women fanned their coals and talked among themselves. The preacher had packed his speaker; his battery had given up, and he was loading it onto a bicycle with the help of a child.

Kwame sat on the low wall by the junction — Last Stop, the barrier, same place — and looked at his hands. They were dusty from counting money. His left forearm throbbed where Kofi's elbow had hit it. He would not mention it. He had been hit harder for less reason and had said less.

Ebo came and sat beside him without invitation. Old drivers did that.

“You do good thing,” Ebo said after a while. “That boy for collect.”

Kwame shrugged a fraction. “He be boy.”

“You borrow that money you no for borrow,” Ebo said, not unkindly. He knew Kwame's house was eight rooms rented singly, that Mansa kept the books, that Mansa would ask where ₵300 had gone. He did not need to say any of that. In Mallam Town everyone knew everyone else's rent.

“I go pay,” Kwame said.

Ebo nodded, satisfied with a thin answer. He had known Kwame long enough to know not to wait for a second sentence.

A small kasoa FM transistor, left on a stool by the barber's kiosk, murmured the 10 a.m. headlines. Market prices. A funeral announcement read with that same emotional voice. A reminder about land registration — “…owners of newly built houses are advised to ensure documentation with the lands commission to avoid land guard disturbances…” — the readers voice adding a little thrill to *disturbances* that the text probably did not contain. Kwame half-listened. The phrase *land guard* passed without catching, that morning. It was background noise, like the PA, like the dust. The kind of thing you heard at the station and forgot by the time you reached home.

Yaw passed with a passenger's bag and called, “Station master, today your sales go spoil. Morning bad luck *Nkoaa*!” — just bad luck — and laughed to show he meant it as a joke and also not.

Kwame did not laugh. He looked down the road that led out of town — good enough by day to pick your line, to swerve if you paid attention — and thought, without wanting to, of the hill, of the gate that would need his palm tonight so it would not screech, of Mansa who would be counting the day's money in her head before he opened his mouth.

He was already in debt and had not told her. That was his closest thing to a secret, and it was not sinister at all. It was just ₵300, borrowed from two men who trusted him, to replace thirty broken eggs for a Madam whose name he did not know.

He stood, brushed dust from his trousers, and went to check the queues. There was still work before the evening rush built again at three. Still time to earn it back before eight, before home.

He did not know that at eight he would arrive home already tired and already poorer than Mansa believed, that at 11:42 p.m. she would call him good for nothing and mean it as fear, that he would leave without answering because answering would be noise and noise made things worse, that at the junction where he now stood he would sit again in six hours trying to make quiet do the work of speech.

He knew, this morning, only this: the station needed its peacemaker. He knew how to be that. You stood in the middle, you took the hit on your forearm, you said little, you paid.

The dust settled slowly over the spill where the eggs had been, yolk paling under sun and grit, until it looked like nothing had happened at all.
