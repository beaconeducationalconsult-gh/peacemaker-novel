# Chapter 27 — Mallam Town

*Epilogue — weeks later*

Weeks later he drove again.

The Corolla, wire fixed — not replaced, fixed, because fourteen years' cars are fixed not renewed — started on the first try now, as if it had been apologized to. The low wall at Last Stop where he had sat at midnight and at midday and at gunpoint was still warm in the way walls keep day. The barrier pole, once hit, now straight, moved smoothly on its hinge. The policeman who fanned himself with a notebook was another policeman, but the fanning was the same.

Mallam Town woke the same way.

Roosters, still nature's most reliable PA system. No fundraising appeal. He listened to them from the hill now with Mansa beside him, not turned away. Her head still bandaged lightly, her arm still splinted but sitting. She counted the roosters — one, two, three — and said, small, “Second one late today.” She was counting again. That was her way of being alive.

At the gate, he did not lift.

He pushed, and it screeched — a short, respectable screech that made Abena's doorway open and Abena's face appear, not with gossip but with a small, loaded, honest smile that said *I told salon no story.* She waved — not the salon wave — a small, real wave that meant *I beg every day, as I said.* He waved back. Mansa, beside him, said, not loud, “Abena bring soup yesterday,” and that was its own record.

He drove the twenty-six minutes with windows down, potholes visible, swerve-able. *During the day, you could see the potholes coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you.* He said it once more, this time to Mansa, not to junior drivers, and she said, “At night, we no drive,” which was her way of adding a verse.

The station was same and not.

Seven queues by 6 a.m. still, dust and horns and, at the edge, a new preacher with a PA that crackled on *holy* the same way the old one's had, but with a battery that lasted longer. Yaw saw him first.

“Station master!” Yaw shouted, forgetting the *eehh* because excitement replaced it. He ran — not walked, ran — and the queue nearest — Kasoa — clapped without knowing why they clapped, then kept clapping because Yaw clapped.

Ebo stood. Kpakpo stood. The small council that had formed around a Madam's eggs and held through a manhunt and a hearing stood again.

Kwame got out. No speech. He put his palm flat on his car's roof — the way he had once put it on the gate — and looked at the station he had kept for twelve years and that had, for a week, kept a different story about him.

“Bad Luck *Nkoaa* finish?” Yaw called, laughing to make it safe.

“Finish,” Kwame said. Terse. But now, after Chapter 26, terse read as steady, not as distance.

Sule the barber left his kiosk, clippers still in hand, and said to the seamstress who had said *fresh house, no paper — na invitation*, “We talk too much, eehh. Kiosk talk be like wind. We talk, we no hear curtain.” The seamstress, who had made Mansa's church dresses, nodded without defending the wind. That was the town's diffuse antagonist learning, without being sentenced, to be quieter.

Chocho passed once at the junction — slippers quiet, Lacoste flat — with one boy, not two, not looking for a house. He nodded to Kwame, a nod that was not apology and not alliance, just the precise, transactional acknowledgement of a man whose outside *Musah* had been proven true and whose own *not my boy* had allowed daylight at a cemetery gate. Kwame nodded back. Same economy as before, but now without suspicion needing to be carried as evidence.

A small boy he did not know brought a transistor to his bench and turned it down, as if offering silence.

At 10 a.m., when the station quieted to its other form — waiting room, not market — Kwame sat where he had counted ₵285 that afternoon the Camry broke the barrier, where he had later counted ₵295 before Mansa called him good for nothing, and where, weeks later, counting was not needed.

Nene came.

She did not come to be thanked. She came because herbs needed selling and the station was where selling happened. She put a small bundle on his bench — leaves tied — and said, practical, “For Mansa head.” Gentle, patient, the voice she used with him. Then, to the boys who had surrounded her hut, unsentimental: “You move small.” They moved.

She sat. She did not need to say *I doubted and now doubt proved*. She had been the fixed point other characters moved around, and now the moving had stopped.

Mansa, who had been brought by Yaw's brother's car — taxi by taxi, because the town is a tissue of lifts — arrived at 10:30 with her bandage smaller, her exercise book still red but unopened, and sat where passengers sit when they are not passengers but family. People who had said *she even called him useless* now looked at her with the particular shame that follows being loud with a wrong story, and she, not performing, simply put her splinted hand on Kwame's wrist where gate-muffling lived.

“We dey,” she said. Two words. In her arithmetic, two words plus quiet could be trust, if quiet was chosen together.

Kwame put his other hand over hers. The sales pouch — new, not leather, cloth — had ₵40 in it, ordinary, small. He would tell her the amount before she asked. That was the change that did not need a screech to be heard.

At 11 a.m. the police pickup passed Last Stop without needing to. Nanfuri was not in it. He was at the post where a recording of *Daddy, you lock?* had once been a background Musah and was now a file closed. He had told Kwame, weeks earlier, *we go cemetery with men, daylight.* He had. The gate east of the hill, crooked post, still stood, now with a small new lock that was not juju but policy. Things that had read as eerie at dusk read as administered by day. That change was the book's thesis in a lock.

At noon — not six — Kasoa FM read news. Market prices, funeral, then: *land commission reminds owners of newly built houses — seven-house house case — to protect documents…* The presenter added no extra thrill to *protect*. That, too, was a change: text without added emotions, because emotions had been spent where they belonged — on a call that cut.

Dust rose when a car pulled out too fast, yolk-pale, then settled. It looked like nothing had happened, then like everything had, then like the ordinary work of the afternoon, which was the work Kwame knew best: keep the queue tight, keep the peace where peace was order, and where order needed speech, speak the three sentences, even small, even terse, because silence, he had learned, is not the same as peace.

He stood to move Kpakpo's car one length forward to keep the line correct. Kpakpo, seeing it, did not need to say *that be station*. The station said it.

Mallam Town kept talking — kiosks, barbering salons, hairdressing salons, apketeshie bars, food joints — its second nervous system firing, but now with a different story to carry to the hill, one that, for once, had been told carefully.

Kwame listened, checked the verse silently where a preacher was close enough, and, when the preacher was not close enough, let dust, not gossip, settle.
