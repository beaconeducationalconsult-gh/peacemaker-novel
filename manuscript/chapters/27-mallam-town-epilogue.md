# Chapter 27 — Mallam Town

*Epilogue — weeks later — hill, station, road, cemetery with lock*

Weeks later he drove again.

The Corolla, wire fixed — not replaced, fixed, because fourteen years' cars are fixed not renewed when you wash them every Sunday whether they need it or not — started on the first try now, as if it had been apologized to after being driven seven houses to a light and then to a stash under blue tarp. The low wall at Last Stop where he had sat at midnight to count silence, at midday to count ₵285 that afternoon the Camry broke the barrier, at midnight again to count a phone that said dead not off, and at gunpoint where wire snapped — that wall was still warm in the way walls keep day even when night has tried them. The barrier pole, once hit with hollow *thwack* and sent swinging and later straightened by a young policeman with two hands, now moved smoothly on its hinge, up, down, no sound. The policeman who fanned himself with a notebook at noon was another policeman — taller, newer — but the fanning was the same, the notebook same, the under-manned same that had let a tinted no-plate Camry choose a junction and that now let a daylight arrest with backup close a gate east of his hill house.

Mallam Town woke the same way.

Roosters, still nature's most reliable PA system. No fundraising appeal at the end, no battery to die, just competition — one near the cemetery fence east where the cleared path waited, one behind the eight single-room self-contained apartments on the hill, one further down toward low houses where the road flattened. He listened to them from the hill now with Mansa beside him, not turned away the way she had been at 4 a.m. when she breathed steady and away. Her head still bandaged lightly — smaller white now, not true white seep — her left arm still splinted but sitting upright, not lying small. She counted the roosters the way she counted everything — one, two, three — and said, small, not sharp, not salon, the hospital voice gone, the home voice returning: “Second one late today.” She was counting again. That was her way of being alive, and of being his wife, and of being thirty-eight with a red exercise book that had taken a dent from his palm.

At the gate, he did not lift.

He had lifted every morning at 4 a.m. and every night at 11:44 after *good for nothing*, every time he needed eight doors not to open — lift, push, no screech, the gate that screeched unless muffled by hand. In the yard at the police station he had let a different gate screech on purpose, small, to show change. Now at his own hill gate — metal, familiar, hinge weight known — he also did not lift.

He pushed.

It screeched — a short, respectable screech, not *Screeeeech*, just enough to make the compound hear itself. Abena's doorway — *Nkonkonsa* but also soup — opened and Abena's face appeared, not with gossip that wants to be carried to salon, but with a small, loaded, honest smile that said *I told salon no story — no small advice as story — I tell them follow advice no be play.* She waved — not the salon wave that is a performance with eyes to the shop door — a small, real wave that meant *I beg every day, as I said to you in the visiting room, as I said to Mansa at the curtain, as I say to the wall now.*

He waved back — not big — a station master's small wai with head. Mansa, beside him with splint, said, not loud because loud is for kiosks, “Abena bring soup yesterday — light soup, not palm — for head.” And that was its own record, not gossip but *soup*, ordinary, given the same outline care as people.

He drove the twenty-six minutes with windows down, as he had the morning he drove with headlights wedge and the night he drove toward three people who said *You go Lapaz or no?* Now potholes were visible. *During the day, you could see the potholes coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you.* He said it once more, this time to Mansa, not to junior drivers, and not as warning but as fact that had become thesis and then lock. She said, “At night, we no drive — we walk, we call, we follow not through window.” Which was her way of adding a verse and unsaying *good for nothing* without needing to unsay.

The station was same and not — the same the way a man is same and not after a file is stamped twice, red, not quite even.

Seven queues by 6 a.m. still, dust and horns and yams hissing, signs hand-painted leaning like tired men, water flicked to keep dust down though dust never listens. At the edge, where the old preacher had crackled on *holy* at seven a.m. and where Kwame had checked Matthew 7:13 silently, a new preacher — not new, just different — with a PA that crackled the same way on *holy* but with a battery that lasted longer because someone had finally fixed the cord — preached *narrow road* and then *appeal*. Yaw was already there, twenty-six, winning arguments with roads.

Yaw saw him first.

“Station master!” Yaw shouted, forgetting the *eehh* because excitement replaced the sentence-final and because excitement, unlike *eehh*, does not need to be light. He ran — not walked, the walk of a station master — ran — and the queue nearest — Kasoa, his old queue — clapped without knowing why they clapped beyond *he is here*, then kept clapping because Yaw clapped and because clapping at a station is contagious like gossip.

Ebo stood. Slow, because old knees are honest about standing. Kpakpo stood. Smaller, sharp-eyed, still evaluating the road for potholes where others see tar. The small council that had formed around a Madam's thirty eggs at seven a.m. and that had said *you stand for boy, we stand for you* and that had held through a manhunt that paused for night and a hearing where *not my boy* was said and a cemetery gate at daylight with backup — that council stood again, not to settle a dispute but to show that some disputes have been settled.

Kwame got out. No speech — not yet — he put his palm flat on his car's roof — fourteen years, wire fixed — the way he had once put it on the gate to muffle and on the table to steady, and looked at the station he had kept for twelve years and that had, for a week, kept a different story about him at kiosks, salons, bars — *he push for other woman* — and that now kept his car.

“Bad Luck *Nkoaa* finish?” Yaw called, laughing to make it safe, to make it small enough to be carried, to make it a joke that was also a question about whether a town can stop blaming juju when a file says *fall not push*.

“Finish,” Kwame said. Terse. But now, after Chapter 26 where he had said *I borrow for eggs, I hide not woman, I refuse at gun*, after gate screech on purpose, terse read as steady, not as distance. The same word at 7 a.m. — *leave am* — had been peace as intervention; this *finish* was peace as return.

Sule the barber left his kiosk — clippers still in hand, cord still careful — and said to the seamstress who had said at midday *fresh house, no wall finish — na invitation* and who had made Mansa's church dresses and who had been part of the second nervous system — “We talk too much, eehh. Kiosk talk be like wind — we talk, we no hear curtain move for draft. We talk, we no hear phone dead meaning impact. We talk, we make two stories when one fall dey.” The seamstress who had made Mansa's church dress — church of Christ dresses are particular, modest, held at the shoulder — nodded without defending the wind. She looked at Mansa's bandage smaller and at Kwame's door handle fixed but still snapped-trace and said nothing, which was its own learning. That was the town's diffuse antagonist — gossip — learning, without being sentenced, without a stamp, to be quieter, because the book does not need to resolve the coercive economy Chocho represents, only to not pretend it isn't there, and does not need to resolve gossip, only to let it hear.

Chocho passed once at the junction — not at the hut, not at the station, at the junction where seven houses measure — slippers quiet, Lacoste flat, collar precise, one boy, not two, not looking for a house or for a barrier to break or a headlight to avoid. He nodded to Kwame — a nod that was not apology and not alliance, just the precise, transactional acknowledgement of a man whose outside *Musah — bring car comot — no make headlight catch wall* had been proven true and whose own *not my boy — Musah be outside community* had allowed daylight at a cemetery gate with backup to close a loop that started with his own *make boys check Nene side last*. Kwame nodded back. Same economy as before at midday — *we dey watch peace* — but now without the extra need to carry suspicion as evidence, because suspicion had been filed as red herring and then stamped as *outside*.

A small boy he did not know — not Yaw, not the station's — brought a transistor to his bench — not Kasoa FM adding emotions, just a game, low — and turned it down a fraction, as if offering silence the way Nene offered *water first*. Small, learned.

At 10 a.m., when the station quieted to its other form — waiting room after market, not market — the way it did at 10:30 in Chapter 2 when queues thinned to traders and nursing mothers, Kwame sat where he had sat at 10 a.m. two days after the egg seller with ₵285 that afternoon the Camry broke the barrier, where he had later sat at 10 a.m. with ₵295 before *good for nothing*, where he had counted and been short, and where, weeks later, counting was not needed and therefore he did not count.

Nene came.

She did not come to be thanked — fixed anchors do not need thanks, they need to be where they are. She came because herbs needed selling and the station at 10 a.m. is where selling happens and because Mansa's head still needed bitter leaves tied. She put a small bundle on his bench — leaves tied with string reused, not new string — and said, practical, unsentimental with the search team but gentle with him: “For Mansa head. Boil, not chew. Morning, not night.” The voice she had used at 5:40 — *stay, you can hear?* — and at the hut — *you no enter* — and at the station — *you go see my pots*. Then, to the boys who had once surrounded her hut and now stood nearby with caps on backwards, the same unsentimental: “You move small, make air come.” They moved — not because she was chief, but because she was Nene, which in this town is chief enough when the chief is not needed.

She sat. She did not need to say *I doubted and now doubt proved* — that sentence, the fixed anchor's only movement, was known without being said, the way a gate that screeches once on purpose is known.

Mansa, who had been brought by Yaw's brother's car — taxi by taxi, because the town is a tissue of lifts and because a wife of a station master is still a passenger where the station respects her husband — arrived at 10:30 with her bandage smaller than the ward's bandage and smaller than the gate's screech, with her exercise book still red but unopened because numbers today did not need writing, and sat where passengers sit when they are not passengers but family — on the bench, close enough that pawpaw tree distance became bench distance.

People who had stood in the compound at Day 1 mid-morning and said *she even called him useless* — neighbours who had heard an argument through thin walls and made it a verdict — now looked at her with the particular shame that follows being loud with a wrong story when the right story has a faint pulse and a curtain draft. She did not perform forgiveness. She simply put her splinted left hand — still splinted, still careful — on Kwame's wrist where gate-muffling had lived for years with palm flat and lift, where rope had been at Nene's hut, where cuff had been loose in a flat room, where Nene's hand had been.

“We dey,” she said. Two words. In her arithmetic, two words plus quiet could be trust, if quiet was chosen together after being chosen poorly alone.

Kwame put his other hand over hers. Not to cover. To hold.

The sales pouch — new, not leather, cloth, bought because leather had been evidence — had ₵40 in it from two short runs that morning — Kasoa and back — ordinary, small, not the ₵300 that had been shame, not the ₵5 short that had been proof. He would tell her the amount before she asked — ₵40 — before she could ask, before *how be market?* needed answering, because that was the small, non-screech change that did not need a gate to be heard.

At 11 a.m. the police pickup passed Last Stop without needing to, without hitting a barrier, tyres full, not on rims. Nanfuri was not in it. He was at the post where a recording of *Daddy, you lock? — Musah bring car — Saaba!* had once been a background Musah and was now a file closed with a new lock on a crooked post. He had told Kwame, weeks earlier, *we go cemetery with men, daylight — we see*. He had, and Kwame had thanked him not for arrest but for *go with men*. The gate east of the hill, crooked post, still stood east, still iron, but now with a small new lock that was not juju but policy, not supernatural but cleared path administered. Things that had read as eerie at dusk — stillness that didn't feel empty — read as administered by day. That change was the book's thesis in a lock the way *During the day, you could see the potholes coming and swerve. At night, the potholes found you* was the book's thesis in a road.

At noon — not six, not the time Kasoa FM used to add emotions — Kasoa FM read news. Market prices, a funeral announcement read with slightly less emotion than before, then: *...land commission reminds owners of newly built houses — following the seven-house new house case — to ensure documentation with the lands commission to avoid land guard disturbances — protect your documents, protect your house...* The presenter added no extra thrill to *disturbances* and no extra thrill to *protect*. That, too, was a change: text without the added emotions the drivers had mocked, because emotions had been spent where they belonged — on a background *Musah*, on a daughter's *Daddy*, on a cut line.

Dust rose when a car — not his, not Camry — pulled out too fast, yolk-pale, the way eggs at seven a.m. two weeks earlier had been yolk-bright on tar before setting, then settled slowly over the station's signs, over the preacher's speaker cord, over the seven queues' dust that never listens. It looked like nothing had happened, for a second — the station as it was at 6 a.m. — then like everything had — the barrier hit, the curtain moved, the phone dead, the half-wall, the hut, the rope, the stamp — then like the ordinary work of the afternoon, which was the work Kwame knew best and had known for twelve years before one night asked him to learn a new work:

Keep the queue tight — that kept order where order was needed. Keep the peace where peace was order.

And where order needed speech — even small speech, even three sentences, even terse, even quiet-then-true, even *I borrow, I hide not woman, I refuse at gun* — speak, because silence, he had learned from a hill gate that once required a palm and now was allowed a screech, from a curtain that moved for a draft, from a phone that died on impact, is not the same as peace.

He stood to move Kpakpo's car one length forward to keep the line correct the way queues correct themselves. Kpakpo, seeing it without being told, did not need to say *that be station*. The station — seven queues, dust, horn, preacher checking verse, gate now allowed to sound — said it.

Mallam Town kept talking — kiosks, barbering salons, hairdressing salons, apketeshie bars, food joints — its second nervous system still firing, still diffuse, still antagonist when it wanted to be, still companion when it needed, but now, for a week at least, with a different story to carry to the hill, one that, for once, had been told carefully, building to building, house to house, gate to gate, last house to first.

Kwame listened, checked the preacher's verse silently where a preacher was close enough to be checked, and, when the preacher was not close enough and the PA was too loud, let dust — not gossip — settle a second longer before he answered.

And when he answered, he answered.
