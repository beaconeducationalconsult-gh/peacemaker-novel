# Chapter 3 — The Black Camry

*Day 0, 3:00 p.m. – 6:30 p.m.*

The station woke up a second time at three.

Kwame felt it in his bench before he saw it. A vibration in the low wall where he sat, as if the queue itself had decided to stand and stretch. Drivers who had been half-asleep at midday folded their phones, clapped their doors shut, straightened those hand-painted signs that had wilted in the heat. Passengers came now with the hurry that morning passengers did not have — market women with basins now empty and wanting to be home before dark, schoolchildren with shirts untucked, men with cement dust on their boots who needed Circle or Lapaz and did not want to bargain.

The sales pouch began, slowly, to make sense.

Kwame did the Kasoa run at 3:10 and was back by 3:40. He did Lapaz at 3:45 — short, sharp, good money for fuel. Kpakpo kept his promise and took the long Madina run that Kwame would have lost time on. Ebo, who claimed his knees hurt when it was convenient and not otherwise, took Circle and complained about every pothole on the way as if Kwame had made them.

By 4:30 Kwame had ₵240.

He counted it standing by the junction end, not sitting. From Last Stop you could see both the station and the road. The barrier pole was down now — the policeman had woken fully for the evening, his shirt tucked, his notebook closed. Chief Inspector Nanfuri's pickup was parked just beyond it, not quite at the post, at an angle that said the driver had stopped to talk and not to buy anything.

Kwame knew Nanfuri by sight the way everyone at the station knew him. Not because Nanfuri came often — he didn't — but because when he came, drivers remembered to close the doors they had left open. Fifty-seven, thirty years in service, a man who gave clipped commands and then went quiet to listen to his own thinking. He was at the post now with two constables, one tall and one who kept touching his belt where his whistle hung.

Kwame was about to look away — he did not like to watch police when they were not watching him — when the Camry came.

He did not hear it before he saw it. That was the first wrong thing.

On the Mallam road by day you always heard a car that wanted to go fast before you saw it — the engine testing the tar, the horns of the taxis it had cut. This one made almost no sound until it was very close, dark-tinted, no plates, unmarked Toyota Camry with the kind of tint that made the windows look like they had been painted.

It came from the town side, toward Last Stop, at a speed that made the market women near the kiosk step back even though they were not in the road. The policeman at the barrier saw it late and reached for the pole — a small, practiced gesture — and then, seeing it was not going to stop, pulled his hand back as if the pole were hot.

The Camry did not slow.

It hit the barrier.

Not hard enough to shatter — the pole was made to give — but hard enough to make a sound Kwame felt in his chest, a hollow *thwack*, and to send the striped pole swinging up and then down again on its hinge, clattering. The Camry went through without deviating, its left mirror missing the policeman's shoulder by a hand's breadth, its blackness swallowing the afternoon for a half-second.

Someone shouted. Yaw shouted. A woman with a basin shouted *Agoo!* and meant it as *look out*.

Nanfuri did not shout.

He was already moving. Kwame saw it not as a decision but as a trained sequence: Nanfuri's hand on the pickup's door, the tall constable in the back, the one with the whistle running for the passenger side, Nanfuri's mouth forming a word that Kwame could not hear over the sudden, ordinary roar of the station realizing something had happened.

The pickup started, reversed a foot, turned sharply, and went.

Kwame stood at Last Stop with his sales pouch still in his hand. The Camry was already past the junction, past the seven houses where Saaba's fresh paint caught the sun — he saw it pass that house, did not think about that house — and then it was on the road that left Mallam, the good-by-day road, and then it was dust.

The pickup followed, same road, same dust, but angrier, lower.

For a moment the station was very quiet in the way a place is quiet after a loud thing stops, not because it is calm but because everyone is deciding what to say.

Then everyone said it at once.

• • •

No one at the station saw the chase.

That was the fact that would become gossip, then evidence, then a story people said they had seen even though they had not.

Kwame stayed where he was because his queue was there, because passengers still needed Lapaz, because his job was to keep the station from becoming only a crowd that had seen a thing. He loaded a Circle taxi at 4:40, a Kasoa taxi at 4:55. Drivers talked while they loaded, as they always did, but now they talked about the same thing with different endings.

“It be land guard car,” Sule the barber said loudly from his kiosk, having left his clippers to come to the road edge. “You see tint? No plate. Na land guard way. They break barrier because barrier be their work place.”

“It no be land guard,” Kpakpo said, but without certainty. “Land guard no go break police barrier for afternoon. They do their work for night.”

“They watch that seven-house house,” the seamstress said, not to anyone, to everyone. “That house for Nanfuri daughter. Fresh house. Rich police pikin. Na that dem target.”

Kwame half-listened. He was counting again. ₵240. Then ₵260 after the next Kasoa run. The Camry and the pickup had turned off the tar within minutes — he had not seen it, but the drivers who had run to the road's bend said so — from tarred to gravel to packed earth where even by day you needed to know the road.

The transistor, turned up again for news, gave them nothing. Kasoa FM was on music, an old highlife with a guitar that sounded like a man trying not to cry.

At 5:15 the road that had taken the two vehicles gave back only wind.

At 5:40 — Kwame checked his phone because Yaw asked — one of the constables' colleagues at the post, a young man who had not gone on the chase, kept walking to the road edge and looking long in the direction they had gone, then walking back to the post as if he had forgotten why he had walked forward.

At 6:10, when the light was already thinning and the queues had thinned with it, the pickup returned.

Kwame saw it first because he was still at Last Stop, where he had been all afternoon. It came slowly, not as it had left. Both left tyres were flat — not punctured, ruined — and the truck moved on rims with a sound that made passengers wince. The front bumper was caked with laterite. The tall constable was driving. The one with the whistle sat in the back with his head down. Nanfuri sat in the passenger seat, dusty to the shoulders, his shirt dark with sweat, his hat not on his head but in his hands.

They did not stop at the barrier. They stopped past it, near the station's edge, where the preacher's speaker had been. They got out without hurry. No one helped them. No one, Kwame saw, quite knew how to help them.

Nanfuri stood for a long minute and looked toward the station, through it, not at it. Kwame had seen that look on drivers who had driven all night and then remembered they had forgotten something at home but could not remember what. Exhaustion, yes. But not only exhaustion. Something else that had not yet found its sentence.

“What happen, Commander?” Sule called, too loud, too eager.

Nanfuri looked at him, and Sule took a half step back.

“Carefully,” Nanfuri said.

Just that. One word. Not to Sule. To his two men, and to himself.

He said it again, quieter, as if confirming it had been the right word that afternoon. “Carefully.”

Then he put his hat on. The two constables followed him toward the post, not toward the station. They did not explain. They did not say *cemetery*. But later — in the way town stories assemble themselves from what tight lips do not say — the word would appear.

Kwame watched them go. He had seen police return shaken before. He had never seen them return *close-mouthed* in that particular way, as if what they had seen near the cemetery gate — though no one said the word yet — had not been what they could report, only what they could feel. The stillness that didn't feel empty. The cleared path where no path should be cleared. The sense, correct at the level of the skin, that they had been measured and warned.

That night, those details would be gossip. That afternoon, they were just a look and a word.

Yaw came to Kwame's shoulder.

“Charlie, you see? Tyres shoot. Both left tyres. Who shoot tyre for afternoon eehh? This no be small thing.”

Kwame nodded. He could still see the flat tyres' shape, the way the pickup had limped.

At 6:30 the drivers were back at their benches, sales pouches open, faces longer than the money inside warranted. Yaw, ever the counter, declared his sales with mock tragedy.

“Bad Luck *Nkoaa*!” he said, holding his pouch upside down. “Morning eggs, afternoon barrier. Business spoil. Station master, we for pray.”

A few men laughed. The others, tired, agreed because it was easier.

Kwame had ₵285.

Fifteen cedis short of the ₵300 he owed before he had sold anything that morning. That was the math that stayed with him as the station's evening rhythm stuttered and then stopped, as the preacher did not return, as the transistor found news again and the presenter's emotional voice said nothing about a Camry but said a lot about a funeral that had many family members attending.

He knew, without being told, that tomorrow Kasoa FM would have something else to read. He did not know it would be a name he did not yet know — Saaba — and that her house, seven houses from where he stood, would be the reason the barrier had not mattered that afternoon.

At seven houses down, lights came on in Saaba's new house — warm, ordinary, glass windows now glowing. A silhouette moved behind them. Ordinary evening. Ordinary call to a father who would later hear that light go out.

Kwame zipped his pouch. ₵285. The road out of Mallam was still visible if you looked carefully, potholes still avoidable if you paid attention. At night it would be different.

He did not know that, in forty minutes of chase that he had not seen, a man had leaned out of a Camry window to photograph Nanfuri specifically before shooting, that the Camry had vanished through the cemetery gate east of his hill house, tyre track ending inside, that Nanfuri had stepped inside, seen signs of use, and chosen carefully to withdraw rather than go further alone.

He knew only that the station had done its usual trick — a loud thing, then talk, then tiredness — and that he was still short, and that home was twenty-six minutes away, and that Mansa would be waiting with thin walls and sharp ears.

He lifted his car keys. The metal was warm from his pocket.

“Make we close small,” he said to Ebo, who nodded as if Kwame had made a speech.

They closed small. The barrier pole, knocked askew, was straightened by the young policeman who had stayed behind. Last Stop, again, was just a junction.

Only later would Kwame understand he had stood that whole afternoon at the exact place where, after midnight, he would try to make silence do the work of speech.
