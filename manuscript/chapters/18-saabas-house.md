# Chapter 18 — Saaba's House

*Later — custody, flat light*

They told him about the house before they let him hear it.

That was how secondhand worked when you were not allowed to be at your own search — and how the ledger's plant was supposed to work: existence before content, questions before answers.

The constable who brought him tea — not the interrogator with *fact*, not Nanfuri, not anyone whose title needed capital letters — mentioned it while placing the sachet on the table's ordinary linework, as if small talk were required by the flat light that made every room look storybook-safe regardless of its content.

“They search that seven-house house today,” he said, not looking at Kwame's face — looking at the cup, at the way tea skin forms if you do not drink quickly. He was young, tired from standing at posts where barriers had been broken, and his deferential way of not looking was also his way of being kind.

“Fine house, glass window,” he went on, because every man in Mallam Town described that house that way whether he had built it or not. “Seven houses from Last Stop, you know. Fresh paint, low wall no render. Land guard target because fresh. No fence finish. Police say recording dey. For inside.”

Kwame's hands, still cuffed loosely to the table ring the way a man is cuffed when his file has two charges and his lip still holds blood, made the same small sound on metal — wanting to be still, not rattling.

“Recording?” he said. Two syllables. His voice still terse, not because he wanted to be terse but because terse was how he checked verses without arguing, how he asked for money without begging, how he refused a pistol without shouting.

The constable shrugged, polite, the way Mallam Town people shrug when they have said more than they meant and less than they know, when a transistor has already said it on Kasoa FM and therefore saying it again feels like repeating gossip rather than giving news.

“Phone record. Girl dey call before robbers enter. They find am for counter where she leave phone for speaker. House record am.”

That was the plant — the existence, not the content — delivered secondhand, as the reworked single-POV rule required: Kwame does not witness the search. He hears about it and has to interpret it with the body of a man who was beaten seven houses away after refusing even at gunpoint.

The constable left the sachet untouched — procedural kindness — and took the empty cup from yesterday. The flat light stayed. Pots and stools not in the room still received their careful outline care in Kwame's mind because the illustration guide said ordinary objects get the same attention as people when a town is trying to stay ordinary.

For an hour nothing else happened. Kwame counted cures the way Nene would later teach him to — not by hours but by small tasks: water first, then hear, then tell. He pictured the house seven houses from Last Stop — the same Last Stop where he had sat at midnight trying to let quiet die down, same barrier hit at four p.m. by a Camry that photographed a commander — and tried not to picture who would have been in that house on a night when lights were warm, when a daughter in a new building calls the man who arrested people for a living to say *I dey house, I lock.*

He knew that house without knowing its name's owner — Saaba, Chief Inspector Nanfuri's daughter — the seamstress at the barber's had named her at midday as *fine girl, father be police man, big one* and Sule had added *new house, no wall finish — na invitation.* That invitation had been answered.

Later — afternoon, same flat light but now the sun through the station's high window had moved onto the stamp pad, making its felt redder — a visitor came.

Not Nene this time. Nene was still the fixed anchor, but she had told him everything the previous evening in the hut and now needed to be not in the town's sight so the town would not say *herbs woman coaching.*

Ebo.

Old driver, senior council, twelve years' colleague, the man who had added ₵150 without counting and said *that be station* and had promised *you stand for boy, we stand for you.* He did not sit the way police sat. He did not sit the way Nanfuri would sit with hat in hands. He sat with his knees a little open, hands on his thighs, looking at Kwame's beaten face and then at the table because looking too long felt like another interrogation and looking at the table felt like respect.

“Station master,” Ebo said. His voice had the faint hoarseness of a man who had slept little since Day 2 when rope became handcuffs and when Kasoa FM said *recording found at seven-house house* with that emotional voice that makes a lockdown sound like a funeral. “You hear small? We hear from radio. Kasoa FM say at noon: recording found at Saaba house, seven houses from Last Stop. She dey phone before attack.”

Kwame waited. He knew Ebo would give it the way drivers give road news — not tidy, not chronological, but accurate in the way drivers are accurate about potholes: where, how deep, when.

“Her father,” Ebo said, and looked up now, eyes wet in a way old men's eyes get when they have not slept and are trying not to show it. “She dey phone her father. Chief Inspector Nanfuri pikin. Nanfuri self dey phone when robbers break. Line cut. That be why Nanfuri hear live, but house phone more clear.”

Kwame closed his eyes.

That was the refinement locked 2026-09-15, the one that gave the recording its full weight: ordinary call, daughter to father, *Daddy, you lock?* The recording at the house was that call — her side — ordinary evening, ordinary check-in, because fresh house, because father is strict, because a daughter in a new building with glass windows that catch sun calls the man who arrested people for a living to say *I dey house, I lock* the way she had many evenings since the mason's boys finished. The kind of call that, later, would make the hearing of the background name devastating because the everyday is more devastating than the dramatic.

He could see it without seeing: Saaba at the counter, phone on speaker while she closed a kitchen window's low side, father on distant ear saying *You lock proper, Saaba*, and then — wood on wood where wood should not be wood at night — a window that should not be opening at night being forced where low wall no render gave fingers purchase.

“They say the phone dey for speaker,” Ebo went on, because he had heard it from a driver who had heard it from a policeman who had heard it from a policeman at the post where barriers break. Gossip, which the book calls its diffuse antagonist, was now accurate. “So house record am — not clean, not studio, dust and pot sound — but voice dey for back. Man talk for window, someone call name. Not Kwame name. Different name. Musah — or like. Not clean, but name there.”

That was the ledger's function: raise questions before answering. Voices, partial vehicle description somewhere in the audio's edge, someone using a name — not a clean confession. Enough for a lead, not enough to know whose network, not enough to know which Musah in a town where many mothers named sons Musah because many grandfathers had been Musah.

“Who name?” Kwame asked, still quiet, still the man who checked verses.

Ebo shook his head, not in denial but in the precise way a driver shakes his head when a road has many potholes and you ask which one. “We no hear proper. Station say name be Musah — like tall man? But Musah plenty for Accra. Police say they go bring Chocho to hear am tomorrow, maybe today late. Chocho know voice — his work be land for Mallam, but not this Camry — they say outside group. But station man —” He stopped himself, because he had been about to say *station man thought it was you because car be your own, because you borrow ₵300 and people think borrow be debt and debt be motive and police pikin house be revenge,* and that sentence, in a room where a man had two charges, needed no finishing.

Ebo put his hand, briefly, on the table near Kwame's cuffed hand, not touching metal, just near enough that the table's careful linework on ordinary objects — sachet, cup — seemed, for a second, like a shared cloth.

“You no dey alone for this story,” he said. Which was not *we believe you* — that would be a lie to a man who had not yet been told why he should be believed — but was, in Mallam Town, as close as a visitor in a police room could get without lying, as close as *we stand for you* had been that morning when he gave ₵150.

After Ebo left — knees open, standing slow because old knees are honest — the room went back to being a procedural diagram: outline uniform, hatching minimal, white walls, ordinary. Kwame sat in it and pictured, without wanting to, the two roads that now connected him to that house: Last Stop where his own car had been taken — reached-in ignition killing, wire snapping, gravel on cheek where potholes find you — and the short drive, seven houses, that made his car an easy tag for murder and made a girl's ordinary lock-check a house's record.

He pictured Last Stop's barrier and cemetery's gate — two different rites of passage, one formal (barrier), one ritual (gate) — and a father on a line that cut, now needing a background *Musah* to become an address outside Mallam.

He did not yet know the name's address. But the name existed now, somewhere between a kitchen window and a phone left on speaker, between a daughter's *Daddy* and a man's *make Musah bring car comot*, waiting for a land-guard leader to say *not my man — outside* and for a commander to say *with men*.

The constable returned with no tea, just a form and a pen that needed to be held left-handed because the right was cuffed.

“Sign,” he said, not unkind, in the voice of a man who stamps.

Kwame signed with his left hand, cramped. The officer wrote in his own small, careful hand that made even cots look neat: *informed of recording found at seven-house house — call with father, background voice (name), awaiting identifier (Chocho).* The words looked bureaucratic. The call was not.

Outside, Last Stop's barrier was up. Someone's new house, seven houses away, had a rope around it. The recording inside it waited, as recordings do, to be heard by the right ear.
