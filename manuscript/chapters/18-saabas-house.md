# Chapter 18 — Saaba's House

*Later — custody*

They told him about the house before they let him hear it.

The constable who brought him tea — not the interrogator with *fact* — mentioned it while placing the sachet, as if it were small talk required by the flat light.

“They search that seven-house house today,” he said, not looking at Kwame's face, looking at the cup. “Fine house, glass window. Land guard target because fresh. No fence finish. Police say recording dey.”

Kwame's hands, still cuffed loosely, made the same small sound on metal — wanting to be still.

“Recording?” he said. Two syllables. His voice still terse.

The constable shrugged, polite, the way Mallam Town people shrug when they have said more than they meant and less than they know.

“Phone record. Girl dey call before robbers enter. They find am.”

That was the plant — the existence, not the content — delivered secondhand, as the reworked single-POV rule required: Kwame does not witness the search. He hears about it and has to interpret it.

Later — afternoon, same flat light — a visitor came.

Not Nene this time. Ebo.

Old driver, senior council, twelve years' colleague, the man who had added ₵150 without counting and said *that be station*. He did not sit the way police sat. He sat with his knees a little open, hands on his thighs, looking at Kwame's beaten face and then at the table because looking too long felt like another interrogation.

“Station master,” Ebo said. His voice had the faint hoarseness of a man who had slept little since Day 2. “We hear from radio. Kasoa FM say at noon: recording found at Saaba house, seven houses from Last Stop. She dey phone before attack.”

Kwame waited. He knew Ebo would give it the way drivers give road news — not tidy, but accurate.

“Her father,” Ebo said, and looked up now. “She dey phone her father. Chief Inspector Nanfuri pikin. Nanfuri self dey phone when robbers break. Line cut.”

Kwame closed his eyes. That was the refinement locked 2026-09-15: ordinary call, daughter to father, *Daddy, you lock?* The recording at the house was that call — her side — ordinary evening, ordinary check-in, because fresh house, because father is strict, because a daughter in a new building calls the man who arrested people for a living to say *I dey house, I lock.* The kind of call that, later, would make the hearing of the background name devastating.

“They say the phone dey for speaker,” Ebo went on, because he had heard it from a policeman who had heard it from a policeman. “So house record am — not clean, but voice dey for back. Man talk for window, someone call name. Not Kwame name. Different name.”

That was the ledger's function: raise questions before answering. Voices, partial vehicle, someone using a name — not a clean confession. Enough for a lead, not enough to know whose network.

Kwame opened his eyes. The flat light stayed flat.

“Who name?” he asked.

Ebo shook his head. “We no hear proper. Police bring Chocho to hear am tomorrow, maybe. Chocho know voice — his work be land for Mallam, but not this car — they say outside group. But station man — ” He stopped, because he had been about to say *station man thought it was you because car be your own* and that sentence needed no finishing.

Ebo put his hand, briefly, on the table near Kwame's cuffed hand, not touching metal, just near.

“You no dey alone for this story,” he said, which was not *we believe you* but was, in Mallam Town, as close as a visitor in a police room could get without lying to a man who had two charges filed.

After Ebo left, the room went back to being a procedural diagram: outline uniform, hatching minimal, pots and stools given the same care as people. Kwame sat in it and pictured, without wanting to, the house seven houses away — white wall, unrendered low wall, glass catching sun — and a father on the other end of a line that cut.

He pictured Last Stop where his own car had been taken — reached-in ignition killing, wire snapping — and the short drive, seven houses, that made his car an easy tag for murder.

He did not yet know the name. But the name existed now, somewhere between a window and a phone that had been left on speaker, waiting for a land-guard leader to say *not my man.*

The constable returned with no tea, just a form.

“Sign,” he said.

Kwame signed with his left hand, cuffed. The officer wrote *informed of recording found at seven-house house — call with father — background voice*. The words looked bureaucratic. The call was not.
