# Chapter 21 — The Man in the Camry

*Flashback delivered as direct conversation — custody*

Nanfuri came without his hat.

That was how Kwame knew he had come as a father, not as Chief Inspector.

The constable opened the flat room's door — Style B's daylight, uniform outline, minimal hatching — and a man of fifty-seven with thirty years in service entered as a man enters a room where his daughter's name is also a file. He did not bring a file. He brought a small, tired way of standing that, at the station that afternoon, had been seen as *shaken and tight-lipped*.

Kwame stood. He had not stood for the other officers. For this one — because he was Saaba's father and because he had said *Carefully* at dusk when others would have said *After them* — he stood and put his palms briefly together, the small wai that had meant *thank you* and *I owe* with Kpakpo's ₵150.

Nanfuri looked at Kwame's face — beaten, healing, behind a door, behind a rope — and for a second his own face did the thing faces do when they have chosen restraint and then watched restraint cost.

“Station master,” he said. Clipped, as always under pressure. Then, quieter, not clipped: “Sit.”

They sat.

For a while Nanfuri murmured to himself, the way the dossier had said he did when alone and turning something over. Not prayer, not performance — a habit of a methodical man being careful with a memory that deserved dread in the telling, fully rational in content, but carried, correctly, as dread.

“You see barrier that afternoon,” Nanfuri said finally, looking not at Kwame but at the table's ordinary linework — pots and stools that were not there, given the same care as people because this book's art believed ordinary objects matter. “You see me chase.”

“I see Camry hit pole for Last Stop,” Kwame said. The junction name came out with the locked geography — *Last Stop and barrier same place*. “You drive after.”

Nanfuri nodded once. He was not asking to be believed. He was telling because Kwame, in custody for a car that had reached Saaba's seven-house house, deserved the other half of that road.

“Forty minutes,” Nanfuri said. Past tense, low, as if measuring. “Tar to gravel to packed earth. No street light. The Camry — tinted, no plate — he lean. Not to shoot, first. To photo.”

He made a gesture with his hand, not large, as if holding a phone out a window.

“Masked man lean out back window, phone. Take me. Not car. My face. Before he shoot. Both left tyres.”

Kwame felt, without wanting to, the Camry's window reaching past his own chest at midnight — *hand reaching past to kill ignition* — and understood the rhyme: reaching, photographing, recording — men who documented before they harmed, as if proof mattered more than the act.

“Pickup crash,” Nanfuri went on. Flat. “We no hurt bad, but car no waka. Camry go through cemetery gate. You know eastern gate? Crooked post. Tyre mark end inside.”

The gate east of Kwame's hill house. The iron that looked neglectful until you stood at it at dusk.

“I stand,” Nanfuri said. He swallowed. The next part was the part that, for forty minutes of pursuit and a whole book's structure, needed real space rather than summary — because per the single-POV decision, this was the only scene where the cemetery's dread would be told directly rather than gossiped about.

“Dusk,” he said. “Stillness. Not empty. Leaves no move but path dey cleared where path no be cleared before. Stash point — you see when you station master see queue form before it forms: small clearing, tyre track continue past where eye think it stop. Smell of man piss fresh where no grave be fresh. That stillness — it no be juju. My body talk true: *armed men just shoot tyre, they dey inside, you alone, no backup, no light.* So I do careful thing.”

He looked at Kwame then, direct.

“I step inside small, see, then I come back. I say to boys, ‘Carefully.’ We withdraw. Not because I fear. Because careful be work when you no get men.”

Kwame breathed. He had sat at Last Stop that night and chosen *I dey come* when duty called, which was also a careful thing that looked like courage. Nanfuri had stood at a gate that night and chosen *Carefully* when courage would have been going in alone. Two men who separately chose restraint that night, now finally speaking — the theme tracker had promised this would be the chapter where the motif pays.

Nanfuri's hands, on the table, did a thing Kwame had not seen police do — they loosened.

“Then night,” Nanfuri said, and his voice, for the first time, was not clipped or murmured but simply a father's, tired, fifty-seven trying not to crack the way Mansa had cracked on *answer me*.

“She call me. Saaba. My pikin. New house seven houses from your barrier. She say — you hear recording — ‘Daddy, you lock?’ Ordinary. I say ‘You lock proper.’ Then — wood. Window for kitchen side where wall low. Background — *Musah*.”

Kwame nodded. He had heard that *Musah — make Musah bring car comot* on the constable's phone copy.

“I hear — start — then line cut,” Nanfuri said. He did not elaborate the sound that made a line cut different from a hung-up line. He did not need to. “I call back. Phone ring, no answer. Not switched off — ring ring, then dead after. Like your Mansa phone. Impact.”

Kwame's throat made the shape again.

“I drive there,” Nanfuri said, flat again, reporting on himself. “Not with siren — with chest. House light on, window shift, car no dey — your car comot already through same Last Stop road, but I no know na your car that time. I knock, no answer, I break small — she down. Same wood sound still warm.”

He looked at the table's linework again, at the way a storybook style could make a room look safe while a man told its opposite.

“Recording — her phone dey speaker for counter, so house record am clear for back voice. My voice small. Musah clear. I hear live, but recording more clear. I bring Chocho to hear. He come quiet, with Lagos? No. With slippers quiet. He listen once.”

Nanfuri reproduced Chocho's transactional cadence, guarded, not performing redemption, self-interested and true:

“Chocho say: ‘No be my boy. Outside group. Different community. Musah be their driver — fresh house work. Not Mallam.’”

Kwame stared. The leader who had leaned out to photograph Nanfuri before shooting both left tyres, the man who had reached past his chest to kill his ignition at Last Stop, the voice that said *Musah bring car* behind glass — same network, different town.

“Why photo me?” Kwame asked, small.

Nanfuri's mouth did a brief, terrible thing that was not a smile.

“Because I be police for that barrier. Because land guard for outside want know which police dey disturb new house money. Because photo be threat. Because pothole for day you see, for night e find you. They find me by day.”

The two men sat in flat daylight that was not dramatic — no beam, no glow — while the memory being described was all Style A: high-contrast black, wedge light, dense hatching on foliage/brick/fabric, the cemetery's flat black sky directing the eye to a tyre track that continued past what should be visible.

Nanfuri, having told what he had correctly sensed and why he withdrew, having told that he was on the phone with his daughter as her window opened where it should not, did what Kwame, in Ch.26, would need to do with his own silence — break it deliberately, on the page, not because external events would later exonerate (Mansa's confession, Chocho's ID) but because speech itself was the climax.

“I choose careful at cemetery,” Nanfuri said, quiet, to Kwame, to himself, to the room that had heard many facts and few truths. “Careful cost my daughter. Your quiet cost your wife floor. We both keep peace wrong.”

Kwame, who had kept quiet at 11:42 long enough for noise to become *good for nothing*, who had not checked outside a window, who had refused even at gunpoint because he was looking for his wife, put his cuffed hands — lightly — on the table, not as a defendant but as a station master who knew how to keep a queue tight and how to let a man speak.

“Thank you for tell me, Commander,” he said. Terse. But this time, terse as true.

Nanfuri stood, hat still not on, and, for the first time, put his hand briefly on Kwame's shoulder the way he had put it on his own daughter's house wall after it was too late to keep the window from being opened.

“We go do arrest for that cemetery gate,” he said. “Tomorrow. With men. Not alone. You hear? — this time with men.”

Kwame heard. The loop that had started with a Camry vanishing through that gate, tyre track ending inside, would close there — not with a man alone saying *Carefully* and withdrawing, but with the same man backed up, returning, arresting at or returning to the same ground, this time daylight making potholes visible.

After Nanfuri left, Kwame sat in the room that now contained, for the first time in Act IV, not a charge but a father's grief, and understood why Nene, fixed anchor, did not need an arc — she was the steady thing they orbited while they learned to speak.
