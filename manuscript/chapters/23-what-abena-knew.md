# Chapter 23 — What Abena Knew

*Same period — custody, visiting room*

Abena did not sit the way Nene sat.

Nene had taken a block and made it a chair. Abena perched on the edge of the visiting-room stool as if it might be taken away, hands clasped around a small plastic bag that contained nothing anyone wanted — a sachet of water she had not offered, a folded cloth. She was nervous and deferential under authority — the two CID officers at the doorway — chatty and confident otherwise, but here there was no otherwise.

Kwame sat where Mansa had lain small and where Nanfuri had sat hatless. Flat light, uniform outline, minimal hatching. The room was storybook again, but not quiet. Abena was, for once, the noise.

She started before the constable said she could.

“Station master,” she said, quick, Ghanaian English with sentence-final *eehh* that usually came after a joke and now came after none. “You — you know I talk plenty? I be *Nkonkonsa*, everybody say — but this time I talk — I talk and —”

The constable at the doorway made a small sound — not *silence*, just *steady*. Abena nodded to him, as if he had forgiven her, and tried again, eyes on Kwame's cuffed hands rather than his face.

“That night — 8 p.m. after you come — Mansa call me for wall. She say you hide money, you hide woman, she say *good for nothing* but before that she say fear. I — I say —”

She swallowed. The next sentence was the beat the Chapter Outline had marked as locked: delivered as her own confession to Kwame directly, not as her own POV scene, not summarized, but said with setting and stakes.

“I say, ‘you still think I should follow him?’” Abena whispered. Exactly the line proposed months before this session, now canon, spoken not as a recommendation but as an admission. “She say *I think* — I say *if you no follow, how you go know? You for know, Kwame keep quiet, eehh, na so they be.* I say follow quiet through window so gate no screech, nobody for hear. I think say be advice. Small advice for salon. Not — ”

She did not finish *not to make a woman try a sill low, worn where hands grip, pawpaw leaves big as hands, ground closer than it looks by day.* She had learned, since the CID's first questioning at his house Day 1 morning when she had been nervous and deferential and answered *she even called him useless* because it was what the officer wrote, that small salon advice, treated as entertainment, had a real cost. She had carried private guilt the whole book — thin-walled guilt, compound-wide by now — which the reader may or may not have been shown early depending on draft, but Kwame now saw fully.

He looked at her. Not with the flat calm that had stopped a mob at the egg seller — that would have been distance. With the terse, checking kind, the same that checked a preacher's verses for accuracy rather than arguing.

“Why you tell am so?” he asked. Not accusing. Asking the mechanism. Because in Mallam Town gossip was not just talk, it was the second nervous system, the diffuse antagonist that filled silence left by absence and got it wrong, and he needed to hear how the current had entered his house.

Abena's hands tightened on the bag.

“Because I want story to carry for salon,” she said, honest, terrible, simple. “Mansa be friend, but *Nkonkonsa* be my name. I think — she follow, she see no woman, she come back laugh, I get story — *Mansa follow Kwame, see nothing*. Small. Not —” She gestured helplessly toward the ward where Mansa lay bandaged, toward the flat room where two charges sat, toward Saaba's seven-house house where a different crime had used his car and needed his silence to look like guilt.

“I no know window low,” Abena whispered. “I no know phone for hand.”

The two officers at the doorway did not write this. It was not a charge. It was character-level payoff, the Ledger's secondary thread where *what she knew and when* was tracked separately so she could eventually be the one who supplied a missing piece. She had supplied it — not a forensic piece, but a human one that made Mansa's *I fall* read as *I was told to fall* without being told to fall.

Kwame breathed. His ribs still made him careful.

“You tell officer?” he asked, still quiet.

Abena nodded, tears now without elongating spelling, without exclamation points — this scene had asked for low volume so that later, high-intensity beats (egg seller mob, arrest) retained impact.

“I tell today for hospital, before they bring you. I say — my mouth bring this. I say for record. They write. They say not charge — but they hear.”

She had confessed to him last, not first. That order mattered — first to Mansa, then to record, then to the man whose immovability had been read as guilt.

Kwame put his cuffed hands — lightly — on the edge of the table nearest her side, not reaching for her, just making a small line between them that was not noise.

“Thank you for talk true,” he said. Terse, but this time the terseness was not silence-as-peace. It was peace that had passed through speech.

Abena, who had enjoyed gossip as entertainment right up until it was someone else's ruin, and whose arc was instigator → guilt → the one who supplied the missing piece late, began to cry properly, not salon-crying, not performance.

“I go beg Mansa,” she whispered. “Every day.”

“You go beg,” Kwame said, simple, not as sentence but as permission. He did not absolve, because absolution would be noise. He allowed the next step, which was also a kind of peacemaking, but one that required words.

The constable at the doorway tapped his wrist — time.

Abena stood, still perched, then stood fully. She left the small plastic bag on the stool — water she had not offered, now a small apology.

“I dey go salon tell them — no story,” she said to the floor. “No *Nkonkonsa* for this. I tell them say follow advice no be play.”

After she left, Kwame sat with the two rooms in his mind: Mansa's *I fall* and Abena's *I told her to follow*. Together they cleared the window. Not the Saaba house — not yet — that needed Chocho's *outside group, not Mallam* and a cemetery gate with backup.

But this window — his own — now had its truth spoken, face-to-face, not gossiped.

That was the installment that let Ch.26 be Kwame's chapter specifically — the emotional climax distinct from mechanical unraveling — because Ch.23 had taken the interpersonal debt and made it speak.
