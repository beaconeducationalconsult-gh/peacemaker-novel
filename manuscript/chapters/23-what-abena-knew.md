# Chapter 23 — What Abena Knew

*Same period — custody, visiting room — flat light*

Abena did not sit the way Nene sat.

Nene had taken a block and made it a chair because a block, when you choose it, is a chair and because a fixed anchor does not need comfort to be steady. Abena perched on the edge of the visiting-room stool as if it might be taken away if she put her full weight on it, hands clasped around a small plastic bag that contained nothing anyone wanted — a sachet of water she had not offered at the hospital curtain where Mansa lay, a folded cloth that had been a hair cloth and was now just a cloth, a receipt for airtime she had bought and not used because gossip is also a kind of airtime.

She was nervous and deferential under authority — the two CID officers at the doorway who had questioned her Day 1 mid-morning at his house and to whom, nervous, she had said *she even called him useless* because the officer wrote and writing made it true — chatty and confident otherwise, bright at the salon where *Nkonkonsa* was not an accusation but a job description, *bring story*. But here there was no otherwise. Here there was a flat light room where her friend's husband sat with loose cuffs and a face that had been behind a half-wall and then behind a door.

Kwame sat where Mansa had lain small and where Nanfuri had sat hatless and where Ebo had sat knees open. Flat light, uniform outline, minimal hatching on the table's edge and the bag's plastic seam, storybook. The room was storybook again, but not quiet the way storybooks are quiet when children are sleeping. Abena was, for once, the noise — not PA, not barber, but human.

She started before the constable said she could, because beginning without permission is also a way of asking forgiveness for what will be said.

“Station master —” she said, quick, Ghanaian English with sentence-final *eehh* that usually came after a joke about a driver and now came after none and therefore sounded wrong, like an instrument tuned wrong. “You — you know I talk plenty? I be *Nkonkonsa*, everybody say — salon, kiosk — but this time I talk — I talk and — I —” Her hands tightened. Plastic crinkled.

The constable at the doorway made a small sound — not *silence*, not *stop*, just *steady*, the constable's hand on an elbow kind of sound — and Abena nodded to him, as if he had forgiven her for starting too fast, and tried again, eyes on Kwame's cuffed hands rather than his face because his face, healing, required more sentences than her mouth had ready.

“That night — 8 p.m. after you come for house — Mansa call me for wall. Thin wall, you know. She say you hide money, you hide woman, she say fear. She say *good for nothing* but before that at wall she say *Kwame keep quiet, so I no know.* I — I say —”

She swallowed. The building's air, flat and warm, made swallowing audible. The next sentence was the beat the Chapter Outline had marked as locked, delivered as her own confession to Kwame directly, not as her own POV scene, not summarized as *Abena advised*, but said with setting and stakes so it would land as a gut punch and so it would give Nanfuri's later *Carefully* a small rival: another place where careful was not chosen.

“I say, ‘you still think I should follow him?’” Abena whispered. Exactly the line proposed months before, now canon, spoken not as a recommendation on a salon floor but as an admission in a police room. Six words. In a flat room they were louder than shouting at the egg spill. “She say *I think* — small *I think* — I say *if you no follow, how you go know? You for know, Kwame keep quiet, eehh, na so they be — men keep quiet when they hide.* I say follow quiet through window so gate no screech, so nobody for hear door — eight rooms, thin wall, you lift gate — you know. I say *window, not gate*. I think say be advice. Small advice for salon tomorrow — *Mansa go see no woman, we laugh.* Not —”

She did not finish *not to make a woman try a sill low, worn where hands over years grip, pawpaw leaves big as hands, ground closer than it looks by day because at night drop finds you, phone in hand for torch, phone dead on impact not switched off.* She had learned, since the CID's first questioning at his house Day 1 mid-morning when the curtain moved and she had answered *she even called him useless* because it was what the officer wrote and because nervous makes you give the answer the room wants, that small salon advice — treated as entertainment, as *bring story* — had a real cost when the person you advise is thirty-eight and counting and afraid and precise. She had carried private guilt the whole book — thin-walled guilt, compound-wide by now, sleeping and waking — which the reader may or may not have been shown early depending on draft, but Kwame now saw fully because she had said it to him and not about him.

He looked at her.

Not with the flat calm that had stopped a mob at the egg seller — that would have been distance, and distance had already cost. With the terse, checking kind, the same that checked a preacher's Matthew 7:13 silently rather than arguing that holy is not fundraising. Terse that is now trying to be careful, not quiet.

“Why you tell am so?” he asked. Not accusing — if he had accused, she would have cried and the officers would have written *accuse* and the room would have made it procedure. Asking the mechanism. Because in Mallam Town gossip was not just talk — it was the second nervous system — kiosks, barbering salons, hairdressing salons, apketeshie bars, food joints — specific, named venues for how information (and misinformation) moves, and he needed to hear, from the mouth that was the node, how the current had entered his own house through concrete that was supposed to be wall but was thin as paper.

Abena's hands tightened until the plastic bag made a small sharp sound that the flat room made louder.

“Because I want story to carry for salon,” she said, honest, terrible, simple — the way Ebo said *that be station* simple. Honest the way Mansa's *five* in the ward was honest. “Mansa be friend, but *Nkonkonsa* be my name for town — my name make me come early give. I think — she follow, she see no woman, she see you count money for Last Stop wall, she come back laugh, we tell salon *Mansa follow Kwame, see nothing, Kwame count Bad Luck.* Small, funny. Not —” She gestured helplessly — a gesture that included, without pointing, three places: the ward where Mansa lay bandaged and said *I fall*, the flat room where two charges sat clipped together, the seven-house new house with glass windows where a daughter's ordinary call to her father had been cut and where his own Corolla had been left to tag him.

“I no know window low,” Abena whispered. Her voice, which at the salon could fill a shop, went very small, the way water goes small when you cup it. “I no know sill wear where hands grip. I no know pawpaw for ground — dark. I no know phone for hand — fall break.”

The two officers at the doorway did not write this. It was not a charge, not even a witness statement that a court would stamp, but character-level payoff, the Ledger's secondary thread where *what she knew and when* was tracked separately so she could eventually be the one who supplied a missing piece late. She had supplied it — not a forensic piece — a human piece that made Mansa's *I fall* read as *I was told to fall quiet through window* without being told to fall, which is also how gossip works: you are told in a way that lets you think you decided.

Kwame breathed. His ribs still made him careful on the left where boot and elbow had shared a spot. He breathed again, to make room for the next sentence, which had to be spoken even at cost — the book's thesis in a small room.

“You tell officer the wall talk?” he asked, still quiet, still checking.

Abena nodded, tears now without elongating spelling, without *Whaaaaaat* or *Stoooooppppp!!* — this scene had asked for low volume so that later, high-intensity beats — egg mob elongated, arrest shouting — retained their impact and so this low intensity could be more true than loud.

“I tell today for hospital, before they bring you for *I fall*. I say for record for woman police — my mouth bring this. I say *my mouth.* They write, they say not charge — but they hear. They say Mansa own *I fall* plus my *I advise window* make curtain not push. They write paper not handcuff.”

She had confessed in the correct order the dossier had noted that would make her arc sharper than if she had merely been a witness: first, private guilt from 8 p.m. onward, carried; second, to the doctor and to Mansa at the curtain's edge — *Abena, we need rest*; third, to the record at the hospital with the woman police who wrote not *Nkonkonsa* but *advice to follow via window to avoid gate noise*; fourth, last, to the man whose immovability had been read as guilt and who had not checked outside the window where she had told another woman to go.

Order matters in peacemaking. Abena had learned it late, but she had learned it in the right order.

Kwame put his cuffed hands — lightly, not to rattle — on the edge of the table nearest her side, not reaching for her hands, just making a small line between them that was not noise but was also not silence. A line that, in uniform outline, would be a single careful hatch mark among minimal hatch marks.

“Thank you for talk true,” he said. Terse, but this time the terseness was not silence-as-peace. It was peace that had passed through speech, on the page, where the outline had said Ch.23 should give its scene weight and not read as summary.

Abena, who had enjoyed gossip as entertainment right up until it was someone else's ruin and whose arc was instigator → guilt → the one who supplied the missing piece late, not just witness → reckoner, began to cry properly now — not salon-crying, not performance with eye to the shop door to see who entered, but ward-crying, ward after hearing *I fall*. Her shoulders, which at the salon were always straight, went small.

“I go beg Mansa — every day, morning, evening — I go bring soup, I go wash — I go —” she whispered, to him as if he were the priest, to the officers as if they were chiefs, to the flat room as if it were a small church.

“You go beg,” Kwame said, simple, not as sentence but as permission. He did not absolve — absolution would be noise and also not his to give alone; Mansa would give or not give. He allowed the next step, which was also a kind of peacemaking, but one that required words, repeated, at cost. “You go tell salon also — no story for this. Tell them follow advice no be play.”

The constable at the doorway — the same who had said *make way* at Nene's — tapped his wrist once — time, not hurry.

Abena stood, still perched half, then stood fully, as if full height would prove. She left the small plastic bag on the stool — water sachet she had not offered, now a small apology, now a relic: the small talk that does not get drunk. She pushed it slightly toward Kwame, not as gift but as *you drink*.

“I dey go salon now,” she said to the floor, to herself, to the other kiosks she would need to tell — barber Sule who held clippers, seamstress who made Mansa's church dress — “— tell them — no *Nkonkonsa* for window advice. I tell them say — phone dead no mean off, window low no mean push, gate quiet no mean peace.”

After she left — slippers, not quiet like Chocho's, ordinary hurried — Kwame sat with the two rooms in his mind: Mansa's ward white curtains *I fall — my fault, I do* and Abena's visiting-room stool *I told her to follow — my mouth*. Together they cleared the window that a constable would later underline. Not the Saaba house window — not yet — that needed Chocho's *outside group, not Mallam* and a cemetery gate with backup and a pale wedge of sun.

But his own window — his own house's low sill where a curtain had moved with a draft he had tugged irritably — now had its truth spoken, face-to-face, not gossiped through kiosks, not *she even called him useless* but *she followed advice quietly so gate would not screech*.

That was the installment the outline had said would let Ch.26 be Kwame's chapter specifically — the emotional/thematic climax distinct from mechanical unraveling of the case — because Ch.23 had taken the interpersonal debt and made it speak at low volume, carefully, so that when Kwame later let his own gate screech on purpose, it would be heard as choice, not noise.

He drank the sachet.
