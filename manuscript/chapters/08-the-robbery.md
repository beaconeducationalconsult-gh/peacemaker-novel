# Chapter 8 — The Robbery

*Night, en route to/from Last Stop — Day 0, ~12:25 a.m.*

He had the car on the lane before he had the reason ready.

The Corolla started as it always did — cough, then hold — headlights making that pale wedge that was Style A's whole grammar: white linework carved out of near-solid black, a single cone cutting dark, foliage/brick rendered in dense cross-hatching where the light hit, everything else flat black to direct the eye. At night Mallam Town became scratchboard. You saw only what your light allowed, and what it allowed was always smaller than the dark around it.

Kwame drove the short distance from his compound toward Last Stop — the same junction whose barrier had been broken at four p.m., same wall he had sat on at midnight — with the sales pouch on the passenger seat and Mansa's unreachable number still lit on his phone. He was going to pick up stranded passengers. That was the story he had told himself to make moving feel like peacemaking. In the ledger, this was the mechanism: not a theft from an unconscious man, but a staged carjacking that escalated because he, true to character, refused even at gunpoint.

The lane joined the main road at Last Stop. The police post bulb still buzzed, still threw its thin light that did not reach the road. Beyond it, seven houses down, Saaba's new house glowed — the warm, ordinary light he had seen at six p.m., still on. In a few minutes that light would still be on when an unmarked, tinted car without plates turned off the lane and stopped outside it. Kwame did not know that. He was looking at the road.

Three figures stood at the edge where the tar thinned to gravel — where the station's dust became the road out toward the cemetery on one fork and toward the uncompleted buildings on the other.

Two men and a woman.

The woman held a small bag. The taller man held a phone whose torch was not on. The shorter, thicker man — who later, on a recording found at Saaba's house, would be the background voice that let slip a name — held nothing in his hands that could be seen. They flagged him the way people flag taxis in Mallam Town at night — not urgently, palm out, small.

Kwame slowed because habit slowed him. The station master's car, after midnight, was still a station's car. He wound the passenger window down a hand's breadth, not all the way. Cold air came with dust.

“Charlie, you dey go station?” the tall man said. His voice was even, polite. Not threatening yet. “We go Lapaz side. You fit carry we?”

Kwame looked past them to where Yaw had said passengers would be — further toward the station, not here at the bush edge. He thought of Mansa's empty room, of the window, of the phone that was dead and therefore, in his translation, off.

“I dey look for my wife,” he said. It was the first plain refusal. Short, terse, not explaining beyond what needed to be known. Same flat that had stopped Kofi's elbow that morning.

“We go pay extra,” the woman said. She had a soft voice, almost apologetic. She lifted her bag slightly, as if it contained something that made *extra* specific. “Add ₵50. For night.”

An extra fifty would have made his pouch ₵345 — more than the ₵300, more than the story he needed to tell Mansa to make three sentences easy. At noon, at the station, an extra fifty at night was a good fare. At midnight, with his room empty, it was a wrong price.

“I no dey go Lapaz now,” Kwame said. Still quiet. Second refusal. He did not check his phone. He did not say stranded passengers. He kept his hands on the wheel.

The short, thicker man shifted his weight. He was the leader. He had been the masked man who leaned out of the Camry that afternoon to photograph Nanfuri before shooting both left tyres — though Kwame did not know that, and would not know it until Nanfuri told him face-to-face in a custody room weeks later, in Ch.21, what that photo had meant. Now the mask was gone. His face was ordinary, which is how the worst faces look before they do what they will do.

“Charlie,” he said, and his voice was still almost friendly, “you go Lapaz or no?”

Kwame looked at him, then at the woman, then back at the road that should have had stranded passengers and did not. He said, “No.”

The leader's hand moved without hurry to his waist and came back with a thing Kwame had not seen that close before outside of a police post. A pistol, small, black, flat black that swallowed light.

He did not raise it high. He held it low, along the door, angled up.

“Come down, Chairman,” he said. The title was mock respectful. “Make we talk for road.”

Everything did what time does in those moments — it did not slow so much as thin, so that there were fewer sounds and each had edges. The barber's transcription radio, off. The police bulb, buzzing. The pawpaw leaves behind his house, still moving somewhere east but not here. The headlight wedge on gravel showing each stone like a separate line drawing — dense hatching on texture, sparse on open ground.

Kwame's hands stayed on the wheel.

This was the beat to dramatize, because it was the same immovability as the egg-seller scene turned inside out. That morning, refusing to escalate had made him beloved. That morning, refusing to explain had been peace. Now the same flat refusal, at gunpoint, for his wife's sake, would get him nearly killed.

“I tell you,” Kwame said, quieter, not louder — which is what made the woman glance at the leader, small and quick — “I dey find my wife. I no dey carry.”

The leader did not argue. He leaned in.

What happened next happened through the driver's-side window that was open only a hand's breadth — a small, practical obstacle the leader solved the way a man solves a door he was told not to open: he reached past Kwame, across his chest, to the ignition and killed it. The engine shuddered and stopped. The pale wedge died. The black came forward in that way flat black does when a light source is removed — total, unhatched.

The air that came through the window was now the air of the man's sleeve, of gun oil, of the woman's bag close behind.

The leader gripped the door handle from outside — impossible angle, practiced — and wrenched. The Corolla's old door, fixed that morning with wire, held a half-second and then gave with a sound that was also the sound of wire snapping.

He dragged Kwame out by the front of his shirt.

Kwame did not let go of the wheel at first. That was not courage. It was the same physical obedience to stillness that made him muffle a gate every night. Then his hands were not on a wheel but on a shirt, on a wrist, on gravel.

He went down on the gravel at Last Stop — the same junction where he had counted ₵285 that afternoon and where, in daylight, you could see potholes and swerve.

They beat him there.

Not efficiently. The woman said “Make we take car, leave am!” once, early, and the tall man kept watch toward the station where no one was coming because the stranded passengers had been a call that, later, would not be traced. The leader did most of it, with a short, economical anger, as if Kwame's refusal had insulted a transaction. A foot, a pistol's side — not a shot, there were police within three hundred meters and they had already shot tyres that day — a knee when Kwame curled.

Kwame did not shout. He made the sound a man makes when quiet is no longer a choice but a throat. He did not beg. He thought, once, very clearly: *Mansa's phone* — not Mansa, the phone — as if the object were the person, dead not off.

They dragged him off the road.

The uncompleted building was not the cemetery. That matters and must be kept separate. The cemetery east of his hill house was the outside crew's operational staging ground with cleared paths and a stash point behind a crooked iron gate — the site of the eventual arrest with backup. The uncompleted building where they dumped him was the generic notorious site surrounded by bush near Nene's hut — known at the station as where hoodlums and drug peddlers went, not cleared, not operational, just unfinished walls with rebar like fingers.

They dragged him through bush that scratched. Dense cross-hatching on foliage, white lines on black where moon found leaf edge. The building's doorway had no door. Inside, the floor was sand and sachet water bags and broken blocks.

They left him behind a half-wall. One of them — the tall man, perhaps, Kwame saw only trousers and slippers at that height — took his phone when it lit in the sand with Yaw's name. Looked, cracked the screen with a thumb or a heel, put it back on his chest as if returning something borrowed, and went.

The Corolla started on the second try — his car, fourteen years old, washed every Sunday — and turned not toward Lapaz but toward the seven-house stretch. Kwame heard it go, because hearing was the last sense to leave when the rest had been beaten quiet.

He lay in the uncompleted building, phone dead-cracked on his chest, sales pouch gone with the car (₵295 plus ₵20 parish offering he had meant to give Sunday — now just money for men who thought houses without papers were houses without owners). Blood in his mouth that tasted like coins. The night, which by day you could navigate, had found him.

• • •

*Seven houses away, a phone rings — not Kwame's. A girl in a new house, glass windows still warm, picks up to her father. “Daddy?” Saaba says. Chief Inspector Nanfuri, fifty-seven, thirty years in service, answers from wherever he is after the chase — “Saaba, you lock?” The recording device at her house, ordinary — a phone left on speaker — captures her reaction and, behind her, a man's voice through a window that should not be opening, letting slip a name that will not mean anything until Chocho hears it.*

*Kwame, meanwhile, lies overnight where the Manor — the town — said was notorious, until Nene, out gathering herbs near the bush at evening the next day, finds a beaten man where a beaten man was expected to be, and motions him to stay quiet.*
