# Chapter 16 — The Arrest

*Day 2, daytime — the road back, the station*

They did not let him walk alone.

The rope at his wrists was held by a boy in front and a boy behind, as if a man with cracked ribs and a moved tooth might run the way a Camry had run through a barrier at four p.m. two days earlier. The crowd that had formed at Nene's — last house no longer last — became a line that became a circle that moved.

Between Nene's hut and the road, the ground was bush and then packed earth and then, after a short stretch that the map kept distinct from the cemetery's cleared paths, the tar. Kwame's hill house was twenty-six minutes by car east; they did not take him there. They took him toward the Mallam road, toward Last Stop, toward the police post whose single bulb still buzzed though daylight made it useless.

Someone shouted. Someone else answered with jubilation that was not justice but relief — the relief of a manhunt that had paused at dark and postponed to morning now finding, behind a door, what it had been told to find. A woman clapped. A boy filmed on a phone that recorded only shouting. Another boy, trying to be helpful, kicked Kwame's shin as he walked, not hard enough to be called beating, hard enough to be called *mob* when listed later.

Kwame did not shout. He had not shouted at the okada crowd, at the robbers' pistol, at Mansa's *good for nothing*. Would shouting now help? He had learned, at the station, that silence could be peace and, at home, that silence could be distance, but here silence was simply silence being taken as consent. The town's noise filled the vacuum his absence had left, and now his presence, tied, filled it with more noise that needed no answer.

At the post, a constable took the rope. Not roughly. Formally. The same formal that makes a rope become *handcuffs* in a report.

“Make way,” the constable said, to the crowd more than to Kwame. “Make way, make way!”

A car — not his Corolla, which seven houses away had already been taken as evidence from Saaba's house where it had been left after the robbery — but a police pickup with both left tyres now repaired, waited. Kwame was put in the back with the constable who had kicked not kicking now, hands still tied before him, dust on his lip again redistributed by sweat.

Nene stood at the edge of the crowd, small, not following. She did not wave. She watched the way fixed anchors watch: evidence that she had not moved.

• • •

The police station was not far but the drive felt long because every kiosk, barbering salon, hairdressing salon, apketeshie bar stopped to watch the pickup pass. Kiosks that had said *he pushed her over another woman* and salons that had said *she pushed him to prove himself and it backfired* now said, with equal certainty, *he was in Nene's hut — herbs woman hide am*.

At the station, the procedures had a rhythm that, in another life, Kwame would have admired for its queue-keeping.

Name. Age. Address — hill, eight rooms, metal gate that screeched unless you lifted. Station master — twelve years — elder. He gave each answer short. He did not explain. The officer writing — also short with him, not unkind, just tired — wrote *Kwame Asante, 44* without looking up to see whether 44 looked beaten.

“Charge,” the officer said after a while, to another officer, not to Kwame, though Kwame heard it the way you hear thin walls.

Two charges.

One: attempted murder — Mansa, found below pawpaw tree outside open window, faint pulse, curtain draft, neighbour testimony: *she even called him useless* — full red herring stack built, each plant reading as evidence *he pushed her*.

Two: murder — Saaba, Chief Inspector Nanfuri's daughter, newest house seven houses from Last Stop, Kwame's Corolla at scene, robbery. Personal motive assumed (jealousy, debt, targeting policeman's family) until opportunistic-crime truth surfaced later via recording.

The officer turned the paper to Kwame.

“You get something to talk?”

Kwame looked at the paper. He could read what it did not say. That his refusal to speak at 11:42 had been heard by the compound as leaving, that his phone being dead had been read as being off deliberately, that his disappearance combined with argument read as flight, that his car at a murder read as intention.

He wanted the words to be three sentences again — *I borrow for eggs, Mansa fall on advice to follow, robbers steal car at Last Stop, I refuse at gun* — but three sentences, in a station, are not three sentences. They are a statement that will be underlined and copied and told at kiosks before it is believed.

“I no do am,” he said. It was the least noise he could make and the most true.

“Which one?” the officer asked, pen poised.

“Both,” Kwame said.

The officer wrote it. *Claims innocence.*

A handcuff replaced rope. Metal, more honest about what it was.

Kwame sat in the daylight procedural room — Style B's flat even daylight with no directional shadow, clean black outline on white, minimal hatching for shading, storybook linework on the table's ordinary objects — and waited for the next chapter, which he would not witness but would learn about through visitors, because single POV means the accused man learns what's happening to him rather than watching procedure.

Outside, the town told its story again, louder now that it had a man in cuffs to attach it to. The station's ordinary linework kept being careful and economical — bowls, stools, baskets — while the man's immovability, which had made him beloved as a peacemaker, sat in a chair and read as guilt.

Act III closed not with a reveal but with a filing: two charges, one body healing in a ward where a woman would later wake and say *I fell*, one recording seven houses away waiting to be heard properly, one cemetery gate east of his house waiting for backup that Nanfuri, having once said *Carefully* and withdrawn alone, would later lead with men.
