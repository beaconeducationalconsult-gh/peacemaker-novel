# Chapter 16 — The Arrest

*Day 2, daytime — the road back, the station*

They did not let him walk alone.

The rope at his wrists was held by a boy in front and a boy behind, as if a man with cracked ribs, a moved tooth, and an eye that still opened only after being called twice might run the way a tinted, no-plate Toyota Camry had run through a barrier pole at four p.m. two days earlier — hollow *thwack*, striped pole swinging, then dust.

The crowd that had formed at Nene's — last house no longer last — became a line that became a circle that moved. That is how jubilation moves in Mallam Town when a manhunt that paused for poor visibility at dark and postponed to morning finds, behind a door, a man where a man was expected to be. Not justice as procedure — relief as ending.

Between Nene's hut and the road, the ground was bush and then packed earth and then, after a short stretch that the map kept distinct — and this distinction the bible had locked and now the prose had to protect — from the cemetery's cleared paths, stash point, crooked iron gate east of his hill house — the tar. The tar was where the station's seven queues made sense and where, in daylight, you could see potholes and swerve and therefore believe in order.

Kwame's hill house was twenty-six minutes by car east, through that tar; they did not take him there first, though his house still had a rope around its pawpaw tree and a window whose curtain still moved. They took him toward the Mallam road, toward Last Stop, toward the police post whose single bulb still buzzed over a desk though daylight made it useless — the same post whose barrier a Camry broke and whose post a commander left in a pickup with both left tyres later shot.

Someone shouted. Not a word. That sound a crowd makes when it has been given a verb. Someone else answered with jubilation that was not justice but relief — the relief of many feet that had walked building to building since 4:30 yesterday with *uncompleted side* now finding, behind a door, what they had been told to find so they could stop walking.

A woman clapped — one clap, then embarrassed of the one, another. A boy filmed on a phone that recorded only shouting and would later be told *stop filming, make way*. Another boy, trying to be helpful in the way helpful becomes harmful in crowds, kicked Kwame's shin as he walked — not hard enough to be called beating in court, hard enough to be called *mob* when listed later under *circumstances of arrest*.

Kwame did not shout.

He had not shouted at the okada crowd when Kofi raised an elbow. He had not shouted when a pistol held low along his door at Last Stop and a hand reached past his chest to kill his ignition and wire snapped. He had not shouted at Mansa's *good for nothing* when *answer me* cracked and became *good for nothing* because fear had turned. Would shouting now help? He had learned, at the station at seven a.m., that silence could be peace — the way he had stood between an elbow and a boy's face and said *leave am* quietly and been obeyed. He had learned, at home at 11:42, that silence could be distance — Mansa hearing quiet as *you be nothing*. Here silence was simply silence being taken as consent, as the town's noise filled the vacuum his absence had left, and now his presence, tied, filled it with more noise that needed no answer and would get it anyway.

At the post, a constable took the rope.

Not roughly. Formally. The same formal that makes a rope become *handcuffs* in a report, that makes *we find you behind door* become *apprehended*. The constable was the same young man who had straightened the barrier pole after the Camry hit it; his fanning notebook was gone, replaced by a radio.

“Make way,” the constable said, to the crowd more than to Kwame, though Kwame heard it the way you hear thin walls in eight rooms — everybody heard, nobody answered separately. “Make way, make way!”

He said it twice more. The crowd, which had made a circle, made a line.

A car — not his Corolla, which seven houses away had already been taken as evidence from Saaba's house where it had been left after the robbery with its wire handle still snapped and its sachet water dust still on the floor mats — but a police pickup with both left tyres now repaired (new rubber, not retread) waited, engine running with the particular unevenness of a car that had been driven on rims.

Kwame was put in the back, not the cab, with the constable who had kicked not kicking now — crowds do that, they borrow and return meanness quickly — hands still tied before him, not behind, dust on his lip again redistributed by sweat that made the line from nostril more honest.

Nene stood at the edge of the crowd, small, not following. She had been the wall between doorway wedge and search at 4:40; now the doorway was empty and the wedge was sun, not search. She did not wave. She watched the way fixed anchors watch: evidence that she had not moved, that her herbs still hung, that her hut was still last house, that her *you no enter* had been overruled by a rope but not by forgetting.

The boy who had shouted *HE DEY!* now shouted *we catch am!* as if catching were hunting, not finding.

The pickup moved.

• • •

The police station was not far — three minutes the way the station measures, but the drive felt long because every kiosk, barbering salon, hairdressing salon, apketeshie bar, food joint stopped to watch the pickup pass and to add a sentence to the story it had already told at midday with two competing endings.

Kiosks that had said *he pushed her over another woman for that seven-house house* and salons that had said *she push you to show you be man and it backfire* now said, with equal certainty and slightly louder because a man in cuffs is a better anchor for a story than a curtain draft, *he was in Nene's hut — herbs woman hide am — see, rope.*

He was not in Nene's hut because Nene hid him as an accomplice; he was in Nene's hut because Nene said *stay* at 5:40 behind a half-wall. The story did not need that distinction to travel.

At the station, the procedures had a rhythm that, in another life, Kwame would have admired for its queue-keeping. He was station master for twelve years precisely because he admired queues — seven destination signs, order without shouting.

Name. He said it. *Kwame Asante*. Forty-four. The officer writing — also short with him, not unkind, just tired from Day 1 morning when two officers had followed drivers twenty-six minutes to his hill house and found a curtain moving — wrote *Kwame Asante, 44* without looking up to see whether 44 looked beaten or whether *Asante* was the surname that had appeared only once in the draft's Chapter 1 and now had to stay consistent.

Address — hill, outskirts, eight single-room self-contained, metal gate that screeched unless you lifted your palm, cemetery east, one lane, pawpaw tree. Station master — twelve years — driver — elder at Church of Christ. He gave each answer short. He did not explain, because explaining in a station is not three sentences about eggs and is instead a statement that will be underlined and copied and told at kiosks before it is believed.

“Charge,” the officer said after a while, to another officer, not to Kwame, though Kwame heard it the way you hear thin walls — everybody heard, nobody was meant to answer separately.

Two charges.

One: attempted murder — Mansa, thirty-eight, found below pawpaw tree outside open window after cool draft moved curtain, faint pulse, rope barricade, ambulance. Neighbour testimony: *she even called him useless* (Abena, nervous under authority, chatty otherwise). Red herring stack built, each plant reading as evidence *he pushed her* though true answer was *she tried to follow after Abena's advice, you still think I should follow him? if you no follow how you go know?* and phone dead from impact not switched off.

Two: murder — Saaba, Chief Inspector Nanfuri's daughter, newest/unregistered house seven houses from Last Stop, fresh paint, low wall no render — Kwame's Corolla, fourteen years, wire-fixed handle now snapped, at scene. Robbery. Personal motive assumed (jealousy over Nanfuri, debt ₵300, fresh house = rich pikin) until opportunistic-crime truth surfaced later via recording's *Musah bring car comot* and Chocho's *not my boy — outside group*.

The officer turned the paper — two sheets, clipped — to Kwame, the way a driver turns a destination sign to a passenger — *this is where we say you go*.

“You get something to talk?” he asked. Pen poised. The linework on his pen was careful, economical, uniform — Style B's minimal hatching for shading on white, no solid fills, no beam.

Kwame looked at the paper. He could read what it did not say and what it would therefore make others say when filed.

That his refusal to speak at 11:42 had been heard by the compound as *leaving* and therefore as *going*.

That his phone being dead — impact, not off — had been read as being off deliberately to punish.

That his disappearance — overnight until 4:15, uncompleted building, then behind door — combined with argument read as flight, and flight reads as guilt when a town's second nervous system has two stories and needs a third.

That his car at a murder — stolen at Last Stop after refusing even at gun, wire snapped, seven-house drive — read as *he drove his own car to kill* when the car's age and washing every Sunday said *he never lent*.

He wanted the words to be three sentences again — *I borrow ₵300 for Madam eggs, Mansa fall on advice to follow through window, robbers steal car at Last Stop, I refuse at gun* — but three sentences, in a station where thin-walled arguments become statements, are not three sentences. They are a statement that will be underlined and copied and told at kiosks before it is believed, and he was tired of being underlying.

So he said what was least noise and most true — terse, checking, not explaining beyond what needed to be known, still the same voice that checked verses silently:

“I no do am,” he said. It was the least noise he could make and the most true. Not loud, not elongated, not *Stoooooppppp!!* — that device, the Style Guide said, was for high-intensity beats and had been used for the egg mob, the argument's peak, and would be used again at standoff. This needed low volume so that later high intensity retained its impact and so that this low intensity could be more true.

“Which one?” the officer asked, pen poised, not as trap but as requirement: two charges need two answers, even if one man is both.

“Both,” Kwame said. Two syllables.

The officer wrote it. *Claims innocence — both counts. Maintains carjacking at Last Stop/junction, 3 persons (2M1F), outside window not checked.*

A handcuff replaced rope. Not dramatically swapped — just rope untied, metal clicked, honest about what it was: cuff, not rope that pretends to be help.

The rope, on the table for a second, looked small.

Kwame sat in the daylight procedural room — Style B's flat even daylight with no directional shadow modeling, clean black outline on white background, uniform line weight throughout, minimal sparse hatching used only for shading on the table's edge and on the pen, no solid black fills, quiet observational framing — and waited for the next chapter, which, per the locked single-POV decision, he would not witness but would learn about through visitors, because single POV means the accused man learns what's happening to him rather than watching procedure. That trade, the outline had said, was honest but thin for Act IV unless visitors enriched it — which they would: Ebo with recording news, Chocho with Musah, Nanfuri with cemetery.

Outside, beyond the storybook outline, the town told its story again, louder now that it had a man in cuffs to attach it to and a rope that had been a handcuff to show. Kiosks that had said *he push* and salons that had said *she push* now said, with equal certainty, *he done, we catch*. The station's ordinary linework kept being careful and economical — bowls, stools, baskets, basins — while the man's immovability, which had made him beloved as a peacemaker at seven a.m. with a Madam's eggs, sat in a chair at two p.m. with a stamp and read as guilt to the town that preferred noise.

Act III closed not with a reveal but with a filing: two charges, one body healing in a ward where a woman would later wake at Kwame's breaking point near arraignment and say *I fell — on advice*, one recording seven houses away waiting to be heard properly by the right ear that would say *not my boy*, one cemetery gate east of his hill house — crooked, neglected — waiting for backup that Nanfuri, having once said *Carefully* and withdrawn alone at dusk rather than go further against armed men who shot tyres, would later lead with men at daylight, closing the structural loop back to Day 0.
