# Chapter 17 — Two Charges

*Day 2 onward — custody, interrogation — flat light*

The room where they laid the case had flat light.

Style B: clean daylight, no beam. White walls, black outline uniform, minimal hatching on the table's edge and on the pen the officer held, no solid fills, no directional shadows, careful economical linework on ordinary objects — sachet water, stamp pad — as if the room were a storybook coloring page left in sun too long. A storybook room for a story that was not a story but a filing, which was why the filing felt so tidy and so wrong.

Kwame sat where he had been put the previous day, after rope became handcuffs became a ring to the table. His lip had been cleaned, not stitched — that would have been a statement they did not want to make on a face they might need to photograph. His eye opened more than it had behind the half-wall at 4:15, but not fully. His ribs still made breathing a counted thing — one for in, two for hold, one for out, the way Nene had counted for him with bread the previous evening. Someone had given him water in a sachet, not a bottle — sachet is cheaper, but also in custody sachet is safer because it cannot be thrown — and he had drunk it without Nene there to hold it, hands shaking slightly around the plastic, metal of the ring tapping his wrist bone.

The officer across the table was not the one who had written the first statement at the barrier. That one had been young, still fanning a notebook. This one was older, mid-fifties, not Nanfuri — Nanfuri, whose daughter's name was now the other charge, would not be in this room yet because procedure kept fathers from interrogating their daughters' files, but also because grief kept them, and the book had decided Nanfuri would enter only in Ch.21 with hat in hands — but a CID man from town who used the word *fact* the way preachers on the junction used *holy* — to start sentences that wanted belief without offering proof.

“Fact,” he said, tapping the first paper with a finger that had a nail cut too short, “argument for night. Whole compound hear. ‘Good for nothing’ — neighbour hear, we write, she write too — Abena. Thin wall, eight rooms, everybody hear door. You leave. Gate no screech — you lift am, we hear you lift am every morning from hill, policeman for post hear too. You leave 11:42. Correct?”

Kwame did not answer immediately. He was listening for where the facts would turn into a story, the way you listen at a station when a queue forms correctly behind the wrong destination sign — everything looks right until you read the sign.

“Fact two,” the officer said, turning a page so Kwame could see the drawing — not a drawing, a photo taken the next morning through the window that he had not looked beyond at 12:18. White curtain, hemmed by Mansa, sill low, pawpaw leaves big as hands, ground where a woman had lain at the tree's base, faint depression in sand. On the back someone had written *Mansa — pawpaw base — window — curtain — phone*. “Window open, curtain blow, Mansa for ground. Phone off — or you say dead?” He looked up, not hostile, procedural. “You search kitchen, washroom, main room. You search compound but not outside window. Why you no check window, Mr. Asante? Station master, you know every corner of station, but you no know your own wall outside?”

The answer — *I think say she walk through door like people who want compound to hear door slam, not window like thief, and I think phone off because she vex, not dead because fall* — sat in his mouth. It needed three sentences: *I call, dead; I think switched off because anger; I no check window because window be for watch not for exit.* Three sentences, but three sentences require speaking, and speaking, at 11:42, had felt like adding noise to noise, and now, in a flat room, not speaking read as *unwilling*.

He said, instead, the smaller true: “I call phone — dead. I hear — nothing.”

“Dead,” the officer repeated, not doubting the word but writing the doubt around it. He wrote *claims phone dead from fall (impact) not switched off* in his careful hand that made even cots look neat, but said aloud, to fill the silence the town had been filling since Day 1 midday with two conspiracy theories, “Or switched off because she vex? You vex her for money? ₵300?” He let the number sit. ₵300 — the borrowed from Kpakpo/Ebo for thirty eggs, the same amount the madam counted note by note while a crowd watched. “Which be true — dead or off? Both make you no find am, but one make you look, other make you go.”

Which was exactly the red herring the Ledger had planned: unreachable reads as deliberate, phone off reads as flight.

Second charge.

The officer slid a second file, thinner, darker at the edge where a hand had held it often. No photo on top yet — photos belonged to Saaba's House chapter — just a receipt for a car and a receipt for a recording.

“Fact,” he said again, because *fact* was his holy. “Your car — Toyota Corolla, fourteen years, wire for handle you fix Monday dawn — find for that seven-house new house. Seven houses from Last Stop, same road Camry use, glass windows, fine girl. Saaba. Chief Inspector Nanfuri pikin. We find am dead for floor, phone record dey for counter — speaker.”

He did not play the recording yet. That belonged to the next chapter, to Saaba's House, to *Daddy you lock?* and *Musah bring car*. He let the car be the villain for now, because cars are easier than people to photograph, easier to attach.

“Witness see your car break barrier that afternoon? No — Camry break barrier, you see. But your car reach there night same road, same dust. People know say new house, fresh paper no dey — Sule barber talk am for station: *new house, no wall finish — na invitation* — land guard see invitation quick. So motive — town make two — ” He held up one finger, chipped, then two.

“One: you target rich police pikin because you owe — ₵300, we hear — so you need quick money, you rob. Two: you target Nanfuri because he commander, you know barrier, you vex for afternoon police waka.” He looked at Kwame's face — beaten, but not the face of a robber, which is also how robbers sometimes look — and did not choose between them.

“Which be true?” the officer asked, as if giving him a choice between two wrong things, as if *which motive* were a question and not a trap.

Kwame thought of that morning at six — dust to the knees, preacher checking Matthew 7:13, the Madam's thirty eggs bright as paint on tar, Kofi's elbow hitting his forearm, ₵150 and ₵150 borrowed, the small wai of both hands meaning *this now be my debt*, the way he had said *I go pay* to Ebo and meant it as a promise to Mansa even though Mansa was not there to hear it. He thought of Last Stop at midnight, gravel edge where three people stood where he had stood at midday: *You go Lapaz or no?* Extra ₵50 — which would have made his pouch ₵345 and therefore made three sentences affordable — pistol low along door angled up, the hand reaching past his chest — across that same chest where an elder's Bible ribbon held Matthew 5 — to kill ignition, wire snapping that Monday dawn fix, gravel on cheek at the junction where he had counted ₵285 and where barrier had been hit. He thought of the uncompleted building, distinct — as the bible now required him to keep — from the cemetery's cleared paths/host point/crooked iron gate east of his hill house — that distinction between notorious bush site and operational staging ground mattered because one meant *hoodlums* and the other meant *land guard outside group* — and of Nene's hand on his wrist saying *water first*.

He wanted to say *car steal* — two words that should be enough — and then add the many words that would make two words believable in a town where many cars are lent and many mothers name sons Musah. He knew, from the station, that two words without the many words reads as excuse the way *small thing* had read as hiding. So he said the least that was not silence, which at 11:42 had already proved dangerous if it stayed least:

“They steal car for Last Stop, for junction,” he said. Voice terse, not explaining beyond what needed to be known, even now the same cadence that checked verses silently. “I refuse carry, even at gun. They thief am — drag, beat, take. Go seven houses.”

The officer wrote: *claims carjacking at Last Stop/junction — refused twice inc. at gunpoint — dragged/beaten — dumped at uncompleted (bush, not cemetery) — vehicle driven 7 houses to Saaba house.*

That was a lot for a terse man's sentence. The officer looked up, pen still.

“Who be ‘they’?”

That was the question the recording would answer — background voice *Musah*, name slipped, Chocho saying *outside group, not Mallam* — but that recording was still seven houses away in a house where a phone had been left on speaker and a father had heard *Saaba! Saaba!* before cut, not yet played in this flat room. Not here. Not yet.

Kwame did not know their names the way the roster would know. He knew their shape the way a station master knows cars: tall man with phone torch off, woman with bag soft *fast, fast*, leader thick, masked that afternoon at barrier before photographing Nanfuri before shooting both left tyres. He knew their method: ask polite, offer extra, threaten low, reach — same reach as Camry lean.

“Three,” he said. “Two man, one woman.”

The officer nodded without surprise. Three had been said before by other drivers about other robberies — three was the number the town liked because it had a woman and therefore gossip. Three was not yet a clue, only a count.

“Fact three,” the officer said, tapping both files together so two charges became one manila stack, making one case of two stories tied with a metal ring to the table. “You missing overnight, till 4:15 next day for uncompleted building. Town search building to building 4:30, Chocho help organize, you hide for Nene hut — last house. Why hide if no do? Why Nene? Why not hospital? Why rope tie when we find?”

Why hide. Because bleeding. Because the town had already decided at midday — kiosk, salon, barber, apketeshie bar — *he push*, *she push*, *he run so he guilty*. Because Nene had said *stay* at 5:40 behind a half-wall and put her body between doorway wedge and search — fixed anchor, no arc. Because missing reads as flight when quiet reads as guilt and when a gate that did not screech proves you lifted it and therefore you left carefully, which reads as planned.

Kwame looked at the flat light, the uniform outline, the careful economical linework on the water sachet's plastic seam, on the stamp pad's felt, on the officer's pen, and said what he had said the previous day and would need to say again until a father's *on phone when she cut* and a land-guard's *not my boy* and a wife's *I fall* gave his *I no do am* its backing:

“I no do am. Both.”

Two words per charge. Four words total.

The officer wrote: *maintains innocence on both counts.* The flat light stayed flat. The case, built entirely from what he *didn't* say or do at 11:42 and at the window — not what he did at the gravel edge at 12:25 — sat between them, thin but tidy, like a queue that had formed correctly behind the wrong destination sign and that a stamp could make official.

Outside, a transistor that someone had left on for the charge room — not for him, for the officer who liked background — gave Kasoa FM's midday voice reading a funeral announcement with that same emotion that made text sound bigger, then a small pause, then a new, shorter item read with the same thrill but faster: *…police confirm recording found at seven-house new house near Last Stop, background voice…*

The officer did not turn it up. He noted the time in the margin — 12:47 — and closed the phone that had played the recording's copy last hour. Later, in Ch.18, Kwame would be told properly what had been found at Saaba's house — the call she was on with her father Nanfuri when robbers entered — but here, in Two Charges, it was enough that the town already knew something had been found and therefore the case, in its two-charge form, felt consolidated, filed in duplicate, thin but official.

The officer stood. Handcuffs stayed but the ring on the table was released with a small metal *click* that in this book was always white line on black.

“You rest,” he said, not unkind, in the voice of a man who says *rest* to mean *stay* and *you are still charged* and *we hear but filing is filing*. “We go bring your visitor tomorrow. Ebo first, then Chocho listening, then Commander self.”

Visitor. Singular. Nene had been first and was now needed to be not seen. The next — per the reworked outline, to keep Act IV from being static with a man alone in a flat room — would need to be someone who could carry the recording into this daylight room without breaking single-POV and without needing a lawyer the cast did not yet have.

For now, the room stayed flat, bright, and storybook — safe, despite its content — while the man who had chosen silence at 11:42 sat with the cost of that silence filed in duplicate and with a margin note that read, in his own small hand, *Last Stop = barrier, 7 houses, car steal not lent*.
