# Chapter 19 — The Recording

*Later — same period, custody*

They let him hear it the next morning.

Not the whole — protocol never lets an accused man with two charges hear the whole when chain of custody still smells of dust from a seven-house house where a rope still says *do not enter* — just the part a constable could play on a copy of a copy on a phone without being there for the search.

The door closed.

The room was the same flat light — white walls, uniform outline, minimal hatching on the table's edge, storybook linework on ordinary objects — but this morning the air tasted of dust even though the room was clean. The officer who pressed play was not the *fact* officer with his *fact* hammer, not Nanfuri — Nanfuri, having been on the call live when it cut, would have already heard its start in the worse audio of memory where grief makes everything louder and less clear — but a younger man who treated the phone as if it were glass and as if the phone itself were evidence, which it was, though it had also been a daughter's kitchen speaker.

“Listen,” he said, unnecessary. He said it the way a preacher says *listen* when the PA is already loud. “You hear name for back. Focus for back, not front.”

Kwame sat with cuffed hands folded so they would not rattle the table and make the recording harder to hear, and so the metal would not make a sound the transcript would call *restless*. He was still Kwame Asante, forty-four, station master twelve years, elder who checked verses silently rather than arguing, which meant he listened the way he listened to preachers: for the verse, not the volume; for the true sentence behind the loud sentence.

He leaned a fraction forward. Not eager — careful.

The constable pressed.

• • •

*--line open, ordinary, 12:?? a.m. Day 0 --*

*Saaba: Daddy? You dey house? I lock — glass window I close but that side for kitchen — the small one for back — I no sure —*

Her voice. Warm, direct, daughterly — not performing, not thriller-heroine, just talking to the man who had taught her to lock correctly and who, on this night, she had called to say *I dey house, I lock* the way she had many evenings since the fine new house seven houses from Last Stop had been finished with glass windows that caught sun and a low wall still unrendered. She spoke with the small upward flick that daughters use with strict fathers who are also, secretly, soft. Kwame had heard that voice once at the kiosk buying airtime — quick, polite — and now heard it where it had lived last.

*Nanfuri (faint, phone speaker, distant but close as fathers are on speakers): — you lock proper, Saaba. You push that kitchen window well? You — you hear any motor for road?*

Ordinary check-in. The ledger had said this power came from normalcy interrupted, not from secret, and it did.

Then — not loud, because the phone was on speaker on a counter, not at an ear, and because the kitchen window's low side was three steps from the counter — a second sound, behind her: wood on wood, not the hill gate Kwame muffled with a palm lift to make it not screech, but a similar service — a window that should not be opening at night being forced from outside where no paper said anyone owned that frame yet, where low wall no render gave fingers purchase.

*Saaba: — Daddy? Someone dey — someone dey for back —*

No scream yet. The small, logical attempt to name before fear, as if naming might make the thing be a neighbour.

A man's voice, in the background, not on the phone — in the room, behind her, not shouting, talking to another man at the window, as if work were being divided and the house were being read as a site, not a home.

“Make Musah bring car comot — fast — no make headlight catch wall —”

Just that. *Make Musah bring car comot.* A name used as a verb's subject, low, practical, flat, as Chocho used names when he sent boys — *Musah move this, Kofi check that* — name meaning *do*, name meaning *you*.

Another man's laugh — short, not cruel, work-laugh — the woman's soft *fast, fast*, not panicked, instructing, a chair scraping loud against a floor that had not been lived on long enough to know its own legs, Saaba's breath — sharp, real, daughterly breath caught in the way breath catches before it becomes a word — and then, after a sound that was not a word, not a scream but the start of a scream being cut, after the father on the other end saying *Saaba? Saaba!* twice, louder each time the way a man says a name when the line is still open but the room is not, the line cut.

Not hung up. Cut. The way impact cuts — phone on counter knocked, or girl knocked, or both — the flat nothing that Kwame had heard at Last Stop when Mansa's phone gave dead not off.

Silence after was also recorded, for two seconds — house dust, not flat-room dust — then a man's hand finding the phone and pressing, and then true nothing.

• • •

The constable stopped the playback. He did not need to ask *you hear?* Kwame's face had done it — not with a shout, not with *Stoooooppppp!!* — just with the small, specific flinch that men who keep quiet do when quiet has been broken for someone else.

“Musah,” Kwame said, quiet. It was not a name he knew in Mallam. It was not his name. It was not Chocho's, not Ebo's, not Kpakpo's. It was, by definition, a lead that was also a question — whose Musah? In Accra many grandfathers had been Musah and many sons were Musah again, the way many economies repeat names.

The officer nodded once, satisfied with the hearing, not with the meaning. Satisfied the accused had been given what protocol allowed.

“We hear Musah too,” he said, pocketing the phone as if it were glass again — which it was. “But Musah plenty. We bring Chocho today to hear whether na his boy or —” He made a small, economical gesture with his hand — *outside* — the flat hand moving away from the table as if from Mallam toward another community, that he understood without the word, because Chocho's network was Mallam and this case had already been gossiped as *outside work* the moment people learned the house was fresh and unpapered and a Camry had broken a barrier and photographed a commander before shooting tyres.

“They go call Nanfuri next,” the officer added, almost to himself, quieter, because Nanfuri had been on the other side of that line when it cut, and hearing the background *Musah* from the clear house copy — not from memory where grief makes audio worse, where a father's ear invents or erases to protect itself — would be a second hearing for him as well, and a second wound. But that hearing belonged to Chapter 21, face-to-face, father to station master, not to this flat room where recording belonged to evidence, not to grief.

Kwame stared at the table's careful linework — pots and stools given the same care as people even though no pots were in this room, even though the only pot had been on Saaba's counter seven houses away — and thought of that ordinary call. *Daddy, you lock?* She had locked, or thought she had locked the glass window she closed but was not sure for kitchen side. Fresh house, fresh paper no dey — Sule had said it at the barber: *new house, no wall finish, no paper — na invitation.* The invitation had been answered at night by men who thought houses without papers were houses without fathers, who thought a fine girl's house was a rich police pikin's house, and then found a father on speaker who taught locking.

“Musah,” he said again, smaller, not because the name mattered more than the life, but because a name was the only thing of that night that could be taken from a room where a girl had been teaching her father she was safe and made to walk toward a cemetery gate east of his hill house where, with backup, an arrest would later close the loop that had started when a Camry photographed a commander at a barrier and when a station master refused at gun at Last Stop with wire snapping.

The officer stood. He did not say *thank you*. In these rooms thank you is not procedure.

“Rest,” he said, again, not unkind, in the voice he used when he meant *stay* and meant *you are still charged* and meant *we hear.*

After he left, the flat light stayed. The recording — four sentences of ordinary plus one name in a background plus a chair scrape plus a father's two calls — sat in Kwame's memory the way a small, true clue sits: not a confession, not clean, not *I do am*, but a hinge where a whole case might swing if the right ear heard it and said *not my man — outside group*, and where a different wrong story — *he targeted Saaba because car be his own* — would finally be unhinged.

Kwame heard, again, though the phone was gone, the wood on wood of a kitchen window that should not be opening. This time, because he had lifted a gate every night to keep it quiet, and because Nene had once heard a bang that others ignored and had not ignored it, he heard it the way an anchor hears: as a sound that, if heard properly at the time — draft on ankle, curtain moving — would have changed everything from window check to seven houses to barrier to cemetery.

Outside, Last Stop's barrier was still straight. Saaba's glass, seven houses away, would never be ordinary again, but the fact that it had been ordinary once — *Daddy, you lock?* — was now the most important fact in the file.
