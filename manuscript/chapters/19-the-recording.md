# Chapter 19 — The Recording

*Later — same period*

They let him hear it the next morning.

Not the whole — the part that protocol allowed a man with two charges to hear without being there for the chain of custody — a copy on a constable's phone, played in the flat room where yesterday they had laid the case and where Ebo had said *her father*.

The door closed. The air tasted of dust even though the room was clean. The officer who pressed play was not the *fact* officer, not Nanfuri — Nanfuri, having been on the call, would have already heard its start live before it cut — but a younger man who treated the phone as if it were glass.

“Listen,” he said, unnecessary. “You hear name for back.”

Kwame sat with cuffed hands folded so they would not rattle the table and make the recording harder to hear. He was still Kwame Asante who checked verses silently rather than arguing, which meant he listened the way he listened to preachers: for the verse, not the volume.

• • •

*--line open--*

*Saaba: Daddy? You dey house? I lock — glass window I close but that side for kitchen —*

*Nanfuri (faint, phone speaker): — you lock proper, Saaba. You —*

A small, ordinary father-daughter check-in. The kind that had happened many evenings since the house, seven houses from Last Stop, had been finished with glass that caught sun. Her voice warm, direct, daughterly — not performing, just talking to the man who had taught her to lock.

Then — not loud, because the phone was on speaker on a counter, not at an ear — a second sound, behind her: wood on wood, not the gate that Kwame muffled, but a window that should not be opening at night being forced from outside where no paper said anyone owned that frame.

*Saaba: — Daddy? Someone dey —*

A man's voice, in the background, not on the phone — in the room, behind her. Not shouting. Talking to another man at the window, as if work were being divided.

“Make Musah bring car comot —”

Just that. *Make Musah bring car comot.* A name used as a verb's subject, low, practical, as Chocho used names when he sent boys: name meaning *do*.

Another man's laugh, the woman's soft *fast, fast*, a chair scraping, Saaba's breath — sharp, real — and then, after a sound that was not a word, after the father on the other end saying *Saaba? Saaba!* twice, louder each time, the line cut.

• • •

The constable stopped the playback. He did not need to ask *you hear?* Kwame's face had done it.

“Musah,” Kwame said, quiet. It was not a name he knew. It was not his name. It was not Chocho's. It was, by definition, a lead that was also a question — whose Musah?

The officer nodded once, satisfied with the hearing, not with the meaning.

“We hear Musah too,” he said, pocketing the phone. “But Musah plenty. We bring Chocho today to hear whether na his boy or —” He made a small gesture with his hand — *outside* — that Kwame understood without the word, because Chocho's network was Mallam and this case had already been gossiped as *outside work* the moment people learned the house was fresh and unpapered.

“They go call Nanfuri next,” the officer added, almost to himself, because Nanfuri had been on the other side of that line when it cut, and hearing the background *Musah* from the clear copy — not from memory where grief makes audio worse — would be a second hearing for him as well. But Nanfuri's hearing belonged to Chapter 21, face-to-face, not to this flat room.

Kwame stared at the table's careful linework — pots and stools given the same care as people even though no pots were in this room — and thought of that ordinary call. *Daddy, you lock?* She had locked. Or thought she had. Fresh house, fresh paper no dey — Sule had said it at the barber: *new house, no wall finish, no paper — na invitation.* The invitation had been answered at night by men who thought houses without owners were houses without fathers, and then found a father on speaker.

“Musah,” he said again, smaller, not because the name mattered more than the life, but because a name was the only thing of that night that could be taken from a room and made to walk toward a gate east of his hill house where, with backup, an arrest would later close the loop that had started when a Camry photographed a commander before shooting tyres.

The officer stood.

“Rest,” he said, again, not unkind.

After he left, the flat light stayed. The recording — four sentences of ordinary plus one name in a background — sat in Kwame's memory the way a small, true clue sits: not a confession, but a hinge where a whole case might swing if the right ear heard it and said *not my man — outside group.*

Kwame heard, again, though the phone was gone, the wood on wood of a window that should not be opening. This time, because he had once lifted a gate every night to keep it quiet, he heard it the way Nene had heard a bang that others ignored — as a sound that, if heard properly, would have changed everything.
