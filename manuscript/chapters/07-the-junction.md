# Chapter 7 — The Junction

*Day 0, 11:42 p.m. – 12:20 a.m. (overlapping Chapters 5–6) — Last Stop/junction wall*

There is a way to sit at Last Stop at night that tells anyone who passes what kind of man you are, and Kwame had learned it the way he learned everything at the station — by watching and not being told.

Kwame sat with his back to the police post, not leaning. Elbows on knees, hands clasped, not slumped — that was for boys who had been beaten and for the preacher after fundraising — and not upright like a man waiting to be seen by a boss. Just enough forward that the road could pass him without asking him to move and that anyone who knew him would know to leave him or to join him depending on what kind of sitting it was.

He had chosen this wall that afternoon to count money — ₵285, then ₵285 again as if counting twice might make it ₵300 — and again at 11:52 to count his silence after *good for nothing* and after the gate's lift that did not screech. Now it was 11:47, then 11:53, then midnight, then 12:03 — the same wall, the same barrier pole raised behind him where the black Camry with tinted no-plate hit at four p.m. with a hollow *thwack* and sent the striped pole swinging, where the young policeman had straightened it afterwards with two hands and a look that pretended it was ordinary. Last Stop and the barrier were the same place. The bible had locked it and his body had always known it: the town said *Mallam* when it meant both a town and a road, the way it said *Last Stop* when it meant both a wall and a post.

The junction was where Kwame sat when he needed to not be in a room with thin walls where eight doors could hear a door.

He was trying not to hear the argument on repeat.

*You're just good for nothing* — not the volume of it — volume at 11:42 had been sharp but not shouted-elongated — but the exact shape where fear had turned into attack. He had seen that turn at the station with crowds — fear becoming a hand raised — and at home it had become a sentence that had edges. He replayed his own answer to it, which was no answer — the standard immovability that had made him beloved as a peacemaker at seven a.m. between Kofi's elbow and a boy and that now, at 11:42, at the gate with a palm lift, read as *you be nothing*. He replayed it other ways, while the police bulb buzzed and the junction smelled of dust and old oil:

*I borrow ₵300 for Madam eggs. I hide because shame. No woman. I be short five.*

Three sentences. He had them now, on the wall, when the room was not there to hear them and when the person they were for was — unbeknownst to him, fifty steps away, soft impact wrapped in leaves — already outside a window where a phone had died on impact, not by choice, and where a witness had heard a bang and ignored it.

A shared taxi passed, not stopping. It did not honk at Last Stop — at night, drivers respected junctions without needing to be told, the way drivers at noon respect queues. Its headlights made the wedge that, in Style A scratchboard, the guide called a pale cone cutting through near-solid black, white linework carved out, single dramatic light source as a wedge, dense fine-line cross-hatching on foliage/brick/fabric where light hit, sparse or absent in flat black sky to direct the eye. On the ground, fine-line cross-hatching would be dust and foliage; in life, it was just dust that caught light for a second and then was dark again the way a story is dark until a beam hits it. Kwame watched the dust without thinking of dust as motif — though the Theme Tracker would need him to later, when dust settled.

Behind him, the police post's single bulb buzzed — that particular 50Hz buzz that comes from cheap ballasts — and threw a cone that did not reach the road where he sat. No policeman after midnight. That was normal in Mallam Town — undermanned enough that a Camry could break the barrier outright at four p.m. and be chased by a commander who had to drive himself and then, at dusk, by a commander who chose *Carefully* at a cemetery gate rather than go further alone against men who shot both left tyres. Formal and informal authority tangled together there, which was why Chocho — Lacoste flat, slippers silent — could watch a new house seven houses away and the station could blame land guards for a Camry and no one could quite prove which land guard, which community, until Musah was named on a speaker.

Kwame thought of that house — seven houses down, glass windows now dark after being warm at six p.m., Saaba's house — without knowing why it entered his mind at 11:53. Perhaps because Last Stop was measured in houses, not metres. Everyone measured distance there that way, not in kilometres: *seven houses away* was a kind of address, precise enough for the town, vague enough for a lie, precise again for a case file. He could have walked it in two minutes if he had needed to. He had seen its glass catch sun at midday when the barber said *new house, no wall finish — na invitation*, and at six p.m. when lights glowed ordinary. He did not need to.

What he needed was to decide whether to go back and, if he went back, whether to speak.

Mansa's phone was unreachable. He had told himself *switched off* at the wall at 11:47, then at the gate at 11:52, then again at the wall at 11:55, then at midnight he tried a fourth time with his thumb leaving a faint smear on cracked screen and told the screen what he had not told Mansa: *I borrow ₵300, I get ₵295, I go pay tomorrow eehh.* To the screen. Not to her. To the screen because the screen would not say *good for nothing* back.

Somewhere behind him, east toward the hill and the cemetery he could not see from here because Last Stop faces south and hills block gates, the dog that had barked with purpose at the iron gate with its cleared path and stash point earlier that afternoon barked once more — a single, corrected bark, as if someone had passed the crooked post and then been told not to, and stopped. The station's dust did not move.

It was then — 12:03 by the phone's small numbers, which later statements would make important though Kwame did not look at the time that precisely then — that the call came.

Not from Mansa. Mansa's phone was the same flat nothing, dead from impact at 11:44.

Yaw.

Voice smaller than at the station's noon when he said *Bad Luck Nkoaa* laughing, trying to be professional and not quite managing because night makes everyone a little younger and because asking a station master who has just argued with his wife to come work is a particular ask:

“Station master, Charlie — you dey Last Stop? Sorry for night — we get two passengers stranded. No car for station. They beg, eehh. You dey come? Make they wait?”

Yaw did not know about the room empty in the way a room is empty not like sleeping, about the curtain that would move with draft, about the pawpaw tree that sometimes brushed the roofing. He knew about stranded passengers the way station masters always know: someone needs a car at the edge of town at midnight because market made them late and because they trust a station master who comes when called, same trait that made him pay for eggs with borrowed money.

Kwame looked at the road home — the lane up the hill, gate that needed a palm flat and a lift to be quiet, window open a hand's breadth he had not looked beyond, curtain with hem by Mansa, pawpaw leaves big as hands, phone dead — and the road to the station — empty, dark, potholes waiting that by day you could see and swerve and at night they find you, two passengers somewhere beyond the station who needed a peacemaker to drive them.

He could have said *I get problem for house.* That would have been true in the exact degree that made it useless as an explanation. In Mallam Town saying *I get problem for house* when your wife has just called you good for nothing at 11:42 and you have left without answering and thin walls have carried both the sentence and the door was a sentence that arrived already doubted. People would fill the silence with gossip — kiosks, salons, bars — *he beat her, he chase her, he run, see his car comot*, and by morning the story would already be bigger than the night before the facts had a chance to be thin and tidy.

He said, instead, what duty always made him say.

The same trait, the same immovability, the same compulsory sense of duty as station master that the dossier said was his want and his contradiction. The trait that got him ₵300 in debt at seven a.m. and that would get him beaten at midnight at the same gravel edge.

“I dey come,” he said. Two words, terse, checking no verse. “Make they wait Last Stop. I go take car for house.”

He said *take car for house* not *go home*. House was where car was. Home was where Mansa was. At that moment, after a silence that had been distance, he could not call it home without adding noise. He stood.

The wall kept his warmth a second longer — the way day stays on walls at night and the way noon stays on poles.

He walked back toward the hill — again — not because he wanted to see Mansa's face (though he did, with a sudden, physical wanting that was not a plan) but because the car was there — Corolla fourteen years, washed Sunday, wire-fixed door that morning — and the car was how he would get paid, and getting paid was how he would make three sentences about ₵300 affordable enough to be called *small thing* rather than *hidden*.

He told himself he would look in, tell her the three sentences quickly — *I borrow for Madam eggs, five short, go pay* — then drive to Last Stop, then to station, then back before she had time to make his quiet into another story. He did not tell himself he would not check outside the window because that thought — *check outside window* — had not yet been invented for him the way *check outside window* would be invented for a constable at mid-morning by a draft.

He passed the kiosk, dark and shuttered, bars on its hatch. The barber's stool, empty, clippers cold. The transistor, off, the presenter who adds emotions to text no longer adding. The station at this hour was not a hub but a memory of one, seven queues' signs leaning like tired men who had been told to wait and had been waiting since six a.m.

At 12:18 — as later statements would put it, though Kwame did not look at the time that precisely then; later statements love precision where lives were vague — he reached his compound again, lifted the gate, quiet, still lift-push, still no screech, still practicing peace as quiet.

He found the room the way Chapter 6 has already shown it, because this chapter overlaps: empty and dark not like sleeping, exercise book red, no sales pouch, curtain moving with cool outside-to-inside draft, window open a hand's breadth — a little more — he put his fingers on sill, curtain brushed wrist, did not lean out, did not put his head beyond frame, did not look down where pawpaw tree base waited with a body and a dead phone.

This is the part the single-POV rule makes quiet, and this chapter's overlap is intentional, not duplication: per the outline, the reader, with Kwame, has only the draft and the curtain and the strange, specific cold on the ankle. The fall itself is not shown in privilege. No Mansa POV. No superior irony. Only the faint, plantable clue that someone else in the compound — unnamed witness — heard a bang at 11:44 and, not knowing where it came from, ignored it — the kind of ignored that the Ledger said would have changed everything if mentioned — and the window sill, worn where hands had gripped it over years, at a height a man would not think to look down from when the door had been heard to close as *he walk comot*.

Kwame took the keys from the nail — again, the second taking, the nail shiny where keys had touched thousands of times — and left the house that had kept its thin-walled silence behind him the way a station keeps a queue. He said *Mansa, if you dey hear me — I dey come back* into air eight rooms heard.

At the lane's mouth he turned on headlights and made the pale wedge — a single cone cutting dark, dense hatching on foliage where it hit, flat black beyond. Potholes were where they had been since the rains. He swerved where he could see them, missed where he could, failed where night found him — which is how the next chapter, eight, would find him at the gravel edge near Last Stop with three people who would say *You go Lapaz or no?*

That decision — to return for the car, to sit at Last Stop in the first place after an argument that demanded three sentences and received none — was not made with knowledge of a fall already happening fifty steps behind him. It was made with character. Which was why, after, when the town filed *he run*, the same decision read like meaning, like motive, like flight, while in truth it was the same peacemaking that had stood between an elbow and a boy's face at seven a.m. — only now at midnight, at a gate that did not screech, with a curtain that did.
