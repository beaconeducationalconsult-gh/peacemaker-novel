# Chapter 7 — The Junction

*Day 0, 11:42 p.m. – 12:20 a.m. (overlapping Chapters 5–6)*

There is a way to sit at Last Stop at night that tells anyone who passes what kind of man you are.

Kwame sat with his back to the police post, not leaning, elbows on knees, hands clasped. Not slumped — that was for boys who had been beaten — and not upright like a man waiting to be seen. Just enough forward that the road could pass him without asking him to move.

He had chosen this wall that afternoon to count money and again at 11:52 to count his silence. Now it was 11:47, then 11:53, then midnight — the same wall, the same barrier pole raised behind him where the Camry had hit it at four in the afternoon and where the young policeman had straightened it afterwards. Last Stop and the barrier were the same place. Everyone said so in the way people say *Mallam* when they mean both a town and a road. The junction was where Kwame sat when he needed to not be in a room with thin walls.

He was trying not to hear the argument on repeat.

*Good for nothing* — Mansa's voice at its specific, not generic, pitch. Not the volume of it — volume fades — but the exact shape of the sentence where fear had turned into an attack. He replayed his own answer to it, which was no answer, and tried it other ways. *I borrow for Madam eggs.* Three sentences. He had them now, on the wall, when the room was not there to hear them.

A shared taxi passed, not stopping. Its headlights made the wedge that, in Style A scratchboard, would be a pale cone cutting through solid black. On the ground, fine-line cross-hatching would be dust and foliage; in life, it was just dust that caught light and then was dark again. The taxi did not honk at Last Stop. At night, drivers respected junctions without needing to be told.

Behind him, the police post's single bulb buzzed. No policeman. That was normal after midnight in Mallam Town — undermanned enough that a Camry could break the barrier outright at four p.m. and be chased by a commander who had to drive himself. Formal and informal authority tangled together, which was why Chocho could watch a new house seven houses away and the station could blame land guards and land guards could be blamed for a Camry and no one could quite prove which land guard, which community.

Kwame thought of that house — seven houses down, glass windows now dark, Saaba's house — without knowing why it entered his mind. Perhaps because Last Stop was measured in houses. Everyone measured distance there that way, not in metres. *Seven houses away* was a kind of address, precise enough for the town. He could have walked it in two minutes if he had needed to.

He did not need to.

What he needed was to decide whether to go back.

Mansa's phone was unreachable. He had told himself *switched off* at the wall, then at the gate, then again at the wall. At midnight he tried a fourth time. Same flat nothing. He stared at his own cracked screen where his thumb left a faint smear and told the screen what he had not told Mansa: *I borrow ₵300, I get ₵295, I go pay tomorrow.* To the screen. Not to her.

Somewhere behind him, east toward the hill and the cemetery he could not see from here, the dog that had barked earlier barked once more — a single, corrected bark — and stopped.

It was then that the call came.

Not from Mansa.

Yaw, voice smaller than at the station, trying to be professional and not quite managing because night made everyone a little younger: “Station master, Charlie — you dey Last Stop? We get two passengers stranded. No car. They beg. You dey come?”

Kwame looked at the road home — lane to the hill, gate that needed a palm, window open a hand's breadth he had not looked beyond — and the road to the station — empty, dark, potholes waiting, stranded people who needed a peacemaker to drive them.

He could have said *I get problem for house.* That would have been true in the exact degree that made it useless as an explanation. In Mallam Town saying *I get problem for house* when your wife had just called you good for nothing and you had left without answering was a sentence that arrived already doubted. People would fill the silence with gossip — *he beat her, he chase her, he run* — and by morning the story would already be bigger than the night.

He said, instead, what duty always made him say. The same trait that had made him pay for eggs at 7 a.m. with borrowed money.

“I dey come,” he said. “Make they wait Last Stop. I go take car for house.”

He stood. The wall kept his warmth a second longer. He walked back toward the hill — again — not because he wanted to see Mansa's face (though he did) but because the car was there, and the car was how he would get paid, and getting paid was how he would make three sentences about ₵300 affordable.

He told himself he would look in and tell her quickly, then drive. He did not tell himself he would not check outside the window, because that thought had not yet been invented for him.

He passed the kiosk, dark. The barber's stool, empty. The transistor, off. The station, at this hour, was not a hub but a memory of one, seven queues' signs leaning like tired men.

At 12:18 — as later statements would put it, though Kwame did not look at the time that precisely — he reached his compound again, lifted the gate, quiet, and found the room the way Chapter 6 has already shown it: empty, dark, curtain moving, phone dead not by choice but by impact.

This is the part the single-POV rule makes quiet. The reader, with Kwame, has only the draft and the curtain and the strange, specific cold on the ankle. The fall itself is not shown. No privileged access. Only the faint, plantable clue that someone else in the compound heard a bang and ignored it, and the window sill, worn where hands had gripped it, at a height a man would not think to look down from when the door had been heard to close.

Kwame took the keys from the nail — again, the second taking — and left. The house kept its thin-walled silence behind him. At the lane's mouth he turned on headlights and made the pale wedge. The potholes were where they had been. He swerved where he could see them. He went to do a job that, in his mind, was still about passengers, not about three people waiting near Last Stop who would ask him for a ride and, when he refused even at gunpoint, reach in to kill his ignition.

That decision — to return for the car, to sit at Last Stop in the first place — was not made with knowledge. It was made with character. Which was why, after, it would read like meaning.
