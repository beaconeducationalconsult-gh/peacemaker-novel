# Chapter 25 — The Cemetery

*Arrest day — Kwame learns*

Nanfuri came the second time without hat as well — but this time with dust on his trousers that was not station dust.

It was cemetery dust.

Kwame was in the flat room again — daylight, uniform outline, pots and stools not there but given the same care if they were — when the door opened and the constable did not say *visitor* but *Commander*.

Nanfuri entered with the particular tiredness of a man who had done the same road twice and the second time with enough men to make the first time's careful withdrawal look like wisdom rather than hesitation.

He sat. He did not ask Kwame to sit — Kwame was already sitting where he had learned that a case built from what you didn't say sits differently when someone finally says.

“We do am,” Nanfuri said, clipped, then, not clipped: “Cemetery. Same gate.”

Kwame breathed.

“Tell,” he said. Terse. But this time terse meant *I need the whole, not the fact.*

Nanfuri murmured to himself a second, organizing, then gave it the weight the Illustration Style Guide said this chapter deserved — Style A's thematic home base: black and white scratchboard, high-contrast, white linework carved from near-solid black, single dramatic light source as a pale wedge cutting dark, dense fine-line cross-hatching on foliage/brick/fabric, sparse detail in flat black sky to direct the eye, noir voyeur framing through foreground foliage.

Though the room was daylight, the memory he described was all noir.

“Daylight,” Nanfuri said. Deliberate. “We go daylight, not night. That change. Potholes — you see them and swerve. At night they find you. That afternoon they found me with tyres. Now we see.”

He described it as a bearing, not a battle.

Backup — two pickups, six men, not the two constables and a flat pickup on rims. Chocho not there — outside group, outside road, outside need — but his *Musah is outside* had drawn the line on the map that led east of Kwame's hill house to the crooked post where tyre track had ended inside on Day 0.

“We reach gate — same iron, same bend post, neglected. Same stillness, but daylight make stillness honest: cleared path where path no be cleared, stash point — small tarp, empty sachet, fresh tyre mark continuing past where eye think it stop, same as before but now you see because sun is beam.”

That beam — pale wedge — Style A's single light source, now the sun rather than moonlight or flashlight, cutting the cemetery's flat black and making texture visible: cross-hatching on iron rust, on brick of low wall, on fabric of a shirt hung to dry that should not be there.

“Three inside,” Nanfuri said. “Tall man, thick man — Musah — and woman with bag. Same three who stop you for Last Stop. Same who take your car seven houses to Saaba house. They no run. They think gate still hide because I withdraw before, they think careful be fear.”

Kwame thought of his own *carefully* at that gate every morning — lift, push, quiet — and of Nanfuri's *Carefully* at dusk to his boys.

“We show,” Nanfuri said, and made a small, contained gesture with his hand — not a sweep, a wedge: light, then men moving along it. “We spread through foliage — frame through leaves, not road — voyeur, tense. They hear branch, they look — one try back path but path dey block where we put man. No shoot. We talk. Musah talk first — *we no do Saaba house, we do* — then stop when he see my face.”

*My face.* The face that had been photographed through a tinted window before tyres shot. The face whose daughter's call had been *Daddy, you lock?* The face that had heard *Saaba! Saaba!* before cut.

“They down,” Nanfuri said. Simple. Not triumphant. Methodical. “Hand for back. Car — your Corolla, fourteen years — dey under tarp near stash. We bring. We also bring that Camry — same, tinted, no plate — dey for far corner with bush cover. Dust for inside still from that barrier that afternoon.”

He looked at Kwame, and for the first time the officer's eyes did what Mansa's had done in the ward — filled without spilling, grief tempered by procedure.

“I tell them, ‘You arrest for Saaba murder and for attack for Last Stop for station master.’ I talk my daughter name first. That be order.”

Kwame closed the eye that closed fully. The loop — Chapter 3's unseen chase to Chapter 25's daylight return with backup — had closed on the same ground. What read as unexplained and eerie at dusk — stillness that didn't feel empty — was literal, operational, and now, with light, seen. What Nanfuri had sensed and not pursued alone had been correct, and choosing not to pursue alone had let him pursue properly now.

“They dey cell,” Nanfuri said. “Different cell. Not your cell. Your —” He paused, chose precision. “Your file go change. Not today. But go change.”

He stood, as if to leave, then sat again a half second, because the personal loop from Chapter 21 — two silences that learned to speak — needed one more sentence.

“That night you sit Last Stop for quiet,” Nanfuri said to Kwame, not as a policeman to a suspect but as a man who had also sat somewhere alone after midnight and been told by a wife or a daughter that silence was not an answer, “— you sit where barrier break. I withdraw for cemetery gate. Both careful. Careful look like fear to town. To town, noise be answer. To us — we learn.”

Kwame, who had learned that peace is not the same as justice, and that justice, in Mallam Town, needs a daylight visit to a gate that had once been a night stillness, put his cuffed hands lightly on the table's ordinary linework.

“Thank you for go with men,” he said, quiet. Not *thank you for arrest* — that was procedure. *Thank you for go with men* — meaning *thank you for not going alone.*

Nanfuri put his hat on for the first time in the room — hat that had been in hands on Day 0's return — and stood.

“We go bring car for you to see tomorrow,” he said, almost as afterthought, because a washed Corolla with wire on the handle, returned after being driven seven houses and then to a cemetery stash, was a small, careful economy — the kind of thing a station master who knew how to keep queues tight would notice.

After he left, the flat room kept its daylight — B, observational, storybook — while the memory inside it kept its noir wedge — A, dramatic, carved white on black — and the two, per the Theme Tracker's recommendation, finally reinforced each other rather than competing.

Outside, east of the hill, a crooked post stood straight for the first time in the book.
